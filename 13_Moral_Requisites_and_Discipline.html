Suggestive Inquiry: Summaries

Of the Mental Requisites and Impediments Incidental to Individuals, Either as Masters or Students, in the Hermetic Art

Part III, Chapter 4

Before reaching “the routine of Practice”, Atwood sets out who is fit for the work and what bars the way (III.4 ¶2). Her main claim is that in Alchemy, unlike any ordinary art, the operator’s intention comes back on him exactly, because the agent of the work springs from himself. Character is therefore a condition of the work itself, and this is why the art was kept secret (III.4 ¶9).

Geber’s impediments and the danger of avarice

All men “have the material and live by it”, yet very few are suited to this research (III.4 ¶2). Quoting Geber, Atwood lists bodily defects and “Impediments of Mind”: lack of the sagacity needed to find “the true Radix” (the root of the science), a fancy “replete with fumosities”, inconstancy, disbelief, and fear “to interposit the necessary charges” through love of money (III.4 ¶3).

She calls Avarice “this first and last defect” (III.4 ¶4). Far worse are those who reached “an exalted energy”, then turned it to private ends, “compelling the Spirit” (III.4 ¶6). The soul, she argues, “must either be filled with a superior or inferior power”: piety lets in the divine, impiety an evil spirit (III.4 ¶6). She glosses Geber’s “necessary charges” as “to abandon the life of selfhood, and return the product to a benevolent and truthful end” (III.4 ¶6). The pious author of the Aquarium, whom she endorses, warns that pride or covetousness makes the art “vanish from under thy hands” (III.4 ¶7).

Why intention returns on the operator

Moral, physical and organic laws seem separate but are “one” at the root (III.4 ¶8). In ordinary arts a work shows the Idea, not the motive: Holbein’s covetousness does not spoil his pictures (III.4 ¶8). In Alchemy “Final Causes” (purposes) appear in the act itself, and “the intention is received back according to its kind most exactly” (III.4 ¶9). The agent, “Springing directly from ourselves”, shapes the conceived Idea as the will directs. It grows stronger through degrees of “concentration” and in “the Conjunction”, and the operator’s accountability grows with it (III.4 ¶9). Hence the secrecy: Norton says the art is taught “fro’ mouth to mouth” under oath, only to one long proved (III.4 ¶10).

Skepticism, discrimination and the qualities required

Next to the covetous, Geber condemns the Skeptics (III.4 ¶12). By “infidelity” Atwood means faithlessness that “without rational foundation, denounces everything that is new” (III.4 ¶12). She names Mesmerism “the neglected Door-keeper” that first led her to the ancient science (III.4 ¶13). Against blind doubt she sets “rational skepticism”, better “called discrimination”, which tests everything and keeps the true (III.4 ¶15).

The searcher should be, like Nature, “faithful, simple, patient, constant”, hopeful and benevolent, and should have “a convenient leisure” (III.4 ¶15). Study of the doctrine comes before “experimental proof” and needs the same qualities (III.4 ¶15). The records are a “two-edged instrument”, because “the inner sense is held aloof from the literal” (III.4 ¶16). Norton, whom Atwood endorses, says only those “of stable wit” finish the work (III.4 ¶17).

Study, desire and prayer

The adepts scattered their teaching so that only one “really desirous, and made fit by long study” could follow it (III.4 ¶19). Its single guide is “the same Rational Light” (III.4 ¶19). The authorities agree: read first, then work (Ricardus); “Pray, says Sendivogius, pray; but work”; a subtle mind, skilled hands and a free will (Arnold) (III.4 ¶20). Atwood defends prayer because the research is “wrapped in and about the Desire” (III.4 ¶20). “Man may conditionate”, that is, prepare the ground by labour, but God gives the increase (III.4 ¶20).

Atwood holds that “every desire of the mind is in its kind a prayer” (III.4 ¶22). In Kirchringius’s account, burning desire to solve an enigma is prayer and active thinking is “cooperation”, until “in a moment, the solution will fall in” (III.4 ¶22). Iamblicus, whom Atwood endorses, names three classes of prayer: one gathers the mind (Illumination), one binds concordant spirits (Communion of Operation), and one is the “seal of union” (Divine Fire) (III.4 ¶24). Prayer and self-sacrifice strengthen each other (III.4 ¶28).

Assistance, vessels, ministers and places

The student needs “suitable assistance”: “without a Subject to work with and reciprocate the design” the work is “abortive” (III.4 ¶29). The Spirit does not increase “without a medium and a bond” (III.4 ¶30). The text leaves open whether this Subject is a person or the material. Norton’s Master revealed that the vessel God gave “is glasse” (III.4 ¶31). Atwood adds: “The Spirit finally constructs its own vessel and vitrifies it” (III.4 ¶32). Few go beyond “the preliminary Gross Work” (III.4 ¶32). For it Norton requires sober ministers who tend the work night and day (III.4 ¶37). Vaughan calls the true furnace “a little simple shell”, the labour light, and women fitter for it (III.4 ¶38). Places must be fitted to each operation, and wind always harms (III.4 ¶41). The place must also be secret and free of lingering “imaginative impressures”, to which “the entranced Subject” is susceptible (III.4 ¶42).

Vaughan’s lessons and the close

Atwood ends with Vaughan’s “lessons”: examine yourself, since you are “a co-operator with the living God”; excess heat divides “the Chaos” and drives off the Quintessence; “Have thy heart in heaven and thy hands upon earth”; “There is nothing stronger than Perseverance” (III.4 ¶44). In this philosophy “the end is implied in the beginning” (III.4 ¶45). Applying “the Master Key” sets the first vital cause moving, and manual practice must show the rest (III.4 ¶47–48).

The Work in this chapter

  • Operator: “rectitude of intention and the blessing of God on a well tried experience” (III.4 ¶11). These are upright intention, divine favour and tested experience (stated).
  • Agent: “Springing directly from ourselves” (III.4 ¶9). The power of the work comes from the operator and follows his will (interpreted).
  • Principle: “the intention is received back according to its kind most exactly” (III.4 ¶9). The motive shapes the result and returns on the operator (stated).
  • Impediment: “necessary charges” (III.4 ¶6). The real cost is giving up selfhood (stated).
  • Procedure: “Pray, says Sendivogius, pray; but work” (III.4 ¶20). Desire and labour must go together (stated).
  • Subject: “a Subject to work with and reciprocate the design” (III.4 ¶29). A responsive partner or material is needed; the text leaves open which (interpreted).
  • Impediment: “if thy heat exceeds the natural proportion” (III.4 ¶44). Violence, literal or of temper, ruins the work (interpreted).

Terms introduced

  • Impediments of Mind: Geber’s mental disqualifications for the work (III.4 ¶3).
  • Radix: the true root of the science (III.4 ¶3).
  • necessary charges: in Atwood’s gloss, giving up selfhood and turning the product to good ends (III.4 ¶6).
  • Final Causes: ends or purposes, which in Alchemy appear in the act itself (III.4 ¶9).
  • Conjunction: that in which the agent multiplies (III.4 ¶9); a period when the Spirit “recreates” under God alone (III.4 ¶20).
  • discrimination: rational skepticism, which tests everything and holds the true (III.4 ¶15).
  • Rational Light: the “One Leader” that frees the veiled teaching (III.4 ¶19).
  • conditionate: to prepare conditions by labour without compelling the result (III.4 ¶20).
  • Subject: what reciprocates the operator’s design (III.4 ¶29); in trance, open to imaginative impressions (III.4 ¶42).
  • Philosophic Vessel: the vessel of the work, which the Spirit finally builds and vitrifies (III.4 ¶30, ¶32).
  • Gross Work: the preliminary stage (III.4 ¶32).
  • Quintessence: “the sweet spirit of Peace” in the elements (III.4 ¶44).
  • Master Key: what sets the chain of vital causes moving; not explained (III.4 ¶47).

Authorities cited

Geber, for the list of impediments and the call to “avaunt” (III.4 ¶3, ¶12, ¶18, ¶38); Norton’s Ordinal, on the few chosen, oral teaching, glass vessels, ministers and places (III.4 ¶1, ¶10, ¶14, ¶17, ¶31–41); Hermes, on why the philosophers are “envious” (III.4 ¶9); the Aquarium (III.4 ¶7); Eirenaeus’s allegory of the candle (III.4 ¶16); Sendivogius (III.4 ¶16, ¶20); the hermetic Master, on the moral qualifications (III.4 ¶15); Ricardus, Zachary, Arnold, Lully and others, on study and prayer (III.4 ¶20); Kirchringius (III.4 ¶22); Iamblicus (III.4 ¶24); Charnocke (III.4 ¶33–35); Vaughan, on the furnace and his “lessons” (III.4 ¶38, ¶44); Cornelius Agrippa and Virgil, on the working-place (III.4 ¶42). Holbein, Galileo and Paradise Lost serve as illustrations (III.4 ¶5, ¶8, ¶12).
