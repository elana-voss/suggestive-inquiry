# II.1_01

*Opening Part II, Atwood separates true Alchemy from modern chemistry and pseudo-alchemy, argues that the ancients deliberately concealed the indispensable vessel, and through cited authorities (the Moorish philosopher, Agrippa, Sendivogius) points to Man himself, regenerate and self-knowing, as the true subject in which the work is found.*

## Argument

- Heading: Part II, Chapter 1, on the true subject of the Hermetic Art and its concealed root; Latin epigraph from Tractatus Aureus. `II.1 ¶1, II.1 ¶2` — ATWOOD
- Alchemy has so far been viewed from without; Atwood now offers the reader a clue. The way is dark, lonely, beyond 'this outer daylight', blocked by doubt and prejudice before the sanctuary of Minerva and its sacred light. `II.1 ¶3, II.1 ¶4` — ATWOOD
- Modern chemistry shares only borrowed terms with Alchemy, kept to veil it; no relation of matter, method or result. Solvents and fire achieve nothing 'vitally alternative' without the vital force; modern experiment expels the nature the ancients prized and is 'essentially atheistic'. `II.1 ¶5` — ATWOOD
- Pseudo-alchemists worked mineral sulfur, mercury and salt from literal receipts, without theory. A few who glimpsed the Universal Subject tried to attract light and ether into their vessels magnetically, but failed, ignorant of the adepts' 'internal fire and vessel'. `II.1 ¶6` — ATWOOD
- After Tobit, adepts display the monarchy's riches but barely mention the king. For the few who perished for lack of attainable knowledge, Atwood will lay open 'the regalia' enough to induce inquiry. `II.1 ¶7` — ATWOOD
- Her earlier inquiry identified the physical basis with a matter now only hypothetical; the ancients' obscurity about their 'conceptive vehicle' caused confusion. Moderns no longer investigate their own inner phenomena or the virtue in man to prove the Universal Source, yet that founded the magistery. `II.1 ¶8` — ATWOOD
- Without a proper vessel the 'ethereal corner stone' would never appear. Hali, Morien, Albert: the place is the principle of supernatural generation; Hermes: the vessel is their water; Maria: the vessel alone is unexplained, a divine secret essential to the magistery. `II.1 ¶8, II.1 n1` — ATWOOD
- The ancients withheld the means of proof on religious principle; open hints at the vessel or 'art of vital administration' were annulled or withdrawn. Sendivogius diverts from Morien's 'This matter, O king, is extracted from thee' with gold in a dead man's teeth. Such equivocations guard the 'living temple'. `II.1 ¶8, II.1 n2` — ATWOOD
- Boehme, and earlier Agrippa and Paracelsus' disciples, alarmed alchemists by applying alchemy openly to human life; the latter suggested the method and medium of attraction. `II.1 ¶9` — ATWOOD
- The Mercurial Spirit is in all things but not equally extractable; choose the noblest subject, one containing all forms. `II.1 ¶9` — QUOTED: Boehme, Agrippa and Paracelsus' disciples ('they say'); reported approvingly
- Man reformed into God's image surpasses the earth; the seeker's best reserve is himself, who draws the Central Salt of nature and in regenerate Wisdom possesses all. `II.1 ¶9, II.1 n3` — QUOTED: the Moorish philosopher (endorsed)
- The soul freed from the senses and body comprehends all. Man, image of God, contains the Universal Reason and corresponds with every level of creation; he acts even on God by knowing and loving Him. Who knows himself knows all, and draws all to himself as a loadstone iron. (The printed paragraph repeats part of this in garbled form.) `II.1 ¶9, II.1 n4` — QUOTED: Agrippa (endorsed)
- Nature's light is hidden by the body of sense; when it enlightens one, the cloud lifts and he sees 'the point of our lodestone'. The body shadows nature's seed and clothes man's nature; the air holds a secret food of life. Philosophers sought knowledge, not gold, and wrote figuratively; the self-knower, a microcosm, can understand. The soul is king, seated in the vital spirit in the purest blood, and reigns absolutely outside the body. `II.1 ¶10, II.1 n5` — QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed)

## Method

- **subject** — “Man reformed by God into his image” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: the Moorish philosopher (endorsed by Atwood))
  - **Plain (stated):** The true subject of the art is the human being, specifically man renewed into God's image; the seeker finds the material of the work in himself.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9: the seeker 'will nowhere find a greater or better reserve to obtain his desire than in himself'; presented as the answer to the search for 'one subject ... which contains all'.
  - Also: “This matter, O king, is extracted from thee” `II.1 ¶8`
  - Also: “will nowhere find a greater or better reserve to obtain his desire than in himself” `II.1 ¶9`
- **principle** — “if one subject should be presented which contains all, and the comprehension of each subordinate form in a superior essence, then this one needs only to be investigated for the discovery of all” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Boehme, Agrippa and Paracelsus' disciples ('they say'); reported approvingly)
  - **Plain (stated):** Rather than testing every substance, one should work on the single subject that contains all lower forms in a higher essence; investigating it reveals everything.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9 directly.
  - Also: “the best and noblest ought to be chosen to operate with” `II.1 ¶9`
- **agent** — “the Mercurial Spirit is everywhere, and to be found in all things according to the nature of each” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Boehme, Agrippa and Paracelsus' disciples; reported approvingly)
  - **Plain (stated):** The active spirit of the work is present in all things, but not every form holds enough of it, nor does it become universal by itself; hence the choice of the noblest subject.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9: 'it is neither apt to become universal of its own accord, or in every Form of virtue sufficient for the Hermetic work'.
  - Also: “since it is neither apt to become universal of its own accord, or in every Form of virtue sufficient for the Hermetic work” `II.1 ¶9`
- **agent** — “unless the vital force be present and in action” `II.1 ¶5` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** No real ('vital') transformation happens through chemical solvents or fire; the vital force must be present and active.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶5: 'nothing vitally alternative is achieved, unless the vital force be present and in action'.
- **impediment** — “modern art drives out, in fact, the very nature which the ancients prized” `II.1 ¶5` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Laboratory chemistry has no relation to Alchemy in matter, method or result; its external, superficial experimenting expels the living nature the work depends on.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶5: shares only 'the borrowed terms', no 'real relation, either of matter, method, or practical result'; method 'superficial and essentially atheistic'.
  - Also: “essentially atheistic” `II.1 ¶5`
- **impediment** — “the means they employed were from literal readings of receipts” `II.1 ¶6` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Pseudo-alchemists read the recipes literally and worked on mineral sulfur, mercury and salt without theory, and so failed.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶6 directly.
  - Also: “without rightly divining the true Identity of nature” `II.1 ¶6`
- **impediment** — “endeavored to draw light into focus of their vessels, to compress and entice the ether by mangetical disposition and attractions of various kinds” `II.1 ¶6` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Even those who glimpsed the Universal Subject failed when they tried to attract light or ether into external vessels, because they lacked the adepts' internal fire and vessel.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶6: 'being ignorant of that internal fire and vessel of the adepts, so essential to the accomplishment of the Hermetic work'.
  - Also: “For how hardly should they divine without instruction, or interpret the dark hieroglyphic seal?” `II.1 ¶6`
- **subject** — “the vessel; which is a divine secret, hidden from idolators, and without this knowledge no one can attain to the magistery” `II.1 ¶8` (QUOTED: Maria (endorsed by Atwood))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The vessel is the one thing the authors never openly explain and is indispensable. The chunk calls it 'internal' and links it to a 'living temple' and to matter 'extracted from thee', which points to the vessel being within the human being.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶6 'that internal fire and vessel of the adepts'; II.1 ¶8 the concealed vessel is guarded as 'that living temple wherein alone the wise ... raise their rejected Corner Stone'; II.1 ¶8 Morien 'extracted from thee'. The identification with man is inferred, not stated.
  - Also: “that internal fire and vessel of the adepts” `II.1 ¶6`
  - Also: “if the wise had not found a proper vessel in which to concoct it, the ethereal corner stone would never have been brought to light” `II.1 ¶8`
  - Also: “vas philosophorum est aqua eorum” `II.1 ¶8`
  - Also: “the place is the principle also of the supernatural generation” `II.1 ¶8`
- **agent** — “that internal fire” `II.1 ¶6` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** An inner fire of the adepts, essential to the work and contrasted with the chemist's fire 'for the tearing in pieces of bodies'. This chunk does not explain it further.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶6 (internal fire essential); II.1 ¶5 (ordinary fire achieves nothing vital).
- **procedure** — “the concealed vessel or art of vital administration” `II.1 ¶8` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Besides the vessel, a concealed practical art of 'vital administration' (handling of the vital force) was kept hidden. This chunk does not explain it.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶8 names it alongside the vessel as what was concealed; II.1 ¶5 makes the vital force the necessary agent.
- **result** — “their rejected Corner Stone and Ens of Light” `II.1 ¶8` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The product of the work, called ethereal and a being of light, raised only within the 'living temple'. This chunk does not explain it further.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶8: 'ethereal corner stone' brought to light only by a proper vessel; raised in 'that living temple'.
  - Also: “the ethereal corner stone” `II.1 ¶8`
- **principle** — “nor yet surmises the virtue that is in him, to prove and magnify the Universal Source” `II.1 ¶8` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The foundation of the whole art was man's knowledge of a power within himself by which he could prove and magnify the Universal Source; modern minds no longer investigate their own inner phenomena.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶8: 'Yet that was the foundation of the whole Hermetic magistery'; the mind unaccustomed to 'investigate, its own intrinsical phenomena'.
  - Also: “its own intrinsical phenomena” `II.1 ¶8`
- **procedure** — “applying the practice of Alchemy to human life; suggesting also, as did the latter, the method and medium of attraction” `II.1 ¶9` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The real practice of alchemy concerns human life, and involves a method and medium of attraction; writers who said so openly were thought to endanger the secret.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9: alchemists feared their art 'could little longer remain a secret' because these writers spoke openly in this way; that this is the true practice is implied by that alarm and by the chapter's argument.
  - Also: “even how he can draw and bring them to himself as a loadstone, iron” `II.1 ¶9`
- **agent** — “who is able to draw to himself the Central Salt of nature in abundance” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: the Moorish philosopher (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Man can attract into himself a central substance of nature. This chunk does not say what the Central Salt is.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9 states the ability; the nature of the Salt is not explained.
- **procedure** — “the soul of man, being estranged from the corporeal senses, adheres to a divine nature, from which it receives those things which it cannot search into by it sown power” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Agrippa (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** When the soul is withdrawn from the bodily senses and the mind is freed from the body, it joins a divine nature and comprehends all things, receiving knowledge it cannot reach by its own power.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9 directly.
  - Also: “when the mind is free, the reins of the body being loosed and going forth, as out of a close prison, it transcends the bonds of the members” `II.1 ¶9`
- **principle** — “Whosoever, therefore, shall know himself, shall know all things in himself; but especially he shall know God, according to whose image he was made” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Agrippa (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Self-knowledge is the key: because man is the image of God and of the world, knowing oneself gives knowledge of God, the world and all creatures, and the power to draw them to oneself.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9 (Agrippa); repeated by Sendivogius II.1 ¶10.
  - Also: “if thou knowest how to know thyself, and art not of a stiff neck, mayest easily comprehend” `II.1 ¶10`
- **principle** — “Thou hast in thy body the anatomy of the whole world, and all thy members answer to some celestials” `II.1 ¶10` (QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Man is a microcosm: every level of creation has a counterpart in him (matter, elements, plants, animals, heavens, angels, God), which is why he can be the one subject containing all.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶10; II.1 ¶9 Agrippa's list of correspondences.
  - Also: “he symbolizes with matter in a proper subject; with the elements in a fourfold body” `II.1 ¶9`
  - Also: “man was said to be the express Image of God, seeing he contains the Universal Reason within himself” `II.1 ¶9`
- **impediment** — “nature, having her proper light, is by the shadowy body of sense, hidden from our eyes” `II.1 ¶10` (QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** The bodily senses and the body itself hide nature's own light; the body covers man's nature as clothing covers the body.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶10 directly.
  - Also: “the body of man is a shadow of the seed of nature; and as man’s body is covered with a garment, so is man’s nature covered with the body” `II.1 ¶10`
- **sign** — “presently the cloud is taken away from before his eyes, and without any let, he can behold the point of our lodestone, answering to each center of the beams (viz. of the sun and moon philosophical)” `II.1 ¶10` (QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** When the light of nature enlightens a person, the obscuring cloud lifts and he perceives a central 'point' of attraction linked to the philosophical sun and moon. The chunk does not explain the point or the sun and moon.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶10: the sequence (enlightened, cloud removed, behold the point) is stated; the meaning of the image is not.
  - Also: “The eyes of the wise look upon nature otherwise than the eyes of common men” `II.1 ¶10`
- **agent** — “there is in the air a secret food of life, whose invisible congealed spirit is better than the whole world” `II.1 ¶10` (QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Man lives by a hidden life-giving spirit in the air. This chunk does not explain it further.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶10: 'Man was created of the earth, and lives by virtue of the air'.
- **operator** — “the soul in man, the lesser world or microcosm, substituting the place of its center, is the king, and is placed in the vital spirit in the purest blood” `II.1 ¶10` (QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** 'The king' is the soul, the centre of man, seated in the vital spirit in the purest blood; it governs the mind, which governs the body.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶10: explicit identification; 'That governs the mind, and the mind the body'.
  - Also: “That governs the mind, and the mind the body” `II.1 ¶10`
- **principle** — “hath a far greater operation out of the body, because out of the body it absolutely reigns” `II.1 ¶10` (QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** The soul acts far more powerfully when operating outside the body than within it; this, with its divine character, distinguishes man from other creatures.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶10: other creatures 'have only spirit and not the soul of Deity'.
- **operator** — “in his regenerate Wisdom possesseth all things” `II.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: the Moorish philosopher, Agrippa, Sendivogius (endorsed))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The operator in view is the regenerate man, who knows himself and God, with faith and wisdom and not stiff-necked; the vessel is hidden from idolators and the mysteries from the unworthy. That these are requirements is inferred.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶9 'regenerate Wisdom', 'by knowing and loving Him', 'preserved with God and the intelligences by faith and wisdom'; II.1 ¶8 'hidden from idolators'; II.1 ¶10 'not of a stiff neck', 'the unworthy'. The texts describe what the regenerate/self-knowing man can do; they do not state these as conditions.
  - Also: “by knowing and loving Him” `II.1 ¶9`
  - Also: “speak of these things sparingly only, and figuratively, lest those divine mysteries, by which nature is illustrated, should be discovered to the unworthy” `II.1 ¶10`
- **impediment** — “his revealment was instantly annulled by false or weakening commentaries, or as quickly as possible withdrawn” `II.1 ¶8` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The texts are deliberately misleading: open hints about the vessel were cancelled by false commentaries or diversions, so the reader must not take such equivocations at face value.
  - Basis: II.1 ¶8, with the example of Sendivogius diverting from Morien's 'extracted from thee' to 'gold found sticking between a dead man’s teeth'.
  - Also: “by inveigling the reader into a doubt artfully raised about some gold found sticking between a dead man’s teeth” `II.1 ¶8`

## Terms

- **Alchemy** — The true art, unrelated to modern chemistry except in borrowed terms used to veil it; requires the vital force; esteemed by philosophers for knowledge of nature and of the Creator, not for gold. (ATWOOD)
  - “No modern art or chemistry, notwithstanding all its surreptitious claims, has any thing in common to do with Alchemy, beyond the borrowed terms” `II.1 ¶5`
  - “not so much out of covetousness for gold or silver, but for the knowledge’s sake” `II.1 ¶10`
- **magistery** (also: Hermetic magistery) — The accomplished art or mastery of the work; its foundation was man's virtue within himself to prove the Universal Source; unattainable without knowledge of the vessel. (ATWOOD)
  - “Yet that was the foundation of the whole Hermetic magistery” `II.1 ¶8`
  - “without this knowledge no one can attain to the magistery” `II.1 ¶8`
- **vessel** (also: vessels, concealed vessel) — The indispensable, deliberately concealed container in which the work is 'concocted'; the adepts' vessel is called internal; Hermes' Latin, in our translation, says it is the philosophers' water. (ATWOOD)
  - = water (of the philosophers): “vas philosophorum est aqua eorum” `II.1 ¶8`
  - “that internal fire and vessel of the adepts” `II.1 ¶6`
  - “with exception of the vessel; which is a divine secret, hidden from idolators” `II.1 ¶8`
- **internal fire** — The adepts' inner fire, essential to the work; not further explained here. (ATWOOD)
  - “that internal fire and vessel of the adepts, so essential to the accomplishment of the Hermetic work” `II.1 ¶6`
- **vital force** — The force without whose presence and action no real transformation is achieved. (ATWOOD)
  - “unless the vital force be present and in action” `II.1 ¶5`
- **art of vital administration** — A concealed practical art, named alongside the vessel as what the ancients hid; not explained here. (ATWOOD)
  - “the concealed vessel or art of vital administration” `II.1 ¶8`
- **Universal Subject** — The one true subject of the art, glimpsed by a few pseudo-alchemists who nonetheless failed. (ATWOOD)
  - “glimpses of the Universal Subject” `II.1 ¶6`
- **true Identity of nature** (also: identity) — What the pseudo-alchemists failed to divine; also what one would have to search out through all creatures if one did not take the one subject containing all. (ATWOOD)
  - “without rightly divining the true Identity of nature” `II.1 ¶6`
  - “to search out the identity through all creatures and minerals, by way of experiment” `II.1 ¶9`
- **ether** — Something some pseudo-alchemists tried to compress and attract into their vessels by magnetical means, without success. (ATWOOD)
  - “to compress and entice the ether by mangetical disposition and attractions of various kinds” `II.1 ¶6`
- **conceptive vehicle** — The ancients' vehicle of conception, about whose nature their obscure instructions caused great error; not further defined. (ATWOOD)
  - “the obscure instructions of the ancients concerning the nature of their conceptive vehicle, has caused incalculable error and confusion” `II.1 ¶8`
- **Universal Source** — That which man, through the virtue in him, could prove and magnify; doing so was the foundation of the magistery. (ATWOOD)
  - “nor yet surmises the virtue that is in him, to prove and magnify the Universal Source” `II.1 ¶8`
- **Corner Stone** (also: ethereal corner stone, rejected Corner Stone) — The product brought to light by the work in a proper vessel, raised within the 'living temple'; called ethereal and joined with 'Ens of Light'. (ATWOOD)
  - “the ethereal corner stone would never have been brought to light” `II.1 ¶8`
  - “raise their rejected Corner Stone and Ens of Light” `II.1 ¶8`
- **Ens of Light** — Named together with the rejected Corner Stone as what the wise raise in the living temple; not separately defined. (ATWOOD)
  - “raise their rejected Corner Stone and Ens of Light” `II.1 ¶8`
- **living temple** — The place, protected by the adepts' equivocations, wherein alone the wise raise the Corner Stone. (ATWOOD)
  - “that living temple wherein alone the wise of all ages have been securely able to raise their rejected Corner Stone” `II.1 ¶8`
- **Mercurial Spirit** — A spirit present everywhere, in all things according to their nature, but not equally extractable from all nor of sufficient virtue in every form. (QUOTED: Boehme, Agrippa and Paracelsus' disciples)
  - “the Mercurial Spirit is everywhere, and to be found in all things according to the nature of each” `II.1 ¶9`
- **Central Salt of nature** — Something man can draw to himself in abundance; not further defined. (QUOTED: the Moorish philosopher)
  - “who is able to draw to himself the Central Salt of nature in abundance” `II.1 ¶9`
- **regenerate Wisdom** — The wisdom of the man reformed into God's image, by which he possesses all things and unlocks nature's mysteries. (QUOTED: the Moorish philosopher)
  - “in his regenerate Wisdom possesseth all things, and with this light can unlock the most hidden and recluse mysteries of nature” `II.1 ¶9`
- **Image of God** (also: image) — Man, so called because he contains the Universal Reason and corresponds with all levels of creation. (QUOTED: Agrippa)
  - = man: “man was said to be the express Image of God, seeing he contains the Universal Reason within himself” `II.1 ¶9`
- **Universal Reason** — What man contains within himself, making him God's image. (QUOTED: Agrippa)
  - “he contains the Universal Reason within himself” `II.1 ¶9`
- **light of nature** — Nature's own light, hidden by the body of sense; when it enlightens someone it removes the cloud and penetrates inward things; the philosophers see all things in it as in a mirror. (QUOTED: Sendivogius)
  - “nature, having her proper light, is by the shadowy body of sense, hidden from our eyes” `II.1 ¶10`
  - “the light of nature doth enlighten any one, presently the cloud is taken away from before his eyes” `II.1 ¶10`
  - “so far doth the light of nature penetrate and discover inward things” `II.1 ¶10`
- **lodestone** (also: loadstone) — Image of attraction: man draws all things to himself as a loadstone draws iron; 'the point of our lodestone' is seen by the enlightened, answering to the centres of the beams of the philosophical sun and moon. (QUOTED: Agrippa; Sendivogius)
  - “draw and bring them to himself as a loadstone, iron” `II.1 ¶9`
  - “he can behold the point of our lodestone, answering to each center of the beams” `II.1 ¶10`
- **sun and moon philosophical** — Named only as the sources of the 'beams' whose centres the point of the lodestone answers; not explained here. (QUOTED: Sendivogius (gloss in parentheses, speaker UNCLEAR))
  - “viz. of the sun and moon philosophical” `II.1 ¶10`
- **seed of nature** — That of which man's body is a shadow. (QUOTED: Sendivogius)
  - “the body of man is a shadow of the seed of nature” `II.1 ¶10`
- **microcosm** — Man as 'the lesser world', having in his body the anatomy of the whole world. (QUOTED: Sendivogius)
  - = lesser world: “the lesser world or microcosm” `II.1 ¶10`
  - “Thou hast in thy body the anatomy of the whole world” `II.1 ¶10`
- **king** — In ¶10, the soul in man, his centre, seated in the vital spirit in the purest blood. In ¶7, the king whose secret is kept and whom the adepts barely mention. (QUOTED: Sendivogius (¶10); ATWOOD (¶7))
  - = the soul in man: “the soul in man, the lesser world or microcosm, substituting the place of its center, is the king” `II.1 ¶10`
  - “make little or no mention of the king at all” `II.1 ¶7`
- **vital spirit** — The seat of the soul, located in the purest blood. (QUOTED: Sendivogius)
  - “is placed in the vital spirit in the purest blood” `II.1 ¶10`
- **soul of Deity** — What man has and other creatures, having only spirit, lack. (QUOTED: Sendivogius)
  - “have only spirit and not the soul of Deity” `II.1 ¶10`
- **pseudo-Alchemists** — Those who sought gold with mineral sulfur, mercury and salt by literal reading of receipts, without theory. (ATWOOD)
  - “The pseudo-Alchemists dreamed of gold, and impossible transformations” `II.1 ¶6`

## Sources cited

- **Tractatus Aureus**, *Tractatus Aureus, cap. 1* — “Opus vobuscum et apud est, quod intus arripens et permanens in terra vel in mare habere potes.” `II.1 ¶2` — Chapter epigraph; in our translation it says the work is with the reader and taken hold of within (no comment by Atwood).
- **Tobit**, *the ancient book of Tobit* — “to be honorable to reveal the works of the Lord; but good t keep close the secret of a king” `II.1 ¶7` — Supports the adepts' practice of concealing 'the king'; Atwood claims to stay within 'the prophet’s advice' while revealing partially.
- **Hali** — “the place is the principle also of the supernatural generation” `II.1 ¶8` — Supports the necessity of the vessel/place (statement shared with Morien and Albert).
- **Morien**, *Morieni de Trans. Metal. Interrog. Et Resp.* — “This matter, O king, is extracted from thee” `II.1 ¶8, II.1 n1` — Supports the vessel/place doctrine; his advice to King Calid points to the matter being taken from man, a hint Sendivogius diverted.
- **Albert** — “the place is the principle also of the supernatural generation” `II.1 ¶8` — Supports the necessity of the vessel/place.
- **Hermes** — “vas philosophorum est aqua eorum” `II.1 ¶8` — Supports: identifies the philosophers' vessel with their water.
- **Maria**, *Maria Practica, in Artis Auriferae, vol. 2* — “Philosophers have spoken sufficiently of all that is necessary concerning the work, with exception of the vessel” `II.1 ¶8, II.1 n1` — Supports: the vessel is a divine secret, indispensable and never openly revealed.
- **Sendivogius**, *New Light of Alchemy* — “the soul in man, the lesser world or microcosm, substituting the place of its center, is the king” `II.1 ¶8, II.1 ¶10, II.1 n2, II.1 n5` — Criticized in ¶8 as an example of diverting readers from Morien's hint; endorsed in ¶10 on the light of nature, man as microcosm, and the soul as king.
- **Jacob Boehme** `II.1 ¶9` — Illustrates: his writings alarmed alchemists by applying alchemy openly to human life.
- **Rosicrucians** `II.1 ¶9` — Illustrates: certain Rosicrucians were alarmed by Agrippa's and Paracelsus' disciples' books.
- **Paracelsus’ disciples** `II.1 ¶9` — Illustrates/supports: applied alchemy to human life and suggested 'the method and medium of attraction'.
- **Agrippa**, *Occult Philosophy, Book 3, chap. 36 and 46* — “Whosoever, therefore, shall know himself, shall know all things in himself” `II.1 ¶9, II.1 n4` — Supports: soul freed from the senses comprehends all; man as image of God and microcosm; self-knowledge gives knowledge and attraction of all things.
- **the Moorish philosopher**, *Centrum Naturae Concentratum* — “contains not so great mysteries and excellences as Man reformed by God into his image” `II.1 ¶9, II.1 n3` — Supports: man himself is the best subject and can draw the Central Salt of nature.

## Ambiguities

- “make little or no mention of the king at all” `II.1 ¶7`
  - Reading: The 'king' is the hidden central subject of the work, which ¶10 (Sendivogius) identifies as the soul in man.
  - Reading: The 'king' is only the scriptural image from Tobit (God or a monarch whose secret is kept), standing generally for the concealed secret.
  - Basis: Reading 1: II.1 ¶10 'the soul in man ... is the king'; ¶8 Morien addresses 'O king' with 'extracted from thee'. Reading 2: ¶7 frames it purely through Tobit's 'secret of a king' and 'his monarchy'; Atwood does not link the two passages explicitly.
- “but this same sol, by which man differs from other animals” `II.1 ¶10`
  - Reading: Misprint for 'soul', continuing the subject 'the soul in man'.
  - Reading: Deliberate 'sol' (sun), linking the soul to the 'sun ... philosophical' earlier in the paragraph.
  - Basis: Reading 1: the sentence continues the soul's description and ends 'the soul of Deity'. Reading 2: 'sun and moon philosophical' appears earlier in II.1 ¶10.
- “the place is the principle also of the supernatural generation” `II.1 ¶8`
  - Reading: 'The place' is another name for the vessel.
  - Reading: 'The place' is distinct from the vessel, the two being the things 'they do not openly reveal either'.
  - Basis: Reading 1: it follows directly on the 'proper vessel' sentence. Reading 2: 'but they do not openly reveal either' implies two items (the place and the vessel/water).
- “the obscure instructions of the ancients concerning the nature of their conceptive vehicle” `II.1 ¶8`
  - Reading: The 'conceptive vehicle' is the vessel discussed in the same paragraph.
  - Reading: It is the hypothetically conceived 'matter' that the physical basis was identified with.
  - Basis: Reading 1: ¶8 goes on to the vessel 'in which to concoct it'. Reading 2: the clause is joined to 'a matter now, at best, hypothetically conceived of only'.
- “he can behold the point of our lodestone, answering to each center of the beams” `II.1 ¶10`
  - Reading: A central point of attraction in nature perceived by the enlightened.
  - Reading: The soul as man's centre, which the same paragraph says substitutes 'the place of its center'.
  - Basis: Reading 1: the lodestone image of attraction (¶9 'as a loadstone, iron'). Reading 2: II.1 ¶10 'substituting the place of its center, is the king'. The chunk does not decide.

## Translations

- `II.1 ¶2` “Opus vobuscum et apud est, quod intus arripens et permanens in terra vel in mare habere potes.” → The work is with you and among you; taking hold of it within, and abiding, you can have it on land or at sea. ('vobuscum' read as 'vobiscum'; 'apud' lacks its object in the text.) *(ours)*
- `II.1 ¶8` “vas philosophorum est aqua eorum” → the vessel of the philosophers is their water *(ours)*

## Threads

- `II.1 ¶3` 'Hitherto we have regarded this mystic labyrinth of Alchemy from without, considering the superficial scheme only' — points back to the exoteric treatment preceding this chapter.
- `II.1 ¶8` 'The inquiries hitherto made by us, concerning the physical basis of the Hermetic Science' — points to earlier inquiry identifying that basis with a matter now only hypothetically conceived.
- `II.1 ¶3` Atwood promises to 'offer the consenting reader our clue' before entering; the chapter's subject is the true subject and its 'concealed root' (heading ¶1).
