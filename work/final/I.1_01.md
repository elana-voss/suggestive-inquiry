# I.1_01

*Atwood opens the exoteric part of the book by setting aside questions of alchemy's temporal origin and name in favour of its means and principles, introduces Hermes Trismegistus and his writings (distinguishing the theosophic books from the Golden Treatise, which gives the physical ground and practical method), quotes the Smaragdine Table as the compressed principle and subject of the art, and presents Egypt's fall, with the Asclepian prophecy, as the loss of a superior sacred science through abuse and over-exposure.*

## Argument

- Part I is an 'Exoteric View' of alchemy; Chapter I surveys Hermetic philosophy's public history. `I.1 ¶1` — ATWOOD
- The Hermetic tradition opens early in the East, its monuments and records dark and enigmatical. Its origin (variously Adam, Noah, Cham, Solomon, Zoroaster, Hermes) is set aside: the inquiry concerns means and principles of occult science; eternal truth does not always originate where it is understood. `I.1 ¶2` — ATWOOD
- She leaves open the Syriadic pillars, Egypt or Palestine, and the Smaragdine table's authorship; alchemy is antique, perhaps coeval with the universe. Against Conringius's 'recent invention', Kircher, Borrichius, Vallensis and Greek authors suffice, though Egyptian record has perished. `I.1 ¶3` — ATWOOD
- A brief history of alchemical philosophers will show her later propositions are not gratuitous; English histories of philosophy neglect this once influential branch. `I.1 ¶4` — ATWOOD
- Per Plutarch, Egypt was called Cham or Chemia for its black soil; others derive name and art from Cham, who first practised the art of Vulcan there. Chemia also meant the eye's pupil and black matters, perhaps why alchemy was called a Black Art. `I.1 ¶5` — QUOTED: Plutarch
- Etymology corroborates but cannot discover. She begins with Hermes Trismegistus, adept king (per Suidas c. 400 years before Moses), famed for skill in nature's secret operations and the 'quintessential perfectibility of the three kingdoms'; hence 'Thrice Great'. `I.1 ¶6` — ATWOOD
- His books perished under Dioclesian; the Asclepian Dialogues and Poimander survive (Ficinus; Everard). The Poimander could move a skeptic to intelligible reality. These books treat divine capabilities and regeneration, not their physical ground, which with the practical method is in the Tractatus Aureus. The Smaragdine Table is said to contain the art's working principle and total subject. `I.1 ¶7` — ATWOOD
- Table: above as below, for the miracles of the One Thing, from which all arose by adaptation; Sun father, Moon mother, wind carrier, Earth nurse; power integral if turned into earth; separate earth from fire, subtle from gross, gently; it ascends, descends, gains strength of superiors and inferiors; overcomes subtle, penetrates solid; so the world was created; it concerns the operation of the Sun. `I.1 ¶8, I.1 ¶9` — QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table)
- The Table is all that remains of Egypt's Sacred Art. Her ruins evidence lost, superior science; Pythagoras, Thales, Democritus and Plato were initiated there into that Art, by which Egypt governed, keeping the profane in abeyance, until self-abuse and neglect of her law brought ruin, as foretold. `I.1 ¶10` — ATWOOD
- Asclepian prophecy: religion becomes fables, divinity returns to heaven, darkness preferred, the religious counted insane, death for followers of the 'Religion of Intellect', divine voice silenced, earth and air corrupted. `I.1 ¶10, I.1 n11` — QUOTED: Asclepian Dialogue of Hermes
- Atwood rejects the modern charge of forgery: the prophecy foretold a falling off, but early Christianity was a revival; Lactantius and Augustine cite it without doubt. Faith then rested on 'reality and living operation'; regeneration was rationally understood. Egypt's fulfilment came partly later, when sacred mysteries over-exposed to the multitude were perverted. `I.1 ¶11` — ATWOOD

## Method

- **principle** — “an inquiry which rather relates to the means and principles of occult science than to the period and place of their reputed discovery” `I.1 ¶2` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The book's concern is how the occult science works and on what principles, not its history of origin.
  - Also: “observe not how truth, being everywhere eternal, does not there always originate where it is understood” `I.1 ¶2`
- **principle** — “words do not convey true ideas unless their proper leader be previously entertained” `I.1 ¶6` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Words reveal their real meaning only to someone who already holds the guiding idea; word-study can confirm a truth once suggested but cannot discover it.
  - Also: “Etymological research has doubtless proved useful in leading on and corroborating truths once suggested, but it is not a way of first discovery” `I.1 ¶6`
- **principle** — “denote rather our divine capabilities and promise of regeneration than the physical ground of either” `I.1 ¶7` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The Poimander and Asclepian books describe human divine capacities and the promise of regeneration, not their physical ground; that physical ground, together with alchemy's practical method, is said to be given in the Golden Treatise.
  - Also: “this, with the practical method of alchemy being further given in the Tractatus Aureus, or Golden Treatise” `I.1 ¶7`
- **subject** — “is said to comprehend the working principle and total subject of the art” `I.1 ¶7` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The Smaragdine Table is said to state, in a few lines, both the operative principle and the whole subject-matter of alchemy.
- **result** — “the quintessential perfectibility of the three kingdoms in their homogeneal unity” `I.1 ¶6` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Hermes' reputed discovery: three 'kingdoms' share one homogeneous nature and can be perfected to a quintessence. This chunk does not name the three kingdoms.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶6, read literally; the parallel 'having the Three Parts of the philosophy of the whole world' (I.1 ¶9) and 'all things arose from this one thing' (I.1 ¶9) support a single common nature, but neither identifies the kingdoms.
- **operator** — “whence he is called the Thrice Great Hermes, having the spiritual intelligence of all things in their universal law” `I.1 ¶6` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Hermes is called 'Thrice Great' because of his reputed discovery about the three kingdoms: he had spiritual understanding of all things under their universal law.
  - Also: “Therefore I am I called Thrice Great Hermes, having the Three Parts of the philosophy of the whole world” `I.1 ¶9`
- **principle** — “that which is above is as that which is below, and that which is below is as that which is above, for performing the miracles of the One Thing” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Higher and lower correspond; this correspondence is what makes the work on the One Thing possible.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9: the clause 'for performing the miracles of the One Thing' ties the correspondence to the work; the chunk gives no further gloss.
- **subject** — “the One Thing” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The single subject of the work, from which all things arose by adaptation and which is called the father of all perfection. This chunk does not say what it is.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9 'all things arose from this one thing by adaptation', 'This is the father of all perfection'; I.1 ¶7 says the Table comprehends the 'total subject of the art'.
  - Also: “as all things were from one, by the mediation of one, so all things arose from this one thing by adaptation” `I.1 ¶9`
- **principle** — “So the world was created. Hence were all wonderful adaptations of which this is the manner.” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The process described for the One Thing is the same process by which the world was created; the work follows the pattern of creation.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9: 'So the world was created' follows directly on the description of the operation; 'as all things were from one ... so all things arose from this one thing'.
- **agent** — “the father of it is the Sun, the mother of it is the Moon; the wind carries it in its belly; the nurse thereof is the Earth” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The One Thing is generated from two principles called Sun and Moon, carried by 'wind' and nourished by 'Earth'. This chunk does not explain these names.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9 only; the parentage imagery is given without gloss.
- **procedure** — “The power of it is Integral, if it be turned into earth.” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The One Thing's power becomes integral (complete) when it is turned into earth. Not explained in this chunk.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9, literal reading; 'it ascends from earth to heaven, and again descends to earth' in the same paragraph also ends in earth.
- **procedure** — “Thou shalt separate the earth from the fire, the subtle from the gross, gently with much sagacity” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** A separation of earth from fire and of subtle from gross, done gently and with great judgment. This chunk does not say which part corresponds to which.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9: the two clauses are set in apposition; 'gently with much sagacity' gives the manner. Pairing of the terms is open (see ambiguities).
- **stage** — “it ascends from earth to heaven, and again descends to earth: and receives the strength of the superiors and of the inferiors” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** After separation the subject rises and falls again, a circulation through which it gathers the powers of both higher and lower; this is when it attains 'the glory of the whole world'. Not further explained here.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9: sequence follows the separation instruction and is followed by 'so thou hast the glory of the whole world'; links to 'that which is above is as that which is below'.
- **result** — “This is the strong fortitude of all fortitudes, overcoming every subtle and penetrating every solid thing.” `I.1 ¶9` (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The completed product is the strongest power, prevailing over both subtle and solid things. Not explained in this chunk.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9, literal reading.
  - Also: “so thou hast the glory of the whole world; therefore let all obscurity flee before thee” `I.1 ¶9`
  - Also: “This is the father of all perfection, or consummation of the whole world.” `I.1 ¶9`
- **principle** — “For what else, but for the knowledge of that mighty Art with which she arose, governed, and dazzled the whole contemporary world” `I.1 ¶10` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The wisdom into which Egypt initiated Greek philosophers was the alchemical Art itself; it was also the basis of Egypt's government and power.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶10: rhetorical questions linking initiation of Pythagoras, Thales, Democritus, Plato to 'that mighty Art'; I.1 ¶10 calls the Table what remains of Egypt's 'Sacred Art'.
- **impediment** — “sacred mysteries, too openly exposed to the multitude, became perverted and vilified by their abuse” `I.1 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Sacred knowledge is corrupted when exposed too openly to the multitude and when its law is neglected; Egypt preserved her Art by keeping the profane in abeyance.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶11 states the over-exposure of 'sacred mysteries'; I.1 ¶10 ties Egypt's fall to self-abuse and neglect of 'the law, by which she governed' while she held the profane in abeyance. Equating the 'sacred mysteries' with the Art itself is inference.
  - Also: “holding in strong abeyance the ignorant, profane, vulgar, until the evil day of desolation came with self-abuse, when, neglecting to obey the law, by which she governed, all fell” `I.1 ¶10`
- **result** — “faith was then grounded in reality and living operation, and the mystery of human regeneration, so zealously proclaimed, was also rationally understood” `I.1 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Atwood holds that human regeneration was once understood rationally, as a real, living operation and not merely a belief.
  - Also: “the Spirit of the Word was potent in faith to heal and save” `I.1 ¶11`

## Terms

- **Alchemy** (also: Alchemy, alchemy, Alchemical) — The Hermetic art/science; antique, perhaps coeval with the universe; Egypt's 'Sacred Art', of which the Smaragdine Table is the sole surviving Egyptian record; its practical method is given in the Tractatus Aureus; stigmatized as a 'Black Art' possibly from the word Chemia. (ATWOOD)
  - “consider Alchemy as an antique arifice coeval, for aught we know to the contrary, with the universe itself” `I.1 ¶3`
  - “whence in part perhaps Alchemy, so obscurely descended, has been likewise stigmatized as a Black Art” `I.1 ¶5`
  - “This Emerald Table, unique and authentic as it may be regarded, is all that remains to us from Egypt of her Sacred Art.” `I.1 ¶10`
- **Hermetic Science** (also: Hermetic Science, Hermetic Philosophy, Hermetic tradition) — The tradition attributed to Hermes; early, eastern, 'romantic and mystical', recorded in dark and enigmatical monuments, emblems and writings. (ATWOOD)
  - “The Hermetic tradition opens early with the morning dawn in the eastern world. All pertaining thereto is romantic and mystical.” `I.1 ¶2`
- **occult science** — The subject of the inquiry, considered as to its means and principles. (ATWOOD)
  - “the means and principles of occult science” `I.1 ¶2`
- **Chemia** (also: Chemia, Chemeia, Chimia, Chemiae) — Per Plutarch, an old name of Egypt, from the blackness of her soil; also the seeing pupil of the eye and other black matters. Others derive name and art from Cham. n8 cites Suidas: 'Chimia est auri et argenti confectio' (our translation: Chimia is the making of gold and silver). (QUOTED: Plutarch)
  - = Egypt: “its mother land, Cham; for so, during a long period, according to Plutarch, was Egypt denominated, or Chemia” `I.1 ¶5`
  - = Cham: “its mother land, Cham; for so, during a long period, according to Plutarch, was Egypt denominated, or Chemia” `I.1 ¶5`
  - “by the word Chemia, says Plutarch, the seeing pupil of the human eye was also designated, and other black matters” `I.1 ¶5`
  - “Chimia est auri et argenti confectio” `I.1 n8`
- **Black Art** — Stigmatizing name for alchemy, perhaps from Chemia meaning black matters. (ATWOOD)
  - “has been likewise stigmatized as a Black Art” `I.1 ¶5`
- **art of Vulcan** — The art said to have been first practised in Egypt by Cham, from whom some derive both name and art of alchemy. (QUOTED: others (unnamed))
  - “it was there that the art of Vulcan was first practiced by Cham, one of the sons of the Patriarch” `I.1 ¶5`
- **three kingdoms** — Not identified in this chunk; Hermes reputedly discovered their 'quintessential perfectibility' in their 'homogeneal unity'. (ATWOOD)
  - “the quintessential perfectibility of the three kingdoms in their homogeneal unity” `I.1 ¶6`
- **One Thing** (also: One Thing, one thing) — In the Smaragdine Table, that from which all things arose by adaptation, whose father is the Sun and mother the Moon; called father of all perfection. Not further identified here. (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - “for performing the miracles of the One Thing” `I.1 ¶9`
- **adaptation** (also: adaptations) — The manner in which all things arose from the One Thing; also the 'wonderful adaptations' of which the Table's process is the manner. (QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table))
  - “so all things arose from this one thing by adaptation” `I.1 ¶9`
  - “Hence were all wonderful adaptations of which this is the manner.” `I.1 ¶9`
- **regeneration** — Subject of the Poimander and Asclepian books together with 'our divine capabilities'; it has a 'physical ground' treated with the practical method in the Tractatus Aureus; in early Christianity it was 'rationally understood'. (ATWOOD)
  - “denote rather our divine capabilities and promise of regeneration than the physical ground of either” `I.1 ¶7`
  - “the mystery of human regeneration, so zealously proclaimed, was also rationally understood” `I.1 ¶11`
- **theosophic** — Characterizes the subjects of the Poemander and Asclepias: ultimate, concerning divine capabilities rather than physical ground. (ATWOOD)
  - “the subjects treated of in the books of the Poemander and Asclepias are theosophic and ultimate” `I.1 ¶7`
- **Religion of Intellect** — In the Asclepian prophecy, the religion whose follower will be punished with death in the degenerate age. Not further explained. (QUOTED: Asclepian Dialogue of Hermes)
  - “a capital punishment shall be appointed for him who applies himself to the Religion of Intellect” `I.1 ¶10`
- **sacred mysteries** — Sacred knowledge/rites which, too openly exposed to the multitude, became perverted and vilified. (ATWOOD)
  - “sacred mysteries, too openly exposed to the multitude, became perverted and vilified by their abuse” `I.1 ¶11`
- **Exoteric** — Qualifies Part I's view of alchemy's progress and theory (used in the heading only). (ATWOOD)
  - “An Exoteric View of the Progress and Theory of Alchemy” `I.1 ¶1`

## Sources cited

- **Herman Conringius**, *De Hermetica Aegyptior, vetere et Paracelsior. Nova Medicina.* `I.1 ¶3, I.1 n1` — Criticized: tried to slight alchemy as a recent invention.
- **A. Kircher**, *Oedipus Aegyptiacus; de Lapide Philos. Dissert.* `I.1 ¶3, I.1 n2, I.1 ¶7` — Supports alchemy's antiquity; also source of the Latin rendering of the Smaragdine Table from Arabic and Greek copies.
- **Olaus Borrichius**, *De Ortu et Progressu Chemiae* `I.1 ¶3, I.1 n3` — Supports alchemy's antiquity ('the learned Dane').
- **Robert Vallensis**, *De Veritate et Antiquitate Artis Chemiae (in the first volume of the Theatrum Chemicum)* `I.1 ¶3, I.1 n4` — Supports alchemy's antiquity.
- **the best Greek Authors, historical and philosophic** `I.1 ¶3` — Provide collateral probability for alchemy's antiquity.
- **Plutarch** — “by the word Chemia, says Plutarch, the seeing pupil of the human eye was also designated” `I.1 ¶5` — Illustrates etymology: Egypt called Cham/Chemia for black soil; Chemia also the pupil and black matters.
- **Suidas**, *de Verbo Chemiae; in Verbo Chemeia* — “Chimia est auri et argenti confectio” `I.1 ¶6, I.1 n6, I.1 n8` — Dating of Hermes before the Pharoahs, about 400 years before Moses; n8 cites his entry on Chemeia (on our translation, defining Chimia as the making of gold and silver).
- **Terullianus**, *de Anime* `I.1 n7` — Reference only (attached to Hermes' 'spiritual intelligence of all things').
- **Hermes (Trismegistus)** `I.1 ¶6, I.1 ¶7` — Starting point of the Hermetic story; adept king credited with the Hermetic writings.
- **Clemens Alexandrinus** `I.1 ¶7` — Names in detail the books attributed to Hermes.
- **Jamblicus**, *Jamblichus de Mysteriis, sect viii, cap. iv* `I.1 ¶7, I.1 n9` — Supports judging Hermes' lost books by the surviving Asclepian Dialogues and Poimander.
- **Asclepian Dialogues**, *Asclepian Dialogue of Hermes (Asclepias)* — “Oh, Egypt! Egypt! Fables alone shall remain of thy religion” `I.1 ¶7, I.1 ¶10, I.1 n11` — Theosophic Hermetic book; source of the prophecy of Egypt's fall, which Atwood defends as authentic.
- **Divine Poimander**, *Poemander* `I.1 ¶7` — Praised for sublimity of doctrine; concerns divine capabilities and regeneration rather than their physical ground.
- **Ficinus** `I.1 ¶7, I.1 n11` — Latin version of the Asclepian Dialogues and Poimander.
- **Dr Everard** `I.1 ¶7` — English translation of the Hermetic books, praised.
- **Tractatus Aureus**, *Hermetis Trismegisti Tractatus Aureus de Lapidis physici secreto (Golden Treatise, seven chapters)* `I.1 ¶7, I.1 n10` — Gives the physical ground and practical method of alchemy.
- **Smaragdine Table**, *Tabula Smaragdina Hermetis (also 'Smagardine table', 'Emerald Table')* — “that which is above is as that which is below” `I.1 ¶3, I.1 ¶7, I.1 ¶8, I.1 ¶9, I.1 ¶10` — Quoted in full as comprehending the working principle and total subject of the art; the only Egyptian remnant of the Sacred Art.
- **Herodotus** `I.1 ¶10` — Historian who recorded Egypt's remaining splendour after Cambyses.
- **T. Taylor**, *rendering of the Asclepian Dialogue; notes to the Prophecy, in Plotinus' Select Works* `I.1 n11, I.1 n12` — Translation used for the prophecy; reference for Augustine's view.
- **Apuleius** `I.1 ¶11` — Contrasts: hypothetical forger of the prophecy, a theory Atwood rejects.
- **Lactantius** `I.1 ¶11` — Supports authenticity of the prophecy: mentions it without doubt.
- **St Augustine** — “instinctu fallacies spiritus” `I.1 ¶11, I.1 n12` — Supports authenticity (mentions it without doubt), though he 'esteemed it instinctu fallacies spiritus' (ours: by prompting of a deceiving spirit), which Atwood attributes probably to the popular notion and thinks he might later have revised.

## Ambiguities

- “the quintessential perfectibility of the three kingdoms in their homogeneal unity” `I.1 ¶6`
  - Reading: three kingdoms of nature (unnamed here) sharing one homogeneous substance
  - Reading: the 'Three Parts of the philosophy of the whole world' named in the Table
  - Basis: Only ¶6 names the 'three kingdoms'; ¶9 links Hermes' title to 'Three Parts of the philosophy'; ¶6 links the same title to the kingdoms. The chunk does not say whether these are the same triad.
- “That which I have written is consummated concerning the operation of the Sun” `I.1 ¶9`
  - Reading: the operation of the 'Sun' named as father of the One Thing
  - Reading: the work as a whole, called the operation of the Sun
  - Basis: ¶9: 'the father of it is the Sun'; the closing line summarizes the whole Table. No gloss given.
- “escaped the destroying hand of Dioclesian (8)” `I.1 ¶7`
  - Reading: n8 documents (on our translation) that the destroyed books concerned chimia, the making of gold and silver, citing Suidas
  - Reading: n8's connection to the sentence is not stated
  - Basis: n8 reads 'Chimia est auri et argenti confectio (etc.)... Suida in Verbo Chemeia' and is attached to the Dioclesian clause without explanation.
- “Thou shalt separate the earth from the fire, the subtle from the gross” `I.1 ¶9`
  - Reading: earth = subtle, fire = gross (by word order)
  - Reading: earth = gross, fire = subtle (not ruled out by the text)
  - Reading: two separate separations, not paired
  - Basis: I.1 ¶9 sets the clauses in apposition without gloss; nothing in the chunk fixes the correspondence.

## Threads

- `I.1 ¶4` 'the propositions we may hereafter have occasion to offer' and 'a stranger subject': later parts of the book will advance propositions that this historical account is meant to support.
- `I.1 ¶7` The Tractatus Aureus (Golden Treatise, seven chapters) is said to give the physical ground of regeneration and the practical method of alchemy; not quoted in this chunk.
- `I.1 ¶11` Sacred mysteries perverted by over-exposure to the multitude: theme of the Mysteries and their abuse, stated here without development.
