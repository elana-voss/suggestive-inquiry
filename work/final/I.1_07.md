# I.1_07

*Continuing the public history of alchemy, Atwood describes how public credulity turned to hatred and then to skepticism, and how true adepts suffered persecution and hid themselves. She then surveys notable figures and writings (Dee and Kelly, Boehme, the German adepts, Maier, Sendivogius' New Light, English authors, Eirenaeus Philalethes), with accounts of projection and notes on which writings are useful to the student.*

## Argument

- Public trust in alchemy turned into hatred of the deluding alchemists and the art. True adepts, whom the public could not tell from frauds, were exiled, arrested and tortured to force confessions. `I.1 ¶66` — ATWOOD
- Seton lives in permanent flight. He possesses all things but enjoys only the truth. When he healed patients the physicians had given up, talk of the Elixir forced him to disguise himself. He wishes gold were as common as street mud. `I.1 ¶66, I.1 n73` — QUOTED: Alexander Seton ("this author", Open Entrance [Correction: Philalethes])
- Sendivogius was imprisoned; Khunrath, Von Welling, Fichtuld, Muller and Harprecht also suffered, and adepts hid their names and the evidence of Alchemy. Then indifference and a skepticism as blind as the earlier credulity set in. Philosophers accepted this, since the common herd valued gain over truth. `I.1 ¶66` — ATWOOD
- Some adepts carried their work into the secret Rosicrucian Fraternity. By report, it was founded by a German adept initiated in Arabia. Thomas Vaughan put its Fame and Confession into English. `I.1 ¶66` — ATWOOD
- Kelly, though something of a knave, probably practised transmutation with Red Powder gained by secret information, if not also able to perfect it himself. The Glastonbury powder (1 upon 272,330) was largely wasted in trials. Dee's diary records a tiny grain turning mercury into nearly an ounce of gold. Ashmole tells of a piece of a pan, merely warmed, turned to silver. `I.1 ¶66, I.1 n74` — ATWOOD
- Such lavishness marks those who came by the treasure by chance, never those who made it themselves. `I.1 ¶66` — ATWOOD
- Dee and Kelly's spirit conversations were mundane and fruitless, and their success is uncertain. One story says a dying monk later gave Kelly the secret; Dee's late diary says Kelly opened 'the great secret' to him. Dee returned home and is said to have died poor. Kelly, held prisoner by Rudolph, wrote De Lapide Philosophorum, a book of little value. `I.1 ¶67, I.1 n76` — ATWOOD
- Boehme and the Pordage and Lead school were on the same original track. Boehme is the plainest exponent of those who have entered the mystery experimentally. His works point explicitly to the physical basis of magic and the hidden material of the stone. A manuscript eulogy says all ancient wisdom is revealed anew in him. `I.1 ¶68, I.1 ¶69, I.1 n77` — ATWOOD
- Qualification: it was revealed anew theosophically, not intellectually. The Greeks' doctrine of Wisdom remains unequalled for scientific explanation of the divine source; this is to be discussed later. `I.1 ¶70` — ATWOOD
- Atwood lists the German and other adepts. Maier ranks first among those who explained fable by the Hermetic key, though he is deeply guarded. The engravings and title pages of these books repay reflection. `I.1 ¶70, I.1 n78, I.1 n89` — ATWOOD
- Sendivogius' New Light (translated by French, and from the French edition by Digby) claims to rest on nature and manual experience. Its deliberate disorder, and its conflict with common ideas of nature, require repeated study and prior knowledge. `I.1 ¶71, I.1 n90, I.1 n91` — ATWOOD
- Britain's few adepts were great and comparatively frank; even the skeptic Dufresnoy grants the English depth in Hermetic science. `I.1 ¶72, I.1 n92` — ATWOOD
- Atwood names the anonymous Eirenaeus Philalethes as the foremost later adept. His identity is unknown and he is confused with Harprecht, Vaughan and Sendivogius/Seton. He says he was English-born, had the secret young and was persecuted. `I.1 ¶73` — ATWOOD
- Starkey says his master had the White and Red elixir and gave him White medicine able to turn 120,000 times its weight into silver. Ignorant and covetous, Starkey wasted most of it. `I.1 ¶73, I.1 n93` — QUOTED: Starkey

## Method

- **result** — “being 1 upon 272,330” `I.1 ¶66` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The Powder of Projection is said to be so potent that one part transmutes 272,330 parts. Misjudging its strength wastes it.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66 gives the ratio and says they lost much in trials before finding its true strength.
  - Also: “they lost a great portion in trial before they found out the true height of the medicine” `I.1 ¶66`
- **procedure** — “projection with one small grain (in proportion no larger than the least grain of sand) upon an ounce and a quarter of common mercury, which produced almost an ounce of pure gold” `I.1 ¶66` (QUOTED: Dee's Diary (reported by Atwood))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** In 'projection', a tiny amount of the medicine or powder is applied to a base metal (here, mercury), which is then reported to become gold.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66: 'making projection' describes this operation, and the warming-pan story says 'the elixir being projected thereon'. The meaning is inferred from these two narrative uses.
  - Also: “of a pound of mercury I have made within less than a scruple of a pound of silver” `I.1 ¶73`
- **procedure** — “the elixir being projected thereon, it was transmuted into pure silver” `I.1 ¶66` (QUOTED: Ashmole)
  - **Plain (stated):** In this account, projection needed only gentle heat: the metal was just warmed in the fire, not melted, and changed into silver without Kelly touching it.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66: 'without Kelly touching or handling it, or melting the copper even, only warming it in the fire'.
- **sign** — “This kind of profusion has been frequently exhibited by such as are reported to have come by the treasure casually, never by those who have themselves confected it.” `I.1 ¶66` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Lavish spending marks someone who came by the stone by chance. Those who actually made it are restrained, like a 'sober philosopher'.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66, applied to Kelly ('openly profuse beyond the modest limits ... of a sober philosopher').
  - Also: “became possessed of the Red Powder by some secret kind of information, if not of the means of perfecting it by his own art” `I.1 ¶66`
- **result** — “having ventured to administer the medicine to sufferers given over by physicians” `I.1 ¶66` (QUOTED: Alexander Seton)
  - **Plain (stated):** The medicine is also used to heal the sick, including cases physicians had given up on, and these cures made people talk of 'the Elixir'.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66: 'the instant the cures became known, a report was spread about of the Elixir'.
- **result** — “he gave me upwards of two ounces of the White medicine of sufficient virtue to convert 120,000 times its weight into the purest silver” `I.1 ¶73` (QUOTED: Starkey)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** There are two forms of the elixir, White and Red. The White transmutes into silver; this passage does not say what the Red does.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶73: 'the White and Red elixir'; the White medicine converts to silver. Matching Red with gold is not stated in this chunk.
  - Also: “in my master’s possession the White and Red elixir in very large quantity” `I.1 ¶73`
- **impediment** — “with this treasure I went to work ignorantly and was caught in the trap of my own covetousness” `I.1 ¶73` (QUOTED: Starkey)
  - **Plain (stated):** Ignorance and greed ruin the work: even someone who has the medicine wastes it if they do not understand its value.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶73: 'I expended or wasted nearly all of this tincture, and did not know its value until it was nearly gone'.
- **impediment** — “even the true adepts --- for the public knew not to distinguish --- suffered equal cruelty and abundant inconvenience” `I.1 ¶66` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Outside obstacles to the art: public hostility and others' greed for gold lead to persecution, so adepts must hide themselves and the evidence of their work.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66: arrests and torture 'in order to extort confession'; 'cautious to conceal their names, with the evidence of Alchemy'.
  - Also: “I am constrained even from the works of mercy, for fear of suspicion and arrest” `I.1 ¶66`
  - Also: “he possessed the secret at a very early age, and was the victim of unremitting persecution” `I.1 ¶73`
- **principle** — “But philosophers were content to have it so; observing the incapability of the common herd, and how little they cared for the truth, or the witness of nature’s greatest miracles” `I.1 ¶66` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Adepts are willing to let the public stay skeptical, because the crowd cannot grasp the truth and values gain above it. This is a reason for secrecy.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66.
  - Also: “the good and innocent now suffered more and more than ever cautious to conceal their names, with the evidence of Alchemy, from the world” `I.1 ¶66`
- **result** — “though I possess all things, I have no rest or enjoyment of any, except in the truth, which is my whole satisfaction” `I.1 ¶66` (QUOTED: Alexander Seton)
  - **Plain (stated):** The adept's real reward is the truth itself, not wealth. Seton wishes gold were as common as mud.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶66, including 'Would to God that gold and silver were as common as the street mud'.
- **subject** — “full of explicit indications concerning the physical basis of magic and occult material of the philosophers’ stone” `I.1 ¶68` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The stone has a hidden ('occult') material, and magic has a physical basis. Atwood says Boehme's works point to both explicitly. This chunk does not say what that material is.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶68; the material itself is not identified here.
- **principle** — “of all who have hitherto entered experimentally into the mystery, the plainest, simplest, and most confidential exponent” `I.1 ¶68` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The mystery is something one enters by experience, not only by study. Boehme writes from such experience and gives the student a 'ground of experience and principles of the Divine Art' for decoding the old adepts' riddles.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶68.
  - Also: “such a ground of experience and principles of the Divine Art in his writings, as may help the student to conceive profoundly and lead him to the means of understanding the enigmas of the old adepts” `I.1 ¶68`
- **principle** — “Revealed anew, it will be observed, theosophically, but not intellectually.” `I.1 ¶70` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Atwood separates two kinds of revelation. Boehme renewed the ancient wisdom theosophically (by inner or divine experience), but did not explain it intellectually. The Greeks' 'doctrine of Wisdom' remains unequalled for clear, scientific explanation of the divine source.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶70 contrasts 'theosophically' with 'intellectually' and praises the Greeks' 'scientific explication of the divine source'. Glossing 'theosophically' as inner experience relies on ¶68 ('entered experimentally into the mystery').
  - Also: “their doctrine of Wisdom in perspicuity, grace of utterance, and scientific explication of the divine source” `I.1 ¶70`
- **principle** — “of all those who have connected ancient fable with philosophy, and explained them by the Hermetic key, Michael Maier ranks first” `I.1 ¶70` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Ancient myths can be read as carrying Hermetic philosophy and decoded with the 'Hermetic key'. Maier is the leading interpreter, though he reveals very little openly.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶70; the chunk does not say what the 'Hermetic key' consists of.
  - Also: “since he is profoundly guarded in his revelations” `I.1 ¶70`
- **principle** — “even the title pages of many of them convey more idea and food for reflection than other modern tomes” `I.1 ¶70` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The engravings, woodcuts and title pages of these alchemical books convey ideas and repay reflection.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶70: 'Highly curious engravings and woodcuts adorn the works of these authors'.
- **principle** — “in consequence of the willful disorder and perplexity of the composition, repeated perusal and a certain knowledge are requisite, in order to gather its recondite drift” `I.1 ¶71` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Alchemical texts are deliberately disordered to hide their meaning. The reader needs repeated study and some prior knowledge to find the hidden drift.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶71 (on the New Light).
- **principle** — “professedly drawn from the fountain of nature, and grounded in manual experience” `I.1 ¶71` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The New Light claims to draw on nature and on hands-on practice, and its theory contradicts ordinary ideas of what nature can do.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶71: 'its theory and asserted facts are at variance with our common conceptions and experience of the possibility of nature'.
- **result** — “Master E. Kelly did open the great secret to me, God be thanked.” `I.1 ¶67` (QUOTED: Dee's Diary)
  - **Plain (stated):** Dee records that Kelly told him 'the great secret'. The chunk does not say what it was, and Atwood calls their success uncertain.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶67.

## Terms

- **Powder of Projection** — The agent of transmutation, said in the Dee/Kelly story to be so strong (1 upon 272,330) that its 'true height' had to be found by trial. (ATWOOD)
  - “a large quantity of the Powder of Projection in a niche amongst the ruins of Glastonbury Abbey” `I.1 ¶66`
- **Red Powder** — What Kelly is believed to have had, either by secret information or by making it himself. The text does not say outright that it is the same as the Powder of Projection (see ambiguities). (ATWOOD)
  - “became possessed of the Red Powder by some secret kind of information” `I.1 ¶66`
- **projection** (also: projected) — The operation of applying the elixir or powder to a metal to transmute it. (ATWOOD)
  - “In Dee’s diary we have the account of Kelly making projection with one small grain” `I.1 ¶66`
  - “However, I made projection of a part” `I.1 ¶73`
- **Elixir** (also: elixir) — The transmuting and healing agent. Seton uses 'the Elixir' in the same passage as 'the medicine' he gave to the sick. Starkey speaks of 'the White and Red elixir'. (UNCLEAR)
  - “a report was spread about of the Elixir” `I.1 ¶66`
  - “the White and Red elixir” `I.1 ¶73`
- **medicine** (also: White medicine) — The transmuting agent, which also heals the sick. Its strength is called its 'height' or 'virtue'. (UNCLEAR)
  - = tincture: “I expended or wasted nearly all of this tincture” `I.1 ¶73`
  - “the true height of the medicine” `I.1 ¶66`
  - “the White medicine of sufficient virtue to convert 120,000 times its weight into the purest silver” `I.1 ¶73`
- **transmutation** (also: transmuted) — Changing a metal (here mercury or copper) into gold or silver. (ATWOOD)
  - “there is reason to believe that he practiced transmutation” `I.1 ¶66`
- **adept** (also: adepts, adeptest) — A true master of the art, as opposed to the 'deluding alchemists'. (ATWOOD)
  - “even the true adepts --- for the public knew not to distinguish --- suffered equal cruelty” `I.1 ¶66`
  - “Henry Khunrath, a pious and learned adeptest” `I.1 ¶70`
- **philosophers’ stone** — The object of the art. It has an 'occult material', to which Boehme's works point. (ATWOOD)
  - “occult material of the philosophers’ stone” `I.1 ¶68`
- **Divine Art** — A name for the art. Boehme gives its 'principles' and a 'ground of experience' for it. (ATWOOD)
  - “principles of the Divine Art” `I.1 ¶68`
- **theosophically** (also: theosophist) — Used in contrast to 'intellectually' to describe how Boehme revealed the ancient wisdom anew. (ATWOOD)
  - “Revealed anew, it will be observed, theosophically, but not intellectually.” `I.1 ¶70`
- **Hermetic key** — The means of interpreting ancient fable as philosophy. The chunk does not say what it is. (ATWOOD)
  - “explained them by the Hermetic key” `I.1 ¶70`
- **doctrine of Wisdom** — The Greeks' teaching, praised as unequalled in its scientific explanation of the divine source. (ATWOOD)
  - “their doctrine of Wisdom in perspicuity, grace of utterance, and scientific explication of the divine source” `I.1 ¶70`
- **great secret** (also: the secret) — The secret of the art. ¶67 speaks of 'the secret of making of the Powder' confided to Kelly by a dying monk, and of Kelly opening 'the great secret' to Dee; ¶73 says Philalethes possessed 'the secret' young. The chunk does not say what it is. (ATWOOD)
  - “the secret of making of the Powder was confided to Kelly some years afterwards by a dying monk” `I.1 ¶67`
  - “Master E. Kelly did open the great secret to me” `I.1 ¶67`

## Ambiguities

- “says this author, in his Open Entrance [Correction: Philalethes]” `I.1 ¶66`
  - Reading: The quoted complaint is Alexander Seton's, as the sentence first states.
  - Reading: The quoted complaint is Philalethes', as the bracketed correction says.
  - Basis: ¶66 names Seton, but the bracketed correction says Philalethes. ¶73 lists 'An Open Entrance to the Shut Palace of the King' among Philalethes' works and says Philalethes has been confused with 'Sendivogius, who has been identified with him under the name of Alexander Seton'. ¶73 also says Philalethes was 'the victim of unremitting persecution', which matches the complaint.
- “possessed of the Red Powder” `I.1 ¶66`
  - Reading: The Red Powder is the same thing as the 'Powder of Projection' found at Glastonbury.
  - Reading: They are distinct, and the text only places them in the same story.
  - Basis: ¶66 mentions the Red Powder and then, beginning 'Thus it was generally reported', the Powder of Projection, but never states that they are identical.
- “something better even than a view of the Promised Land” `I.1 ¶68`
  - Reading: Boehme actually reached the goal of the art, not just a glimpse of it.
  - Reading: Boehme reached a spiritual attainment different from (and above) the art's goal.
  - Basis: ¶68 says only that Boehme and the others were 'on the same original track'. ¶70 says he revealed the wisdom 'theosophically, but not intellectually'.

## Threads

- `I.1 ¶66` 'the former credulity' refers back to the earlier period of public credulity and courting of alchemists described before this chunk.
- `I.1 ¶70` 'but of this hereafter': the Greeks' doctrine of Wisdom and the 'perfect veracity and ideality of their ground' are to be treated later.
- `I.1 n75` Footnote 75 ('See notes appended to the Theat. Chem. Brit.') has no marker in the main text; it probably belongs to the Dee/Kelly Glastonbury, warming-pan or ring accounts in ¶66.
- `I.1 ¶73` The confusion of Philalethes with Seton, Sendivogius, Harprecht and Thomas Vaughan bears on who wrote the 'Open Entrance' quoted in ¶66.
