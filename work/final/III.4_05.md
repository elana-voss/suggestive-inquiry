# III.4_05

*Concludes Atwood's instructions: the working-place must be secret, still and unpolluted by evil associates or lingering imaginative impressions; Vaughan's 'lessons' prescribe self-examination, moderate heat, charity, piety, solitude and perseverance; Atwood then ends her 'suggestive' introduction, saying the end is implied in the beginning and the rest must come from practice and the Master Key.*

## Argument

- Secrecy was a principal aim, hard to combine with other conditions varying with the spiritual guide; a locality like Virgil's for his Bees, without evil associates, is desirable. `III.4 ¶42` — ATWOOD
- Air currents disturb communion; imaginative impressures absorbed by the entranced Subject linger and pollute a place's ether (adepts, Cornelius Agrippa). A fitting working-place is a grace. `III.4 ¶42, III.4 ¶43` — ATWOOD
- Vaughan's lessons, adopted as conclusion: examine yourself; you co-operate with God. Excess heat divides the Chaos; the Quintessence leaves violent hands. Speak little. Knowing the First Matter finds Nature's Sanctuary; only the Door remains. `III.4 ¶44` — QUOTED: Vaughan (endorsed by Atwood)
- Vaughan: charity nurses faith; heart in heaven, hands on earth; live heavenly to commune with superiors; be one, not alone; day as contracted year; indeterminate agents until a determinate one; avoid extremes; perseverance ends in miracles. `III.4 ¶44` — QUOTED: Vaughan (endorsed by Atwood)
- Enough for suggestive purposes. Perseverance ranks next to benevolence and right intention; effects lead to causes; as in a seed, the end is implied in the beginning. `III.4 ¶45, III.4 ¶46` — ATWOOD
- The introduction to the Sphinx’s lair ends; applying the Master Key moves the first vital cause; practice opens the rest. `III.4 ¶47, III.4 ¶48` — ATWOOD

## Method

- **procedure** — “Secrecy having been a principal object with those practicing this Art” `III.4 ¶42` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Practitioners aimed above all at keeping the work secret, which was often hard to secure together with the other required conditions.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶42 states it directly.
- **operator** — “the other conditions, which vary with the constitution and instinct of the spiritual guide” `III.4 ¶42` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The proper conditions of the work are not fixed but depend on the constitution and instinct of the 'spiritual guide'; the chunk does not say who or what the guide is.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶42 only; see ambiguities for the identity of the 'spiritual guide'.
- **procedure** — “It is a grace - To finde a fitting working-place.” `III.4 ¶43` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The work needs a suitable place, like the locality Virgil appointed for his Bees, kept free of corrupt and evil associates (exclusions the text calls 'allegorical') and of strong air currents.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶42 calls such a locality 'desirable' and names the exclusions as allegorical; ¶43 gives the verse. The chunk does not describe Virgil's locality further or decode the allegory.
  - Also: “Just such a locality as Virgil appointed for his Bees, has been mentioned as desirable with all his appropriate allegorical exceptions of corrupt and evil associates” `III.4 ¶42`
- **impediment** — “Strong currents of air are well known to disturb communion” `III.4 ¶42` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Draughts or wind in the working-place disturb the 'communion' the work depends on; this chunk does not define communion.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶42 states it directly.
- **impediment** — “the entranced Subject is more or less susceptible of all imaginative impressures, which, even after their act has passed away, hang and pollute the ether of their pertinacious abode” `III.4 ¶42` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The person in trance (the 'Subject') takes in imaginative impressions; these linger in a place after the act that produced them and contaminate its 'ether', so the place must be clean of such residues.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶42 states susceptibility and lingering pollution, citing adepts and Cornelius Agrippa.
- **operator** — “consider well with thyself what manner of man thou art, and what it is that thou wouldst do: For it is no small matter.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan (endorsed by Atwood as 'lessons of an English Adept'))
  - **Plain (stated):** Before attempting the practice, the operator must examine his own character, intention, knowledge and practice.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 direct precepts.
  - Also: “let him first examine his knowledge, and especially his practice” `III.4 ¶44`
- **principle** — “Thou hast resolved with thyself to be a co-operator with the living God, and to minister to Him in his work of generation.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (stated):** The operator does not make the result himself; he assists God's own generative work and must not hinder it.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44, followed by 'Have a care, therefore, that thou dost not hinder his work'.
  - Also: “Have a care, therefore, that thou dost not hinder his work” `III.4 ¶44`
- **impediment** — “if thy heat exceeds the natural proportion, thou hast stirred up the wrath of the moist natures, and they will stand up against the central fire” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Applying 'heat' beyond the natural proportion sets the moist natures and the central fire against each other and divides the Chaos; the passage ties this failure to the operator's violent handling. What the heat literally is, is not said.
  - Basis: Consequences stated in III.4 ¶44; the link to operator violence rests on 'thy violent destructive Hands', 'a few violent knocks' and 'charitable and not destructive to thyself' in the same paragraph. See ambiguities.
  - Also: “there will be a terrible division in the Chaos” `III.4 ¶44`
- **agent** — “the sweet spirit of Peace, the true eternal Quintessence, will depart from the elements, leaving both of them and thee to confusion” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (stated):** The Quintessence, identified with the 'spirit of Peace', abides in the elements; under violent handling it withdraws and will not act on the Matter, leaving matter and operator in confusion.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44: the apposition asserts the identity; departure and refusal to 'apply himself' are stated.
  - Also: “neither will he apply himself to that Matter as long as it is in thy violent destructive Hands” `III.4 ¶44`
- **operator** — “Nature gave us one tongue and two ears, that we might hear much and speak little” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan, citing Zeno)
  - **Plain (stated):** The operator should listen and learn much and speak little, not publishing his ignorance.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 draws this application explicitly.
  - Also: “Let not any man therefore be ready to vomit forth his own shame and ignorance” `III.4 ¶44`
- **impediment** — “lest upon the experience of a few violent knocks he presume to judge of Nature in her very sobrieties” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Failures from forcing the work are no basis for judging how Nature works when handled gently.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 contrasts 'violent knocks' with Nature's 'sobrieties', following the warning against excess heat and violent hands.
- **subject** — “if thou knowest the principal First Matter, know also for certain thou hast discovered the Sanctuary of Nature” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (stated):** Knowing the First Matter is equivalent to having found the inner seat of Nature's treasures.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 asserts the equivalence directly.
- **stage** — “There is nothing between thee and her treasures but the Door: that indeed must be opened.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Once the First Matter is known, one further step remains: opening a 'Door' to Nature's treasures. This chunk does not explain the Door.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 sequence: First Matter known = Sanctuary found; only the Door remains.
- **operator** — “Have therefore a charitable seraphic mind, charitable and not destructive to thyself.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (stated):** The operator needs a charitable disposition: charitable works produce hope of heaven, hope produces faith; works of darkness yield no hope and only the devils' faith 'to believe and tremble'.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 gives the chain charity -> hope -> faith explicitly.
  - Also: “as God is the Father, so Charity is the nurse of Faith” `III.4 ¶44`
- **operator** — “Have thy heart in heaven and thy hands upon earth. Ascend in piety and descend in charity.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (stated):** The operator keeps his mind directed upward while his practical work stays on earth, rising by piety and turning outward in charity; this is called 'the Nature of Light'.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44: 'For this is the Nature of Light and the way of the children of it.'
  - Also: “Settle not in the lees and puddles of the world.” `III.4 ¶44`
- **operator** — “You must live, as says Agrippa, according to God and the angels, rejecting all things that are dissimilar to the heaven; otherwise thou canst have no communion with superiors.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan, citing Agrippa)
  - **Plain (stated):** Communion with higher powers requires a way of life made like heaven, rejecting what is unlike it.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 states the condition.
- **operator** — “Unus esto non solus. Avoid the multitude, as well of passions as of persons.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The operator should be inwardly unified, not merely alone, withdrawing from crowds both of people and of passions.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44: the following English sentence is read as glossing the Latin (not marked as a gloss); 'inwardly unified' rests partly on our translation.
- **principle** — “every day is a contracted year, and that each year is an extended day. Anticipate the year in the day, and lose not a day in the year.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** A day and a year are treated as the same cycle, contracted and extended; how this applies to the work is not explained in this chunk.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 only; see ambiguities.
- **agent** — “Make use of indeterminate agents till thou canst find a determinate one: the many wish well, but one only loves.” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Work with general, non-specific agents until the one specific agent is found; the chunk identifies neither.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 only; contrast 'the many' / 'one only'. See ambiguities.
- **principle** — “Circumferences spread, but centers contract; so superiors dissolve and inferiors coagulate” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (stated):** Higher things dissolve (expand, as a circumference spreads), lower things coagulate (condense, as a center contracts).
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44: the 'so' states the analogy directly.
- **sign** — “stand not long in the sun nor long in the shade, where extremes meet, there look for complexions” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Avoid staying in either extreme; the 'complexions' sought appear where extremes meet. This chunk does not say what the complexions are.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 only; fits the earlier warning against heat exceeding 'the natural proportion'.
- **operator** — “There is nothing stronger than Perseverance, for it ends in miracles” `III.4 ¶44` (QUOTED: Vaughan; endorsed by Atwood)
  - **Plain (stated):** Persistence, learning from errors and misfortunes, is essential; after benevolence and right intention it is the requirement most insisted on.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶44 (Vaughan) and III.4 ¶45 (Atwood) state it.
  - Also: “Nothing is more generally insisted on, next to benevolence and rectitude of intention, than perseverance for this experiment” `III.4 ¶45`
  - Also: “Learn from thy errors to be infallible, and from they misfortunes to be constant.” `III.4 ¶44`
- **principle** — “effects rationally investigated lead into their causes” `III.4 ¶45` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Reasoned study of effects leads back to their causes.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶45 direct statement.
- **principle** — “in this philosophy the end is implied in the beginning, and the purpose is by the product made manifest” `III.4 ¶45` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** As a seed grows according to its species in congenial soil, the outcome of the work is contained in its start; the product reveals the purpose, proceeding from motive through action and a metaphysical cause into a physical effect.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶45 gives the seed analogy and the chain motive -> action -> metaphysical cause -> physical effect.
  - Also: “the motive, through the resulting action, by the metaphysical cause, into physical effect” `III.4 ¶45`
- **procedure** — “The first link in the chain of vital causes moves, as we apply the Master Key.” `III.4 ¶47` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Applying the 'Master Key' sets the chain of vital causes in motion; this chunk does not say what the Master Key is.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶47 only.
- **procedure** — “Portus Explicit, at Praxis manaulis caetera pandet.” `III.4 ¶48` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The introductory part ends; the remainder is to be learned by hands-on practice.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶48 (our translation) with ¶45 'without practical information' and ¶47 'we conclude our introduction'.

## Terms

- **spiritual guide** — The one with whose constitution and instinct the conditions of the work vary; not further defined here. (ATWOOD)
  - “which vary with the constitution and instinct of the spiritual guide” `III.4 ¶42`
- **entranced Subject** — The person in trance, susceptible to imaginative impressions that linger in the place. (ATWOOD)
  - “the entranced Subject is more or less susceptible of all imaginative impressures” `III.4 ¶42`
- **communion** — The connection the work requires: disturbed by air currents; communion 'with superiors' needs a heavenly way of life. (ATWOOD)
  - “Strong currents of air are well known to disturb communion” `III.4 ¶42`
  - “otherwise thou canst have no communion with superiors” `III.4 ¶44`
- **imaginative impressures** — Impressions of the imagination which persist in a place after their act and pollute its ether. (ATWOOD)
  - “even after their act has passed away, hang and pollute the ether of their pertinacious abode” `III.4 ¶42`
- **ether** — Something belonging to a place, which lingering imaginative impressions can pollute. (ATWOOD)
  - “pollute the ether of their pertinacious abode” `III.4 ¶42`
- **Philosophic Work** — The 'ultimate' work to which 'the practice' refers. (UNCLEAR)
  - = the practice: “the practice (that is of the ultimate Philosophic Work)” `III.4 ¶44`
- **heat** — What the operator applies ('thy heat'), which must not exceed 'the natural proportion'; its literal nature is not stated (see ambiguities). (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “if thy heat exceeds the natural proportion” `III.4 ¶44`
- **moist natures** — Natures that, provoked by excess heat, rise against the central fire. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “thou hast stirred up the wrath of the moist natures, and they will stand up against the central fire” `III.4 ¶44`
- **central fire** — The fire which the moist natures oppose, and which opposes them, when heat is excessive. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “they will stand up against the central fire, and the central fire against them” `III.4 ¶44`
- **Chaos** — That in which division occurs when moist natures and central fire are set against each other. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “there will be a terrible division in the Chaos” `III.4 ¶44`
- **Quintessence** — The eternal spirit of Peace in the elements, which departs from them under violent handling. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - = spirit of Peace: “the sweet spirit of Peace, the true eternal Quintessence” `III.4 ¶44`
  - “will depart from the elements, leaving both of them and thee to confusion” `III.4 ¶44`
- **First Matter** — The 'principal' matter whose knowledge amounts to discovering the Sanctuary of Nature. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “if thou knowest the principal First Matter, know also for certain thou hast discovered the Sanctuary of Nature” `III.4 ¶44`
- **Sanctuary of Nature** — Where Nature's treasures lie, behind a Door that must be opened; discovered by knowing the First Matter. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “There is nothing between thee and her treasures but the Door: that indeed must be opened.” `III.4 ¶44`
- **Charity** (also: charitable) — The nurse of Faith: charitable works yield hope of heaven, and hope leads to belief. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “as God is the Father, so Charity is the nurse of Faith” `III.4 ¶44`
- **superiors / inferiors** (also: superiors, inferiors) — Higher and lower orders: superiors dissolve, inferiors coagulate; communion with superiors needs a heavenly life. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “so superiors dissolve and inferiors coagulate” `III.4 ¶44`
  - “no communion with superiors” `III.4 ¶44`
- **indeterminate agents / determinate one** — Agents to be used provisionally until a single determinate agent is found; not further defined. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “Make use of indeterminate agents till thou canst find a determinate one” `III.4 ¶44`
- **complexions** — What is to be sought where extremes meet; not further defined. (QUOTED: Vaughan)
  - “where extremes meet, there look for complexions” `III.4 ¶44`
- **experiment** — Atwood's word for the work, for which perseverance is insisted on. (ATWOOD)
  - “than perseverance for this experiment” `III.4 ¶45`
- **Sphinx’s lair** — Figure for that to which Atwood's preceding exposition has been an introduction; not explained. (ATWOOD)
  - “we conclude our introduction, as it may be called, to the Sphinx’s lair” `III.4 ¶47`
- **Master Key** — That whose application moves the first link in the chain of vital causes; not explained here. (ATWOOD)
  - “The first link in the chain of vital causes moves, as we apply the Master Key.” `III.4 ¶47`

## Ambiguities

- “the constitution and instinct of the spiritual guide” `III.4 ¶42`
  - Reading: A human director of the work, paired with the entranced Subject, whose own constitution sets the conditions.
  - Reading: A guiding spirit or spiritual influence active in the work.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶42 gives no further description; the same paragraph's 'entranced Subject' suggests a guide/subject pair, while 'communion' and 'spiritual' fit either.
- “if thy heat exceeds the natural proportion” `III.4 ¶44`
  - Reading: Literal heat applied in an operation.
  - Reading: The operator's own vehemence or violence of handling.
  - Basis: Fire/moist natures language supports the first; 'thy violent destructive Hands', 'a few violent knocks' and 'charitable and not destructive to thyself' (III.4 ¶44) support the second. The text does not gloss 'heat'.
- “every day is a contracted year, and that each year is an extended day” `III.4 ¶44`
  - Reading: A rule of diligence: use each day fully ('lose not a day in the year').
  - Reading: A correspondence in the work's process: a short cycle reproduces the long one in small ('Anticipate the year in the day').
  - Basis: Both rest on the following sentence in III.4 ¶44; 'contracted'/'extended' also parallels 'centers contract'/'Circumferences spread' in the same paragraph, favouring the second.
- “Make use of indeterminate agents till thou canst find a determinate one: the many wish well, but one only loves.” `III.4 ¶44`
  - Reading: Agents in the operation: general means until the one specific agent is found.
  - Reading: Persons: many well-wishers versus one who truly loves, i.e. a single devoted helper or guide.
  - Basis: 'agents' suggests the operation; 'the many wish well, but one only loves' and the preceding 'Avoid the multitude, as well of passions as of persons' suggest persons.
- “the practice (that is of the ultimate Philosophic Work)” `III.4 ¶44`
  - Reading: Atwood's interpolated gloss within the quotation.
  - Reading: Part of Vaughan's own text.
  - Basis: The parenthesis precedes 'says Vaughan'; the text does not mark whose words it is.
- “Portus Explicit, at Praxis manaulis caetera pandet.” `III.4 ¶48`
  - Reading: 'Explicit' as the conventional closing word: 'Here ends the harbour/approach'.
  - Reading: 'Explicit' as 'unfolds': 'The harbour (approach) is unfolded'.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶47 says the introduction is concluded; ¶45 says more words are of little use 'without practical information'. Both readings rest on our translation.

## Translations

- `III.4 ¶44` “Unus esto non solus.” → Be one, not alone. *(ours)*
- `III.4 ¶46` “Qui capit, Ille sapit.” → He who grasps it, he is wise. *(ours)*
- `III.4 ¶48` “Portus Explicit, at Praxis manaulis caetera pandet.” → Here ends the approach (harbour); but manual practice will lay open the rest. *(ours)*

## Threads

- `III.4 ¶42` 'has been mentioned as desirable' points back to an earlier mention of a locality like Virgil's for his Bees.
- `III.4 ¶45` 'the foregoing instructions' refers back to the instructions given before this point (not specified here).
- `III.4 ¶47` Atwood calls everything so far an 'introduction ... to the Sphinx’s lair' and says the first vital cause moves 'as we apply the Master Key', pointing forward to the practice.
- `III.4 ¶48` Latin close (our translation): manual practice will lay open 'the rest'.
