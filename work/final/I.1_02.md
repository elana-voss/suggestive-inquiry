# I.1_02

*Atwood continues the public history of the Hermetic art: from Egypt under the Ptolemies through the Greek philosophers (Democritus, Maria, Anaxagoras, Aristotle, Plato), the Egyptian book-burning and Cleopatra, to Rome and its perpetual lamps. She ends with the 'divine water' whose ether burns perpetually and with the Bononian Enigma, which the adepts say refers to the hidden material of the art.*

## Argument

- Back in Egypt under the Ptolemies, her Art and Wisdom spread abroad. Foreigners were initiated into the mysteries of Isis, and India, Arabia, China and Persia rivalled her in magian skill. `I.1 ¶12` — ATWOOD
- Pliny: Ostanes, a Persian with Xerxes' army, first 'inoculated Greece'. Greek philosophers then sought the eastern temples, above all Memphis. `I.1 ¶12, I.1 n13` — QUOTED: Pliny (Atwood endorses)
- After Thales and Pythagoras (writings lost) comes Democritus, 'father of experimental philosophy'. His Sacred Physics treats the Hermetic art and the occult discovery under all ancient philosophy. Synesius commented on it and Flamel excerpted it; only the ignorant doubt it. `I.1 ¶12, I.1 n14, I.1 n15, I.1 n16` — ATWOOD
- Pliny attests Democritus' occult practice, magic flourishing with him as medicine did with Hippocrates. Seneca records his artificial precious stones. `I.1 ¶12, I.1 n17, I.1 n18, I.1 n19` — ATWOOD
- At Memphis he studied with Maria, a Hebrew woman skilled in the Hermetic Art; her treatises survive. `I.1 ¶13, I.1 n20` — ATWOOD
- Anaxagoras is next. Norton's verse says the masters all wrote 'under coverts', so practitioners lose their costs. Anaxagoras wrote 'plainest of them all' ('Conversions Naturall'), and Aristotle envied and rebuked him. `I.1 ¶14, I.1 ¶15, I.1 n21` — QUOTED: Norton (Atwood endorses his praise of Anaxagoras)
- The adepts accuse Aristotle of veiling his knowledge and misleading people from true experiment. Atwood doubts deliberate deceit. He faulted only his predecessors' literal language, never their true meaning, and his metaphysics matches Anaxagoras, Plato and Heraclitus. Works on the stone are attributed to him. `I.1 ¶16, I.1 n22` — ATWOOD
- Plato's philosophy is hyperphysical and not understood. His dread of writing the truth down suggests a practical science drew Dionysius to him. Wisdom must be sought for itself, not for gold. `I.1 ¶17, I.1 n23` — ATWOOD
- Records perished at Alexandria, so Atwood will not call Aristotle or Plato Hermetic philosophers. She names them because she will argue that the theosophic schools rested on similar experimental ground. `I.1 ¶17` — ATWOOD
- Suidas: about 284 AD the Emperor burned all chemical books, envious that the Egyptians made gold and silver to fund troops. The art kept reviving; Cleopatra dissolved her earring in philosophers' vinegar. `I.1 ¶18, I.1 n24, I.1 n25` — ATWOOD
- Rome achieved little, but its perpetual lamps show knowledge of 'the occult laws of light'. One near Alestes, made by Maximus Olybius, probably burned upwards of 1500 years on a pure liquor held in gold and silver vessels. His inscription speaks of elements 'digested' by laborious art. `I.1 ¶18, I.1 ¶19, I.1 n26, I.1 n27` — ATWOOD
- Hermolaus Barbarus: a divine water, also called Scythian latex, is a spirit of the nature of the ether, and potable gold and the stone begin from it. Khunrath cites an ancient author on this water; one of them ('he') affirms its ether burns perpetually if outside air is restrained. `I.1 ¶20, I.1 n28` — QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus; H. Khunrath
- The adepts claim the unsolved Bononian Enigma refers to their occult material. Its subject is neither man, woman nor androgyne 'but all', lies 'everywhere', and is both tomb and corpse. `I.1 ¶20, I.1 ¶21, I.1 n29` — QUOTED: the adepts (reported by Atwood)

## Method

- **subject** — “the occult material of their art” `I.1 ¶20` (QUOTED: the adepts (reported by Atwood))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The hidden material that the art works on. The adepts say the Bononian Enigma refers to it exclusively; the Enigma describes it only by negations and totality (neither man, woman nor androgyne, 'but all'; in neither heaven, earth nor waters, but 'everywhere'; tomb and corpse at once). This chunk gives no plainer identification.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶20 says the adepts claim the Enigma refers to 'the occult material of their art'; the reading of its features rests on the epitaph text in I.1 ¶21. Atwood reports the claim and introduces the Enigma as something that 'should not be omitted here'.
  - Also: “Nec vir, nec mulier, nec androgyna,” `I.1 ¶21`
  - Also: “Nec coola, nec terris, nec aquis, Sed ubique jacet!” `I.1 ¶21`
- **subject** — “There is a celestial, or rather a divine water of the chemists, with which both Democritus and Trismegistus were acquainted” `I.1 ¶20` (QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus)
  - **Plain (stated):** A special 'water', different from any ordinary water or liquid, which is a spirit with the nature of the ether. Potable gold and the philosophers' stone begin from it.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶20 glosses it directly: 'which is a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things; whence potable gold, and the stone of philosophers, takes its beginning', and 'distinct from every other water or liquor'.
  - Also: “calling it divine water, Scythian latex, &c., which is a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things” `I.1 ¶20`
  - Also: “a particular kind that is distinct from every other water or liquor” `I.1 ¶20`
- **principle** — “the ether in this praeter-perfect aqueous body will burn perpetually, without diminution or consumption of itself, if the external air only be restrained” `I.1 ¶20` (UNCLEAR: H. Khunrath or the ancient author he cites ('he devoutly affirms'))
  - **Plain (stated):** The ether held in this water burns forever without being used up, provided outside air is kept from it.
  - Basis: Explicit in I.1 ¶20; the text does not make clear whether 'he' is Khunrath or the ancient author.
- **result** — “a very pure liquor, by whose virtue it is probable these lamps had continued to shine for upwards of fifteen hundred years” `I.1 ¶18` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The perpetual lamps were probably kept burning by a pure liquid held in gold and silver vessels. Atwood credits their maker with skill in 'adjusting the gaseous elements, or other ethereal adaptations', and takes the lamps as proof of knowledge of 'the occult laws of light'.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶18 states it directly. Whether this liquor is the divine water of I.1 ¶20 is not said; see ambiguities.
  - Also: “some superior skill in adjusting the gaseous elements, or other ethereal adaptations than is known at this day” `I.1 ¶18`
  - Also: “the Romans understood something of chemistry and the occult laws of light” `I.1 ¶18`
- **procedure** — “Namque elementa gravi clausit digesta labore” `I.1 ¶19` (QUOTED: inscription of Maximus Olybius)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The maker 'digested' the elements by laborious work and sealed them in a small vessel. The book's translation reads: 'The elements has chained. Digested With laborious art, From secret science gained.' This chunk does not explain what the digestion consists of.
  - Basis: Based on I.1 ¶19, the inscription and the book's own translation, which concern the lamp-maker's work.
  - Also: “Olybius, in this slender vase, The elements has chained.” `I.1 ¶19`
- **agent** — “such a sharp vinegar as is only known to philosophers on the ground of nature” `I.1 ¶18` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** A dissolving agent, not ordinary vinegar, that only the philosophers know. Cleopatra used it to dissolve her earring. This chunk does not explain it further.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶18: the phrase 'only known to philosophers' marks it as no common vinegar; its use is dissolving ('dissolving her earring').
- **result** — “the facility with which the Egyptians were able to make gold and silver” `I.1 ¶18` (QUOTED: Suidas)
  - **Plain (stated):** Results ascribed to the art: making gold and silver (the reason the Emperor burned the chemical books), artificial precious stones (Democritus), and potable gold and the philosophers' stone (from the divine water).
  - Basis: I.1 ¶18 (Suidas), I.1 ¶12 (Seneca on Democritus), I.1 ¶20 (Hermolaus Barbarus).
  - Also: “Seneca also mentions his artificial confection of precious stones” `I.1 ¶12`
  - Also: “whence potable gold, and the stone of philosophers, takes its beginning” `I.1 ¶20`
- **principle** — “that occult discovery on which the systems of ancient philosophy appear to have been very uniformly based” `I.1 ¶12` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** One hidden discovery appears to underlie the systems of ancient philosophy. In I.1 ¶17 Atwood hopes to show the theosophic schools rested on 'a similar experimental ground'. This chunk does not say what the discovery is.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶12 states the discovery (hedged: 'appear to have been'); linking it to the 'experimental ground' of I.1 ¶17 is an inference from the shared argument.
  - Also: “the most famous schools of theosophy have in all ages been based on a similar experimental ground and profound science of truth in their leaders” `I.1 ¶17`
- **impediment** — “Wrote under coverts and Aristotle alsoe.” `I.1 ¶15` (QUOTED: Norton)
  - **Plain (stated):** The masters deliberately hid the art in poetry, parables and metaphors. Readers who try to practise from the books waste their money and despair.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶15 says so explicitly: 'They hid this art that noe man find it can'; 'They leefe their costs'; 'much people are brought to despaire'.
  - Also: “In poysies, parables, and in metaphors alsoe,” `I.1 ¶15`
  - Also: “They leefe their costs, as men see alle daye.” `I.1 ¶15`
  - Also: “They hid this art that noe man find it can.” `I.1 ¶15`
- **principle** — “apparently contradictory, as respects their language when taken in a literal sense; for he never quarrels with their true meaning” `I.1 ¶16` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The philosophers' writings contradict each other only when read literally. Their true meaning agrees, and a reader must look past the literal wording.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶16 contrasts the 'literal sense' with the 'true meaning' and says Aristotle's metaphysics differs in nothing essential from that of Anaxagoras, Plato and Heraclitus. Together with the 'coverts' of I.1 ¶15, this supports the reading.
- **operator** — “Wisdom must be sought for her own sake, neither for gold or silver or any intermediate benefit, lest these all should be denied together without the discovery of their source.” `I.1 ¶17` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The seeker must pursue wisdom for its own sake, not for gold or silver. Seeking the profits misses both the profits and their source.
  - Basis: Atwood states it directly in I.1 ¶17.
- **operator** — “it is a parturition respecting this ingenerated in the soul, from which he who is not liberated will never in reality acquire truth” `I.1 ¶17` (QUOTED: Plato (Epistle II))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Inquiry into 'the cause of all beautiful things' involves something brought to birth in the soul. Whoever is not 'liberated' from it never really attains truth.
  - Basis: Based on the quoted Epistle in I.1 ¶17 and n23. Atwood uses it with her claim that Plato's science was practical; how she relates the two is not explained (see ambiguities).
- **principle** — “why such dread as is there expressed about setting the truth to paper?” `I.1 ¶17` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Plato's dread of writing the truth down suggests his science was more probably practical than moral or abstract philosophy only.
  - Basis: Atwood draws this inference herself in I.1 ¶17: 'the science which drew the tyrant to the philosopher was more probably practical and profitably interesting'.
  - Also: “the science which drew the tyrant to the philosopher was more probably practical and profitably interesting than abstracts would appear to be to such a mind” `I.1 ¶17`
- **impediment** — “led mankind astray from the path of true experiment” `I.1 ¶16` (QUOTED: Adepts (qualified by Atwood))
  - **Plain (stated):** The adepts charge that Aristotle hid his own knowledge and steered people away from real experiment. Atwood doubts he did so deliberately, while granting that 'the barrenness of his philosophy may be deplored'.
  - Basis: I.1 ¶16 gives the adepts' complaint and then Atwood's qualification.
  - Also: “veiled the knowledge which he secretly possessed” `I.1 ¶16`

## Terms

- **Hermetic art** (also: Hermetic Art, Art) — The art that Democritus' Sacred Physics treats, in which Maria was advanced. It is linked with 'that occult discovery' underlying ancient philosophy. Its practitioners are 'Hermetic philosophers'. (ATWOOD)
  - “treats especially of the Hermetic art, and that occult discovery on which the systems of ancient philosophy appear to have been very uniformly based” `I.1 ¶12`
  - “particularly in the department of the Hermetic Art” `I.1 ¶13`
- **Hermetic philosophers** — A title Atwood will not give Aristotle or Plato without more evidence that they practised the art. (ATWOOD)
  - “to prove their claim to the title of Hermetic philosophers” `I.1 ¶17`
- **Adepts** (also: adepts) — Practitioners of the art. They blame Aristotle and claim the Bononian Enigma as referring to their occult material. (ATWOOD)
  - “Aristotle is much blamed by Adepts in general” `I.1 ¶16`
  - “the adepts claim it as having exclusive reference to the occult material of their art” `I.1 ¶20`
- **experimental** (also: experiment) — Applied to Democritus' fame (alongside 'skill in the occult sciences and practice of them'), to the 'path of true experiment' Aristotle is said to have obscured, and to the ground of the theosophic schools. In I.1 ¶17 the practical science is contrasted with abstracts. (ATWOOD)
  - “frequently styles the father of experimental philosophy” `I.1 ¶12`
  - “Pliny bears witness to the experimental fame of Democritus, and his skill in the occult sciences and practice of them” `I.1 ¶12`
  - “led mankind astray from the path of true experiment” `I.1 ¶16`
  - “based on a similar experimental ground” `I.1 ¶17`
- **hyperphysical** — Applied to Democritus' occult researches, such as making precious stones, and to Plato's whole philosophy with its mystic, recondite character. The chunk gives no definition. (ATWOOD)
  - “these and such-like hyperphysical researches” `I.1 ¶12`
  - “But the whole philosophy of Plato is hyperphysical” `I.1 ¶17`
- **occult discovery** — The hidden discovery on which the systems of ancient philosophy were uniformly based. This chunk does not say what it is. (ATWOOD)
  - “that occult discovery on which the systems of ancient philosophy appear to have been very uniformly based” `I.1 ¶12`
- **occult material** — The hidden material of the art. The adepts claim the Bononian Enigma refers to it exclusively. (QUOTED: the adepts)
  - “the occult material of their art” `I.1 ¶20`
- **divine water** (also: celestial, or rather a divine water of the chemists) — A water distinct from every other water or liquor, known to Democritus and Trismegistus. It is a spirit of the nature of the ether, and potable gold and the philosophers' stone begin from it. The ether in it burns perpetually if outside air is excluded. (QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus)
  - = Scythian latex: “calling it divine water, Scythian latex, &c.” `I.1 ¶20`
  - = a spirit of the nature of the ether: “which is a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things” `I.1 ¶20`
  - = praeter-perfect aqueous body: “concerning this water; and he devoutly affirms, that the ether in this praeter-perfect aqueous body will burn perpetually” `I.1 ¶20`
  - “There is a celestial, or rather a divine water of the chemists” `I.1 ¶20`
- **ether** — Contained in the divine water ('praeter-perfect aqueous body'), it burns perpetually without consuming itself if outside air is kept out. The divine water is 'of the nature of the ether'. (UNCLEAR: H. Khunrath or the ancient author he cites)
  - “the ether in this praeter-perfect aqueous body will burn perpetually, without diminution or consumption of itself” `I.1 ¶20`
- **quintessence of things** — Named in the gloss on the divine water. The grammar leaves it unclear whether the water is the quintessence or is of its nature (see ambiguities). (QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus)
  - “a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things” `I.1 ¶20`
- **potable gold** — A product that, like the philosophers' stone, begins from the divine water. (QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus)
  - “whence potable gold, and the stone of philosophers, takes its beginning” `I.1 ¶20`
- **Philosophers' stone** (also: Philosophers’ stone, philosophers’ stone, stone of philosophers) — Subject of treatises attributed to Aristotle and Plato. It begins from the divine water. (ATWOOD)
  - “Certain epistles to Alexander the Great on the Philosophers’ stone, attributed to Aristotle” `I.1 ¶16`
  - “whence potable gold, and the stone of philosophers, takes its beginning” `I.1 ¶20`
- **sharp vinegar** — A solvent known only to philosophers. Cleopatra dissolved her earring in it. (ATWOOD)
  - “such a sharp vinegar as is only known to philosophers on the ground of nature” `I.1 ¶18`
- **perpetual lamps** — Ancient lamps found still burning in tombs and urns, kept alight by a pure liquor. Atwood treats them as proof of Roman knowledge of chemistry and 'the occult laws of light'. (ATWOOD)
  - “the perpetual lamps best prove, and without offence, that the Romans understood something of chemistry and the occult laws of light” `I.1 ¶18`
- **chemistry** (also: chemical, chemists) — Used of the art itself: the 'chemical' books burned for teaching how to make gold and silver, the 'chemists'' divine water, and Roman knowledge shown by the lamps. (ATWOOD)
  - “every chemical book was to be seized and burned together in the public market-place” `I.1 ¶18`
  - “a divine water of the chemists” `I.1 ¶20`
- **theosophy** — Its most famous schools rested on a similar experimental ground; Atwood hopes to show this. (ATWOOD)
  - “the most famous schools of theosophy have in all ages been based on a similar experimental ground” `I.1 ¶17`

## Ambiguities

- “the adepts claim it as having exclusive reference to the occult material of their art” `I.1 ¶20`
  - Reading: Atwood presents the Enigma as a genuine description of the art's subject.
  - Reading: Atwood only reports the adepts' claim without endorsing it.
  - Basis: Reading 1: she says it 'should not be omitted here' and places it right after the divine water. Reading 2: it is phrased as 'the adepts claim', and she calls it 'long famous, without a solution' and says it puzzled antiquaries.
- “which is a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things” `I.1 ¶20`
  - Reading: The water is a spirit having the nature of both the ether and the quintessence of things.
  - Reading: The water is a spirit of the nature of the ether, and is itself the quintessence of things.
  - Basis: The grammar of the single clause in I.1 ¶20 allows either reading.
- “each of which was full of a very pure liquor, by whose virtue it is probable these lamps had continued to shine” `I.1 ¶18`
  - Reading: The lamps' liquor is the divine water, whose ether burns perpetually if outside air is restrained.
  - Reading: The lamps are only a parallel; Atwood ascribes them to skill in 'adjusting the gaseous elements, or other ethereal adaptations' without naming the liquor.
  - Basis: Reading 1: I.1 ¶20 puts the perpetually burning ether in the water directly beside further accounts of burning lamps ('There are also ... lamps found burning'), and the lamp was sealed in nested urns. Reading 2: no identity is stated anywhere.
- “it is a parturition respecting this ingenerated in the soul, from which he who is not liberated will never in reality acquire truth” `I.1 ¶17`
  - Reading: The inquiry is an inward process born in the soul, which must be completed before truth is gained.
  - Reading: Atwood cites it as a sign of the practical, secret science that drew Dionysius to Plato.
  - Basis: Reading 1: the soul language of the quote itself. Reading 2: the preceding sentence ('more probably practical and profitably interesting'). Atwood does not say how the two relate.
