# III.4_02

*Continuing her account of the mental requisites and impediments of the Hermetic artist, Atwood condemns covetousness and irrational skepticism (with Norton's verse in support), and sets against them rational skepticism or discrimination, moral qualifications, leisure, stable theory and patient study. She argues that the Art stays concealed because the inner sense of its two-edged records is held apart from the literal, and the unprepared cannot receive it.*

## Argument

- None attains the fruits without rectitude of intention and God's blessing on well-tried experience. The pursuit endangers the ill-informed, but success has degrees and every step under the Rule of Reason is progressive. `III.4 ¶11` — ATWOOD
- Avarice, ambition or curiosity is not the true Form of Gold; Reason alone enters it. Mammon heaps dead metal but cannot quicken the aurific seed; too gross for the 'ethereal profundity', it flees Wisdom's fiery ordeal. `III.4 ¶11` — ATWOOD
- Geber condemns Skeptics next above the Covetous; Atwood says they would merit less reproval but for hindering others. Infidelity is fashionable faithlessness that irrationally denounces the new. Atwood would as soon live in Galileo's days as see 'the recovered secret of ages' sink into obloquy. `III.4 ¶12` — ATWOOD
- Skeptics ape sound reason, 'stolid bolts before the gate of Truth'. Yet Nature opens a door to the multitude: Mesmerism, the neglected Door-keeper that first introduced Atwood to antique science. `III.4 ¶13` — ATWOOD
- Nature opens 'this casket' not as she gives common gifts: she must be moved subtly, conscientiously, courteously, and yields only to a philosopher disciplined in her schools and proven. `III.4 ¶13` — ATWOOD
- Verse: God withholds the science from great doctors and grants it to few faithful, lowly men. Failed searchers call it fable, yet it is true. As a blind man cannot paint, proud clerks cannot reach 'the secretest pointe of kinde'. `III.4 ¶14, III.4 n9` — QUOTED: Norton (endorsed)
- Rational skepticism, better called discrimination, proves all and holds the true; the Hermetic Student needs it, as it establishes truth's supremacy, essential to practical research. Who never held the true ideal will not recognize the image when shown. `III.4 ¶15` — ATWOOD
- The searcher should be, like Nature, faithful, simple, patient, constant, hopeful, benevolent; the hermetic Master adds virtue, sound reason and guarding the secrets. With leisure, progress 'by a living experience into the Light' may be hoped; a busy head, faithless heart or impure hands fail. `III.4 ¶15, III.4 n10` — ATWOOD
- Bodily impediments matter little. Reading the doctrine needs the same patient hope and free thought and imagination as the experimental proof. The many alchemical Keys and Guides direct to one Royal Road once found, but are useless to flaccid modern readers. `III.4 ¶15` — ATWOOD
- The enigmas are too abstruse for the natural understanding. The records are two-edged; their inner sense is held aloof from the literal, and plain truth is disbelieved, as Sendivogius' friends doubted 'there was any water in our sea'. `III.4 ¶16` — ATWOOD
- Allegory: men in darkness fled the narrator's candle, keeping a false light (fox-fire, glow-worms) by which they read Geber and Rhasis; his light stood separate from the uncomprehending darkness. `III.4 ¶16, III.4 n11` — QUOTED: Eirenaeus
- In 'outward application', Alchemy's light is not for the gross mass. Incredulity guards the Art lest folly rushing into practice perish or break divine legislation; it will stay concealed. Without stable theory and desire of truth, all is vanity. `III.4 ¶16` — ATWOOD
- Verse: such 'butterflies' should cease. Per the 'holi letter', proceed mightily to the end, disposing all with suavity of grace. Only the stable-witted finish; the mutable and credulous fail. `III.4 ¶16, III.4 ¶17, III.4 n12` — QUOTED: Norton (endorsed)

## Method

- **operator** — “rectitude of intention and the blessing of God on a well tried experience” `III.4 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Three conditions for attaining the fruits of the philosophy: upright intention, God's blessing, and tested experience.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶11: 'None ever truly attained to the fruits of this philosophy, as the wise declare, without' these.
- **principle** — “there are many degrees of success in the legitimate path, and every step is progressive where the Rule of Reason is pursued” `III.4 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Success is graded, not all-or-nothing; each step taken under the guidance of reason is real progress.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶11.
  - Also: “all may be hoped for progressively passing by a living experience into the Light” `III.4 ¶15`
- **result** — “neither will be found to be the true Form of Gold; Reason alone can enter into it” `III.4 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The real 'gold' of the art is not material metal and is not reached by greed, ambition or curiosity; only reason gives access to it.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶11 sets avarice, ambition and curious hope against Reason as the only entry, and contrasts the Form of Gold with 'the dead metal in heaps' Mammon gathers. Not further defined here.
- **procedure** — “Mammon may draw the dead metal in heaps about its sordid circumference; but it cannot quicken the aurific seed in life” `III.4 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The work brings to life a living, gold-producing seed, as opposed to inert metal. This needs a spirit subtle enough to permeate the 'ethereal profundity', which the covetous spirit is not. This chunk does not say what the seed is.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶11 sets 'dead metal' against 'aurific seed in life' and says 'that Spirit is too gross to permeate the ethereal profundity'.
  - Also: “that Spirit is too gross to permeate the ethereal profundity” `III.4 ¶11`
- **stage** — “Wisdom’s fiery ordeal” `III.4 ¶11` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** A fiery trial belonging to Wisdom that the covetous cannot endure or enter. This chunk does not explain the ordeal or the 'vaporous essence'; see ambiguities.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶11: the covetous spirit 'is the first to fly from Wisdom’s fiery ordeal, not able to enter with his camel form'.
  - Also: “daring to prove vaporous essence in Her pure Light” `III.4 ¶11`
- **impediment** — “by infidelity, we mean that fashionable kind of faithlessness, which, without rational foundation, denounces everything that is new” `III.4 ¶12` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Irrational skepticism, which rejects anything new or unfamiliar without rational ground and believes or venerates nothing, blocks progress for oneself and others. It only imitates the sound reason it lacks.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶12 gives the definition; III.4 ¶13 adds that skeptics 'ape' sound reason.
  - Also: “It is the wisdom of modern skeptics to ape the thing which they stand most in need of, viz., sound reason” `III.4 ¶13`
  - Also: “they push forward as so many stolid bolts before the gate of Truth” `III.4 ¶13`
- **principle** — “the neglected Door-keeper that gave us first introduction to the vestibule of antique science” `III.4 ¶13` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Mesmerism is the door Nature opens to the multitude, relieving suffering and offering a new guide to knowledge; it first led Atwood to the entrance of the ancient science. This chunk does not say what part it plays in the work itself; see ambiguities.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶13: 'We allude to Mesmerism'.
  - Also: “offer a new guide to knowledge and felicity” `III.4 ¶13`
- **procedure** — “opens not this casket after the same rule; she must be moved to it subtly, conscientiously, courteously” `III.4 ¶13` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Nature does not give up this secret freely as she does her common gifts; she must be approached with subtlety, conscientiousness and courtesy.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶13 contrasts Nature's freely lavished 'common gifts' with 'this casket'; the casket's contents are not named, but the context is the secret of the art.
- **operator** — “she will surrender to none but a philosopher, one too that has been disciplined in her schools, tried and proven to ensure his ability to bear the sacred trust” `III.4 ¶13` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Only a person trained in Nature's ways and tested as able to keep the trust receives the secret. Norton's verse adds that God grants it to very few, the faithful, true and humble.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶13; III.4 ¶14.
  - Also: “And granted it to few men of is mercy, Such as be faithful, trew and lowly,” `III.4 ¶14`
  - Also: “Soe among millions of millions of mankind Scarcely seven men maie this science finde.” `III.4 ¶14`
- **impediment** — “Such men presume too much upon their minde, They weene theire wits sufficient this arte to finde;” `III.4 ¶14` (QUOTED: Norton (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Learned men who trust their own cleverness fail, then declare the art a fable.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶14: failed searchers 'say how there is noe such arte'.
  - Also: “From great doctors hath this science forbod;” `III.4 ¶14`
  - Also: “For such be not invited to our feast, Which weeneth themselves wise, and doe leaste.” `III.4 ¶14`
- **principle** — “How should a born blinde man be sure To write or make good portraiture?” `III.4 ¶14` (QUOTED: Norton (endorsed))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Those who have never seen the thing cannot judge or represent it; only a 'lawful witness' can speak of the art.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶14 sets this image directly after 'How he which hereof lawful witness be' and against proud clerks who deny the art.
  - Also: “A man that never had sight to peinte.” `III.4 ¶14`
- **subject** — “To fetch out the secretest pointe of kinde;” `III.4 ¶14` (QUOTED: Norton (endorsed))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The aim is to bring out the most hidden point of 'kinde' (rendered as nature in our translation only). This chunk does not explain what that point is.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶14 only; the rendering 'nature' rests on an 'ours' translation.
- **operator** — “Such skepticism, more properly perhaps called discrimination, is as much required by the Hermetic Student as the other is obnoxious.” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The student needs critical discrimination: testing everything, rejecting the false, holding the true. It strengthens the mind and makes truth supreme in the understanding, which practical success requires. Irrational skepticism is only its 'mock ally'.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15.
  - Also: “It is the province of reason to inquire and endeavor, by perscrutination, to prove all things” `III.4 ¶15`
  - Also: “cultivate that distinctive supremacy of truth in the understanding spirit which is so essential to success in the practical research” `III.4 ¶15`
- **operator** — “they who have never entertained the true ideal should fail to recognize the image when represented before their eyes” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The operator must already hold a correct idea of what is sought, or he will not recognize it when it actually appears to him.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15 (the sentence itself); III.4 ¶16: 'Without a stable theory, and the desire of truth absolutely leading, all is mere vanity'.
- **operator** — “The searcher of nature ought to be, as she herself is, faithful, simple, patient, constant, giving his mind to the discovery of truth alone, hopeful and benevolent.” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The operator's character should mirror Nature's own qualities: faithful, simple, patient, constant, single-minded for truth, hopeful, benevolent.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15.
- **operator** — “to free himself from the usurpation of vice, and to be good, just, and of sound reason, ready at hand to help mankind” `III.4 ¶15` (QUOTED: the hermetic Master (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Moral prerequisites for entry into the hidden Wisdom: freedom from vice, goodness, justice, sound reason, readiness to help mankind, a serene countenance, diligence to save, and patient keeping of the secrets.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15, citing III.4 n10; Atwood adopts the list into her own argument.
  - Also: “be himself a patient guardian of the arcane secrets of philosophy” `III.4 ¶15`
- **operator** — “if to these qualifications a convenient leisure be added” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Besides character, the operator needs adequate free time; then progress may be hoped for.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15.
- **impediment** — “neither will a busy head nor a faithless heart, by impure hands, be able; nor does a vagabond inclination enter in by the narrow way of life” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** A distracted mind, lack of faith, impurity and a wandering disposition each bar success.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15.
  - Also: “opinionative, unstable in purpose, willful and dissimulating” `III.4 ¶15`
- **impediment** — “With respect to the impediments of body mentioned by Geber, these are less numerous and more commonly supplied: Hands and eyes are to be had in abundance” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Physical limitations are minor; ordinary bodily means are easily found, and when joined to the mental and moral conditions other hindrances can be passed by.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15.
- **stage** — “The same patient hope and free perspicuity of thought and imagination also will be called for, in acquiring the Hermetic doctrine, by perusal, as is afterwards needed for the experimental proof.” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Study of the doctrine comes first and experimental proof follows. Both need the same patient hope and clear, free thought and imagination, so the student should cultivate in study the qualities he will later practise.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15.
  - Also: “he should, as a matter of course, be possessed, or learn, at least, to cultivate the incipient qualifications he intends afterwards to bring to practice” `III.4 ¶15`
- **principle** — “all directing on the same Royal Road when this is found; but useless to most wayfarers” `III.4 ¶15` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** The many alchemical books (Revelations, Keys, Gates, Guides and the like) all point to one path, but are usable only once that path is found. This chunk does not say what the Royal Road is.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶15 lists the titles, then this clause.
- **principle** — “In almost all the records of Alchemy, the inner sense is held aloof from the literal” `III.4 ¶16` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Alchemical texts carry a hidden meaning separate from their literal wording, too abstruse for the natural understanding at first view. Even plain statements of the truth have been ignored or disbelieved.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶16.
  - Also: “if, by hazard or benevolent design, the truth has escaped in plain discourse, it has been either slighted over or disbelieved” `III.4 ¶16`
  - Also: “the subject is too abstruse, and too intricately dealt with, for the natural understanding to apprehend at first view” `III.4 ¶16`
- **principle** — “their records have proved like a curious two-edged instrument --- to some it has cut out dainties, and to others it has only served to cut their fingers” `III.4 ¶16` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** The adepts' writings benefit those who know how to use them and harm those who do not; the fault lies with the unskilled reader.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶16.
  - Also: “It is not for the ignorant to blame the power of that which they do not known how to handle” `III.4 ¶16`
- **principle** — “not believing, as he quaintly expresses it, that there was any water in our sea” `III.4 ¶16` (QUOTED: Sendivogius)
  - **Plain (interpreted):** Sendivogius' image for what his friends would not believe when he explained the Art word by word. This chunk does not explain 'water' or 'our sea'.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶16 gives the phrase only as his friends' disbelief after 'word by word explaining it'.
- **principle** — “seeing my Light in my hand, which they could not discern well” `III.4 ¶16` (QUOTED: Eirenaeus (applied by Atwood))
  - **Plain (interpreted):** In Atwood's outward application, the candle is the abstruse light of Alchemy, which the gross understanding cannot bear; the fox-fire by which they read and comment on Geber and Rhasis is a false light that seems sufficient to them.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶16: Atwood applies the allegory: 'the abstruse light of Alchemy is not fir for the understanding of all'; the darkness 'had a false fore of its own'. The mapping of fox-fire to book-commentary is inferred from the scene.
  - Also: “they had with them another light, as it were, Fox-fire or Rotten wood, or Glow-worm’s tails” `III.4 ¶16`
  - Also: “that darkness I now perceived had a false fore of its own” `III.4 ¶16`
- **impediment** — “lest folly or willfulness, precipitously passing into practice, should either perish or break the divine legislation in inharmonious effects” `III.4 ¶16` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** Practice is dangerous. General incredulity guards the Art by keeping the foolish or wilful from rushing into practice, where they might be destroyed or produce disordered effects that break divine law.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶16; compare III.4 ¶11 on danger to the ill-informed.
  - Also: “To say that the pursuit is without danger to the ill-informed would be presuming too much” `III.4 ¶11`
- **principle** — “Without a stable theory, and the desire of truth absolutely leading, all is mere vanity and a vexation of the spirit.” `III.4 ¶16` (ATWOOD)
  - **Plain (stated):** A firm theory and an overriding desire for truth must guide the work; belief without the power of realizing good profits nothing.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶16.
  - Also: “what would it profit him to learn to believe without the power of realizing any good?” `III.4 ¶16`
- **procedure** — “Attingens a fine usque ad finem fortiter, Disponens omnia suaviter;” `III.4 ¶17` (QUOTED: Norton (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Carry the work strongly from beginning to end despite opposition, and arrange everything in between gently, with a mildness that comes of grace.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶17: the verse glosses it 'That is proceede mightily to th’ End From the beginning maugre the Fiend. All things disposing, in the meane space, With great suavity that cometh of grace'.
  - Also: “proceede mightily to th’ End From the beginning maugre the Fiend.” `III.4 ¶17`
- **operator** — “But some Lords be of stable wit, Such only be apt to finish it” `III.4 ¶17` (QUOTED: Norton (endorsed))
  - **Plain (stated):** Only people of steady mind complete the work; the short-witted, changeable and credulous, who drop the old for every new tale, fail and lose money.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶17.
  - Also: “All short-witted men and mutable Such must needes be variable;” `III.4 ¶17`
  - Also: “To every new tale of them told, They give credence and leave the old.” `III.4 ¶17`
  - Also: “Such credence doth their coffers grieve;” `III.4 ¶17`

## Terms

- **Rule of Reason** (also: Reason, reason, sound reason) — Reason as the guide of the pursuit: under it every step is progressive, it alone can enter the true Form of Gold, and its province is to prove all things. (ATWOOD)
  - “every step is progressive where the Rule of Reason is pursued” `III.4 ¶11`
  - “Reason alone can enter into it” `III.4 ¶11`
  - “It is the province of reason to inquire and endeavor, by perscrutination, to prove all things” `III.4 ¶15`
- **true Form of Gold** — The real object of Alchemy's 'golden promise', which avarice, ambition or curiosity cannot reach; only Reason enters it. Contrasted with 'the dead metal'. Not further defined here. (ATWOOD)
  - “neither will be found to be the true Form of Gold” `III.4 ¶11`
- **aurific seed** — A living gold-producing seed that must be 'quickened in life'; contrasted with dead metal. The covetous spirit cannot quicken it. Not further explained here. (ATWOOD)
  - “it cannot quicken the aurific seed in life” `III.4 ¶11`
- **Wisdom** (also: hidden Wisdom) — Personified as feminine. Her 'fiery ordeal' and 'pure Light' repel the covetous; one is 'introduced into this hidden Wisdom' by moral qualification. (ATWOOD)
  - “he is the first to fly from Wisdom’s fiery ordeal” `III.4 ¶11`
  - “who would be introduced into this hidden Wisdom” `III.4 ¶15`
- **infidelity** (also: faithlessness) — Fashionable faithlessness that without rational foundation denounces everything new or unfamiliar and believes nothing worthy of belief or veneration; perhaps the most absurd obstacle to human advancement. (ATWOOD)
  - “by infidelity, we mean that fashionable kind of faithlessness, which, without rational foundation, denounces everything that is new” `III.4 ¶12`
- **Skeptics** (also: skepticism, modern skeptics) — Those Geber condemns next above the Covetous; they remain ignorant by choice and hinder others. Modern skeptics 'ape' sound reason. Their skepticism is the 'mock ally' of rational skepticism. (ATWOOD)
  - “next above the Covetous, Skeptics are condemned by Geber” `III.4 ¶12`
- **rational skepticism** — Reason's inquiry that proves all things, rejecting the false and holding the true; required of the Hermetic Student. (ATWOOD)
  - = discrimination: “Such skepticism, more properly perhaps called discrimination” `III.4 ¶15`
  - “Rational skepticism has quite another object and never exhibits itself in the refractory form of its mock ally.” `III.4 ¶15`
- **perscrutination** — The searching examination by which reason proves all things. (ATWOOD)
  - “It is the province of reason to inquire and endeavor, by perscrutination, to prove all things” `III.4 ¶15`
- **Mesmerism** — The door Nature opens to the multitude, relieving suffering and offering 'a new guide to knowledge and felicity'; the Door-keeper that first introduced Atwood to the vestibule of antique science. (ATWOOD)
  - = Door-keeper: “We allude to Mesmerism: not ashamed, but grateful to acknowledge the neglected Door-keeper” `III.4 ¶13`
  - “the neglected Door-keeper that gave us first introduction to the vestibule of antique science” `III.4 ¶13`
- **philosopher** (also: philosophers) — The only one to whom Nature surrenders her casket: disciplined in her schools, tried and proven able to bear the sacred trust. (ATWOOD)
  - “she will surrender to none but a philosopher, one too that has been disciplined in her schools” `III.4 ¶13`
- **Alchemy** (also: Alkimy) — The science and Art in question. God grants it to few; its records hold the inner sense aloof from the literal; it will probably stay concealed for ages. (ATWOOD)
  - “Yet is this science of Alkimy full trew;” `III.4 ¶14`
  - “the Art will probably continue concealed though many ages still” `III.4 ¶16`
- **Hermetic doctrine** — The theory acquired by reading, which calls for the same faculties as the later experimental proof. (ATWOOD)
  - “in acquiring the Hermetic doctrine, by perusal, as is afterwards needed for the experimental proof” `III.4 ¶15`
- **experimental proof** — The practical stage of the art, following study of the doctrine. (ATWOOD)
  - “as is afterwards needed for the experimental proof” `III.4 ¶15`
- **Royal Road** — The single path toward which all Hermetic treatises direct, 'when this is found'. Not otherwise defined here. (ATWOOD)
  - “all directing on the same Royal Road when this is found” `III.4 ¶15`
- **inner sense** — The hidden meaning of the alchemical records, kept apart from their literal sense. (ATWOOD)
  - “In almost all the records of Alchemy, the inner sense is held aloof from the literal” `III.4 ¶16`
- **Light** (also: light, the Light, Her pure Light) — The goal one passes into 'by a living experience'; also the 'abstruse light of Alchemy', not perceptible to the gross intelligence of the many. In Eirenaeus' allegory, the narrator's light, contrasted with the false light of the darkness. (ATWOOD)
  - “progressively passing by a living experience into the Light” `III.4 ¶15`
  - “the abstruse light of Alchemy is not fir for the understanding of all” `III.4 ¶16`

## Sources cited

- **Geber** `III.4 ¶12, III.4 ¶15, III.4 ¶16` — Supports: his ranking of the unfit (Skeptics condemned next above the Covetous) and his 'impediments of body' are followed. Also named as an era ('Geber’s and Norton’s time') and as read by those in darkness in Eirenaeus' allegory.
- **Norton**, *Ordinal, chap. 1* — “Scarcely seven men maie this science finde.” `III.4 ¶14, III.4 n9, III.4 ¶15` — Supports: the science is granted by God to few faithful, lowly men and withheld from proud clerks who trust their wits and then call it fable. Also named as an era in III.4 ¶15.
- **Norton**, *Ordinal, chap. 6* — “But some Lords be of stable wit, Such only be apt to finish it” `III.4 ¶16, III.4 ¶17, III.4 n12` — Supports: the unfit should cease; the fit proceed mightily and suavely; only those of stable wit finish.
- **the hermetic Master**, *Tract. Aur., cap. 2* — “to free himself from the usurpation of vice, and to be good, just, and of sound reason” `III.4 ¶15, III.4 n10` — Supports: moral qualifications for one to be introduced into the hidden Wisdom.
- **Sendivogius** — “there was any water in our sea” `III.4 ¶16` — Illustrates: friends, who would be accounted philosophers, disbelieved his word-by-word explanation of the Art.
- **Eirenaeus**, *Ripley Revived, First Gate, p. 121* — “that darkness I now perceived had a false fore of its own” `III.4 ¶16, III.4 n11` — Illustrates (in 'outward application'): the light of Alchemy is not perceptible to the gross understanding, which has its own false light.
- **Rhasis** `III.4 ¶16` — Named in Eirenaeus' allegory as read by those sitting in darkness.
- **Galileo** `III.4 ¶12` — Contrasts: Atwood would as soon have lived in Galileo's days as see the recovered secret sink into obloquy through modern skepticism.
- **the wise / adepts** — “As the adepts indeed foretold, their records have proved like a curious two-edged instrument” `III.4 ¶11, III.4 ¶16` — Supports: they declare none attain without right intention and God's blessing, that the pursuit is dangerous to the ill-informed, and that their records are two-edged.
- **this holi letter** — “Attingens a fine usque ad finem fortiter, Disponens omnia suaviter;” `III.4 ¶17` — Cited within Norton's verse as the sentence to follow; unattributed in the text.
- **Revelations, Open Entrances, New Lights and True Lights, Sunshine and Moonshine, Auroras, Dawns, Manuals, Introductory Lexicons, Triumphal Chariots, Banners, Gates, Keys, Guides** `III.4 ¶15` — Illustrates: generic titles of alchemical books, all directing to the Royal Road once found but useless to most modern readers.

## Ambiguities

- “Wisdom’s fiery ordeal, not able to enter with his camel form, or daring to prove vaporous essence in Her pure Light” `III.4 ¶11`
  - Reading: A moral or spiritual test that the covetous cannot pass.
  - Reading: An actual stage or trial within the work involving fire and a 'vaporous essence'.
  - Basis: III.4 ¶11 says only that the covetous spirit is 'too gross to permeate the ethereal profundity' and flees the ordeal; nothing further explains the fire or the essence.
- “the neglected Door-keeper that gave us first introduction to the vestibule of antique science” `III.4 ¶13`
  - Reading: Mesmerism is only Atwood's personal and historical entry point to the ancient science.
  - Reading: Mesmerism is part of the Hermetic method itself, an initial stage or means of the work.
  - Basis: Reading 1: 'gave us first introduction', 'vestibule'. Reading 2: Nature offers through it 'a new guide to knowledge and felicity' (III.4 ¶13). This chunk does not settle the matter.
- “How he which hereof lawful witness be;” `III.4 ¶14`
  - Reading: Wise clerks may consider that only one who is a lawful witness, i.e. has experienced the art, can attest to or judge it.
  - Reading: The line is incomplete as printed and its exact sense is uncertain.
  - Basis: Reading 1 is supported by the blind-painter lines that follow. Reading 2: the sentence beginning 'Yet every wise clerke well consider may' lacks a main verb in this line (III.4 ¶14).
- “progressively passing by a living experience into the Light” `III.4 ¶15`
  - Reading: The Light is an experiential attainment within the work.
  - Reading: The Light is understanding of the abstruse doctrine.
  - Basis: Reading 1: 'living experience'; 'Her pure Light' (III.4 ¶11). Reading 2: 'the abstruse light of Alchemy is not fir for the understanding of all' (III.4 ¶16).
- “This striking illustration of Eirenaeus, in the outward application, bears not unaptly to our conclusion” `III.4 ¶16`
  - Reading: 'Outward application' implies the allegory also has an inner, operative meaning that Atwood does not give here.
  - Reading: It simply means applying the allegory to the outward reception of Alchemy by the public.
  - Basis: Reading 1: compare 'the inner sense is held aloof from the literal' (III.4 ¶16). Reading 2: the stated conclusion concerns only 'the understanding of all' and 'the mass of mankind' (III.4 ¶16).
