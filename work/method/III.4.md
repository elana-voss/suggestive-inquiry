# Method material — III.4 Of the Mental Requisites and Impediments Incidental to Individuals, Either as Masters or Students, in the Hermetic Art

Conventions: refs are to the chapter (III.4). Label = status; voice. "Interpreted" readings keep the basis given by the chunk readings.

## The subject (what the work is done on)

1. **The material is something every person has and lives by. Having it does not make anyone fit to handle it.** The chapter does not say what it is.
   - "because all men have the material and live by it, that every one s therefore fitted to handle the same" (¶2)
   - *stated (that all have it); ATWOOD.* What it is: see Open questions 1.

2. **To start operating, the student needs a "Subject" that works with him and responds to his design. Without one the work never takes form.** The chapter files this under finding "suitable assistance". It also says there is no increase of the Spirit without a medium and a bond.
   - "without a Subject to work with and reciprocate the design it remains abortive, as a statue in the conception without the marble to give it utterance" (¶29)
   - Also: "to find suitable assistance in the undertaking; many have halted there a long while unprofitably" (¶29); "Behold, two are better than one, says the Preacher" (¶30)
   - *stated (need for a Subject); interpreted (whether the Subject is a person or the material); ATWOOD.* See Open questions 2.

3. **An "entranced Subject" takes in imaginative impressions, and these linger in the working-place.** This is the only place the chapter describes a Subject's state.
   - "the entranced Subject is more or less susceptible of all imaginative impressures, which, even after their act has passed away, hang and pollute the ether of their pertinacious abode" (¶42)
   - *stated; ATWOOD (citing adepts and Cornelius Agrippa).* Whether this is the same Subject as in point 2 is not stated (Open questions 2).

4. **The philosophers' gold and silver are not common metal. What is taken is a seed ("sperme") out of a body, which contains the luminaries, life, light and the four elements, all coming from one Image.** The body is not identified.
   - "Our gld and silver ben nocommon plate, But a sperme owte of a body I take," (¶21)
   - Also: "In the which is alle, Sol, Lune, Life and Light, Water and Erth, Fyre and Fryght: And alle cometh of one Image," (¶21)
   - *interpreted (whether the seed is identified with "Our gld and silver"); QUOTED: Pierce, the Black Monk (in Atwood's chain of endorsed authorities).*

5. **What the work brings to life is a living, gold-producing seed, not dead metal.** Greed can heap metal but cannot quicken the seed.
   - "Mammon may draw the dead metal in heaps about its sordid circumference; but it cannot quicken the aurific seed in life" (¶11)
   - *interpreted; ATWOOD.* The chapter does not say what the seed is.

6. **To know the principal First Matter is to have found the Sanctuary of Nature.** After that only "the Door" remains (see Procedure 15).
   - "if thou knowest the principal First Matter, know also for certain thou hast discovered the Sanctuary of Nature" (¶44)
   - *stated (the equivalence); QUOTED: Vaughan, endorsed by Atwood as "lessons of an English Adept".*

7. **The Philosophic Vessel is described in mixed bodily and laboratory terms. Atwood distrusts these accounts as over-sophisticated.**
   - "the Philosophic Vessel and its multiform distillatory apparatus, of nerves, veins, and alembics" (¶30)
   - *interpreted (whether the vessel is partly a living body); ATWOOD.* See Open questions 13.

8. **The essential vessel is God's gift, and it is "glass", the material that keeps spiritual matters from escaping. In the end the Spirit builds and vitrifies its own vessel. Only the first instruments are left to the artist's choice.**
   - "Si Deus non dedisset nobis vas Nihil dedisset, and that is glasse" (¶31)
   - Also: "But other vessels be made of glasse, That spiritual matters should not outpasse" (¶31); "The Spirit finally constructs its own vessel and vitrifies it" (¶32); "the artist is at liberty to make choice according to convenience of his instruments in the beginning" (¶32)
   - *stated (vas = glass: Norton's Master, via Norton; the Spirit builds its vessel: ATWOOD); interpreted (that ¶31 and ¶32 describe the same vessel).*

9. **The best vessel matches "vessel of nature" and suits the quantity and circumstances of the matter. Each operation has its own vessel shape** (small for separation, broad for humectation, long for precipitation, and so on).
   - "The best fashion is ye maie be sure, She that concordeth with vessel of nature;" (¶31)
   - Also: "As every Chapter hath divers intents, So hath it divers instruments," (¶31); "Which are full derke, To ordeyne instruments according to the weke." (¶31)
   - *interpreted; QUOTED: Norton (offered by Atwood as "one hint").* "vessel of nature" is not explained.

10. **The vessels are nested one inside another, with a third outside them to regulate the heat. They are plain and unlike ordinary ware.**
    - "One within another, it is a pretty feate, The Third without them to guide up the heate." (¶33)
    - Also: "Yet they are plain without wrinkle or hem;" (¶33)
    - *stated as description, not decoded; QUOTED: Charnocke.*

11. **The true furnace is small and simple, a "shell" you can hold in one hand. There is one glass, though two may be used. Geber calls it Athanor. Elaborate furnaces mislead or "did nature wrong."**
    - "The true furnace has been described as a little simple shell; thou mayst easily carry it, says Vaughan, in one of thy Hands" (¶38)
    - Also: "the glass is one, and no more; but philosophers have sued two, and so mayest thou" (¶38); "Geber calls this furnace Athanor" (¶38); "Many of them did nature wrong." (¶37)
    - *stated (size, simplicity, name); QUOTED: Vaughan; Norton; ATWOOD (Athanor, "misleading subtlety").*

12. **Something becomes most precious in a philosopher's hands and worthless or worse in anyone else's.**
    - "lest That, which in the hands of a philosopher becomes most precious, should be otherwise made worthless, or worse than all" (¶18)
    - *interpreted (whether "That" is the knowledge or the matter); ATWOOD.* See Open questions 10.

13. **The aim is to bring out the most hidden point of "kinde".**
    - "To fetch out the secretest pointe of kinde;" (¶14)
    - *interpreted ("kinde" = nature rests only on our translation); QUOTED: Norton (endorsed).*

## The agent (what does the work: fire, spirit, light, intellect…)

1. **The active power of the work comes from the operator himself. Even unstrengthened, it tends to give form to whatever idea the will conceives. It is strengthened by degrees of concentration and multiplied in the Conjunction.** It is probably the "ministering Spirit" named in the sentence just before.
   - "Springing directly from ourselves, this highly effective agent, even in the natural state, inclines, as the will directs, to image the conceived Idea" (¶9)
   - Also: "without intervention or concealment in the ministering Spirit throughout" (¶9)
   - *stated (it springs from ourselves and follows the will); interpreted (agent = ministering Spirit); ATWOOD.*

2. **Only one guide can free the single hidden spirit and intention of the writings: the "Rational Light". It grows stronger in practice, is then raised to "the allegoric siege of life", and there, kindled by divine wrath, overcomes a stronghold of evil.**
   - "there is but One Leader, who should undertake the deliverance; one only, we repeat; he that is allied --- the same Rational Light that, in practice strengthening, afterwards is raised to the allegoric siege of life" (¶19)
   - Also: "and by the fire of his divine wrath enkindled, to overcome the stronghold of evil therein allied" (¶19)
   - *stated (One Leader = Rational Light); interpreted (the operative sequence); ATWOOD.* See Open questions 11.

3. **The Spirit, the power the abusers "compel", is what the depraved raise to an exalted energy and then turn to private ends.**
   - "compelling the Spirit to their private ends" (¶6)
   - Also: "having succeeded in inducing an exalted energy, have willfully denied the Light its true fulfillment" (¶6)
   - *interpreted ("exalted energy", "the Light" and "the Spirit" name the work's power; whether this Spirit is the ministering Spirit of ¶9 is not stated); ATWOOD.*

4. **God is the real giver of increase. He is called Nature's Will. In the conjunction the Spirit passes beyond the operator's control and recreates by God's will alone.**
   - "There is a period too, when in conjunction, the Spirit transcends all earthly control, and passing under the Divine Hand, recreates by His sovereign will alone." (¶20)
   - Also: "it is vain to look for a blessing from Nature without His cooperation who is her Will" (¶20); "Paul may plant and Apollos may water, but God alone giveth the increase" (¶20)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

5. **The Quintessence, identified with "the sweet spirit of Peace", lives in the elements. Violent handling makes it withdraw, and it will not act on the Matter.**
   - "the sweet spirit of Peace, the true eternal Quintessence, will depart from the elements, leaving both of them and thee to confusion" (¶44)
   - Also: "neither will he apply himself to that Matter as long as it is in thy violent destructive Hands" (¶44)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Vaughan (endorsed).*

6. **Something called "the Water of the Wood" brings about the union ("marriage").** It is not explained.
   - "But the Water of the Wood, maketh the marriage." (¶21)
   - *stated as image, not decoded; QUOTED: Pierce, the Black Monk.*

7. **Fire.** Kindling "the Fire" is outwardly trivial but holds every secret of corruption and generation. Excessive heat sets the moist natures against the "central fire". The third class of prayer gives a "plenitude of Divine Fire". The chapter does not say whether these fires are one thing.
   - "the excitation of the Fire is a very trivial, slight, almost a ridiculous thing; nevertheless, all the secrets of corruption and generation are therein contained" (¶38)
   - Also: "they will stand up against the central fire, and the central fire against them" (¶44); "through the energy of the third, we receive a perfect plenitude of Divine Fire" (¶24)
   - *stated (each statement); interpreted (any link between them); QUOTED: Vaughan; Iamblicus.*

8. **Desire, concentrated as prayer, is the means by which the research finally becomes manifest. Prayer is the "Divine Key" to Wisdom.**
   - "a research which is so much wrapped in and about the Desire as to be ultimately made manifest through its means" (¶20)
   - Also: "It likewise is the Divine Key which opens to men the penetralia of Wisdom" (¶24)
   - *interpreted (Desire as operative means); ATWOOD; stated for the Divine Key (QUOTED: Iamblicus, endorsed).*

9. **The Human Will, acting with its Final Cause, contributes to the work.**
   - "the corroborative efficacy of the Human Will, acting in concert with its Final Cause to fulfill it" (¶28)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.* "Final Cause" is not defined here beyond ¶6 and ¶9.

## The operator (requirements: knowledge, moral state, will, guidance, secrecy…)

1. **Innate aptitude is needed and rare. A few years of study or casual handling are not enough.**
   - "Few, we fear, judging by our own observation, and very few according to the testimony of more experienced observers, are endowed with a disposition naturally adapted towards this peculiar research" (¶2)
   - Also: "nor, et us be assured, will a few short years of study or idle handling of the matter, be sufficient to admit a man to the arcane of Hermetic science" (¶2); "Quod inter mille, millia, vix sunt tres electi" (¶1, Norton); "Soe among millions of millions of mankind Scarcely seven men maie this science finde." (¶14, Norton)
   - *stated; ATWOOD, supported by Norton.*

2. **The operator must be sound in body and soul, because the art is perfected through his own members serving nature. Atwood treats the bodily impediments as minor and easily made up.**
   - "because he is helped by those members, by mediation of which likewise, as ministering to nature, this art is perfected" (¶3)
   - Also: "Hands and eyes are to be had in abundance" (¶15)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Geber (taken by Atwood "as a foundation"); ATWOOD (bodily impediments minor).*

3. **A naturally penetrating mind is needed, one that investigates nature's principles and the techniques that follow nature's action.**
   - "He that hath not a natural sagacity and soul, searching subtly, and scrutinizing natural principles, the Fundamentals of Nature, and Artifices which can follow Nature in the properties of her action, cannot find the true Radix of this most precious science." (¶3)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Geber.*

4. **Three conditions for attaining the fruits: upright intention, God's blessing, and tested experience. Next to benevolence and right intention, perseverance is the requirement most insisted on.**
   - "rectitude of intention and the blessing of God on a well tried experience" (¶11)
   - Also: "Nothing is more generally insisted on, next to benevolence and rectitude of intention, than perseverance for this experiment" (¶45); "There is nothing stronger than Perseverance, for it ends in miracles" (¶44, Vaughan); "Learn from thy errors to be infallible, and from they misfortunes to be constant." (¶44)
   - *stated; ATWOOD; Vaughan (endorsed).*

5. **The real "cost" is giving up self-centred life and devoting the product to good and truthful ends.** Atwood glosses Geber's "necessary charges" this way.
   - "being afraid in their own persons to interposit the necessary charges, i.e., to abandon the life of selfhood, and return the product to a benevolent and truthful end" (¶6)
   - Also: "are afraid to interposit the necessary charges" (¶3, Geber)
   - *stated (Atwood's "i.e." gloss); ATWOOD reinterpreting Geber.*

6. **Critical discrimination is needed: test everything and keep the true. It makes truth supreme in the understanding, which practical success depends on.**
   - "Such skepticism, more properly perhaps called discrimination, is as much required by the Hermetic Student as the other is obnoxious." (¶15)
   - Also: "It is the province of reason to inquire and endeavor, by perscrutination, to prove all things" (¶15); "cultivate that distinctive supremacy of truth in the understanding spirit which is so essential to success in the practical research" (¶15)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

7. **The operator must already hold a correct ideal and a stable theory, or he will not recognize the thing when it appears.**
   - "they who have never entertained the true ideal should fail to recognize the image when represented before their eyes" (¶15)
   - Also: "Without a stable theory, and the desire of truth absolutely leading, all is mere vanity and a vexation of the spirit." (¶16)
   - *interpreted (as a requirement); stated (stable theory); ATWOOD.*

8. **The operator's character should mirror Nature's. Moral prerequisites include freedom from vice, goodness, justice, sound reason, readiness to help mankind and keeping the secrets.**
   - "The searcher of nature ought to be, as she herself is, faithful, simple, patient, constant, giving his mind to the discovery of truth alone, hopeful and benevolent." (¶15)
   - Also: "to free himself from the usurpation of vice, and to be good, just, and of sound reason, ready at hand to help mankind" (¶15, the hermetic Master, adopted by Atwood); "be himself a patient guardian of the arcane secrets of philosophy" (¶15); "Such as be faithful, trew and lowly," (¶14, Norton); "Must be meeke and full of mercy:" (¶21, Pierce)
   - *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: the hermetic Master; Norton; Pierce (all endorsed).*

9. **Leisure is required as well as character.**
   - "if to these qualifications a convenient leisure be added" (¶15)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

10. **A steady, unchanging mind is needed. Only the stable-witted finish; the changeable and credulous fail.**
    - "But some Lords be of stable wit, Such only be apt to finish it" (¶17)
    - Also: "All short-witted men and mutable Such must needes be variable;" (¶17); "a soul movable from opinion to opinion, and from will to will" (¶3, Geber)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Norton (endorsed); Geber.*

11. **Long study and real desire are required. The writings are veiled so that only such a person understands them.**
    - "he only who was really desirous, and made fit by long study to pursue the work, should be able to understand them" (¶19)
    - Also: "we treat of it in such words as to the wise shall, by pursuit become intelligible" (¶18, Geber)
    - *stated; ATWOOD; Geber.*

12. **Arnold names three requisites: a subtle mind, manual skill, and a free will for the operation. Lully adds Divine Favour and books.**
    - "three requisites, viz., subtlety of mind, manual skill, and a free will for the operation" (¶20)
    - Also: "a sufficiency of the Divine Favour, and books to open the understanding and give it zest for truth" (¶20)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Arnold; Lully (in Atwood's endorsed chain).*

13. **Prayer must be joined with self-sacrifice. Each strengthens the other.**
    - "prayer and self-sacrifice conspiring, mutually corroborate and confer on each other a perfect efficacy in Divine works" (¶28)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

14. **The operator must examine himself: what kind of man he is, what he means to do, and what he knows and has practised. He is a co-operator with God, not the maker.**
    - "consider well with thyself what manner of man thou art, and what it is that thou wouldst do: For it is no small matter." (¶44)
    - Also: "let him first examine his knowledge, and especially his practice" (¶44)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Vaughan (endorsed).*

15. **Required dispositions: charity, which feeds hope and faith; the mind turned upward while the hands work on earth; a heaven-like way of life, needed for communion with superiors; inner unity apart from crowds of people and passions; listening much and speaking little.**
    - "Have thy heart in heaven and thy hands upon earth. Ascend in piety and descend in charity." (¶44)
    - Also: "Have therefore a charitable seraphic mind, charitable and not destructive to thyself." (¶44); "as God is the Father, so Charity is the nurse of Faith" (¶44); "You must live, as says Agrippa, according to God and the angels, rejecting all things that are dissimilar to the heaven; otherwise thou canst have no communion with superiors." (¶44); "Unus esto non solus. Avoid the multitude, as well of passions as of persons." (¶44); "Nature gave us one tongue and two ears, that we might hear much and speak little" (¶44)
    - *stated (the precepts); interpreted ("inner unity" partly rests on our translation of "Unus esto non solus"); QUOTED: Vaughan, citing Agrippa and Zeno (endorsed).*

16. **Teachers: the art is taught by mouth, under oath, only to someone tested over time for truth, virtue and a steady mind. The teacher carries the peril.**
    - "But he were proved perfectly with space, Whether he were able to receive this grace. For his trewth, virtue and for his stable wit," (¶10)
    - Also: "That it must needs be taught fro’ mouth to mouth;" (¶10); "Receive it with a most sacred oath," (¶10); "Upon the teacher resteth dreadfully" (¶10)
    - *stated (oral, oath, teacher's peril); interpreted ("with space" = over time, from our translation); QUOTED: Norton (endorsed).*

17. **Nature yields the secret only to a philosopher trained in her schools and proven, and only when approached subtly, conscientiously and courteously.**
    - "she will surrender to none but a philosopher, one too that has been disciplined in her schools, tried and proven to ensure his ability to bear the sacred trust" (¶13)
    - Also: "she must be moved to it subtly, conscientiously, courteously" (¶13)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

18. **Helpers (ministers) in the Gross Work must be sober, wise, diligent, truthful, watchful, timorous, close of tongue, clean and obedient. The operator must stand over the potter and show by likeness what to make. Good servants are rare.**
    - "Noe Minister is apt to this intent But he is sober, wise, and diligent;" (¶37)
    - Also: "First then with the potter thou must begin, Which cannot make what he hath never seen." (¶33, Charnocke); "shew him what to do by some sign or similitude" (¶33); "A good servant, saith Solomon, let him be unto thee, As thine own hearte,in each degree:" (¶35, Charnocke)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Norton; Charnocke.*

19. **Tending the work is light. Vaughan thinks women are fitter for it than men.**
    - "a lady may read the Arcadia, and at the same time attend this philosophy without disturbing her fancy" (¶38)
    - Also: "I think women are fitter for it than men, for in such things they are more neat and patient" (¶38)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Vaughan.* For the conflict with Norton's eight ministers, see Open questions 15.

20. **The working conditions depend on the constitution and instinct of a "spiritual guide".**
    - "the other conditions, which vary with the constitution and instinct of the spiritual guide" (¶42)
    - *interpreted; ATWOOD.* Who or what the guide is: see Open questions 2.

21. **The student must be a good steward. A gift that is wasted or hoarded is taken away.**
    - "God withholds not this increase alone, but deprives the talent likewise, where it is wasted or hoarded without interest or promotion" (¶20)
    - Also: "he therefore must be a good steward who would overlook the rich treasury of life" (¶20)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

## The procedure and its stages (in the order the chapter gives, if it gives one)

The chapter's order runs: disposition (above), then study of the doctrine with prayer, then finding a Subject or assistance, then instruments, ministers, furnaces and places, then the Preparation and the Gross Work, then the ultimate Philosophic Work. It ends by handing the rest over to practice.

1. **Study comes first, experimental proof second, and both need the same qualities.** The student cultivates in study what he will later practise.
   - "The same patient hope and free perspicuity of thought and imagination also will be called for, in acquiring the Hermetic doctrine, by perusal, as is afterwards needed for the experimental proof." (¶15)
   - Also: "nor will he be able advantageously to bring his Hand to the practice whose Mind is sluggish in studying the theory" (¶20, Ricardus); "thou ought first to read with unwearied patience and perseverance the writings of the philosophers before thou extendest thy Hand to the Philosophic Work" (¶20, Zachary); "study is required in the beginning, and meditation, that a good foundation may be laid" (¶20, Lucerna Salis)
   - *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Ricardus, Zachary, author of the Lucerna Salis (endorsed).*

2. **Gather the teaching scattered across the books and test what you gather by experiment until you understand the whole.**
   - "collecting our dispersed intention, which we have described in divers places that we might not expose it to malignant, ignorant men" (¶20)
   - Also: "let him prove his collection even into the knowledge, studying and experimenting with the instance of sagacious labour, till he come to an entire understanding of the whole" (¶20)
   - *stated; QUOTED: the prince (endorsed).*

3. **Prayer goes with the work, never instead of it. God grants the art only through means, namely instruction and manual labour. Without prayer, work already begun is blocked or ends badly.**
   - "He does not either grant it immediately to any person, but always by mediate dispositions, to wit, by instructions and the labour of the hands" (¶20)
   - Also: "Pray, says Sendivogius, pray; but work." (¶20); "Mediums nevertheless are to be employed" (¶20, Zachary); "Man may conditionate: --- ought, by patient labour disciplining, to prepare the way of the Greater in the Lesser Good" (¶20, ATWOOD)
   - *stated; QUOTED: author of the Lucerna Salis; Sendivogius; Zachary; ATWOOD.*

4. **Method of solving the enigmas (and, esoterically, of the operations): burning desire is prayer, and varied active thought alongside it is "cooperation". Persist until the solution arrives suddenly.** Adepts prayed in the middle of the operations "when the mind is lifted up".
   - "for if you burn with a great desire of knowing them, that is prayer: and when you incline your mind to this or that, variously discussing and meditating many things, this is cooperation" (¶22)
   - Also: "adepts are eloquent in their imputations of its efficacy in Spagyric Works, when the mind is lifted up" (¶22); "even in the midst of the operations of Vital Chemistry, full of labour and toil, they prayed, says Kirchringius" (¶22); "that your prayer may not be, for want of exertion a tempting of God" (¶22)
   - *stated (for riddle-solving); interpreted (prayer as part of the operations themselves); QUOTED: Kirchringius (endorsed; extent of quotation unclear, Open questions 12); ATWOOD.*

5. **Three classes of prayer, in order:** (1) "Collective", gathering the mental powers under their leader to enter the Enigma, which pertains to Illumination; (2) binding concordant spirits before intellectual alliance, a Communion of Operation; (3) the final seal of union, where desire led by faith reaches its end and Divine Fire is received.
   - "The first of which, as pertaining to the early initiations, was called Collective, having for its object to gather the mental powers into accord with their leader, seeking a clue whereby it may enter the intelligible profundity of the Enigma." (¶24)
   - Also: "The second effects the bonds of concordant spirits; calling forth, prior to intellectual alliance." (¶24); "The third is the final authoritative seal of union; when the desire, leading from faith, becomes into its true end." (¶24); "The first, recapitulates our author, pertains to Illumination; the second, to a Communion of Operation" (¶24)
   - *stated (the classes); interpreted (that they are stages of the Hermetic work); QUOTED: Iamblicus (endorsed).*

6. **Effective desire is turned inward, addressed "within the Living Temple from the Divine Light within", rather than outward for common concerns.**
   - "addressed within the Living Temple from the Divine Light within" (¶27)
   - *interpreted; ATWOOD.* "Living Temple" is not explained.

7. **Once general knowledge is gained, the next difficulty is finding suitable assistance, a Subject** (see Subject 2). The work is not to be undertaken alone.
   - "The ascent to Unity is arduous, and the descent is not undertaken in safety alone" (¶30)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

8. **Choose instruments, and test them beforehand. Use indeterminate instruments or agents at first, until the determinate ones declare themselves, proved fit by the Spirit.**
   - "indeterminate instruments must be employed in the beginning, until the determinate shall declare themselves as by the Spirit they are proved fit" (¶40)
   - Also: "Make use of indeterminate agents till thou canst find a determinate one: the many wish well, but one only loves." (¶44, Vaughan); "And prove them before hand, if ye be wise" (¶39, Norton)
   - *stated (¶40 as Atwood's report of Norton; ¶44 Vaughan); interpreted (that ¶40 and ¶44 make the same point; whether "agents" are means or persons, Open questions 14); ATWOOD / Norton / Vaughan.*

9. **Each operation needs a suitable place: dry and sealed from air, dark, very bright, or moist and cold. Wind always harms the work. The work is done "within locks nyne". The place must be secret and still, and free of evil associates and lingering imaginative impressions.**
   - "Therefore for every worke in season Ye must ordaine places by reason." (¶41)
   - Also: "How it should be wrought within locks nyne." (¶41); "Just such a locality as Virgil appointed for his Bees, has been mentioned as desirable with all his appropriate allegorical exceptions of corrupt and evil associates" (¶42); "It is a grace - To finde a fitting working-place." (¶43)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Norton; ATWOOD.* "Locks nyne" and the Virgilian allegory are not decoded.

10. **Secrecy was a principal aim of practitioners, and it was hard to secure along with the other conditions.**
    - "Secrecy having been a principal object with those practicing this Art" (¶42)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

11. **The Preparation: every faculty of the mind must be exercised, because the parts of the Spirit are made one in a renewed harmony and then unfold again to complete their cycle.**
    - "Neither will it therefore be proper to omit any part of this concord, or deny any faculty of the mind its due exercise in the Preparation" (¶28)
    - Also: "these diversified parts of the Spirit are in the renascent harmony made one; thence again to be evolved in catholic procession to complete the equilibriated circle of their Law" (¶28)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

12. **The Gross Work (also called the Preparatory Practice) is preliminary. In Norton's account it is tended continuously, night and day from Evensong to Evensong, except on Sunday, by helpers working in alternating shifts: eight for a reasonable quantity, four for a little. Only a few go beyond it.**
    - "of the many that may be called together, at first, few, it will be understood, are chosen to proceed beyond the exigencies of the preliminary Gross Work" (¶32)
    - Also: "For of this Arte ye shall not have praye But it be ministered as well by night as daye;" (¶37); "Continually, except the holi Sunday alone; From Evensong begin till Evensong be done." (¶37); "Then one half of them must werke Whilethe other half sleepeth or goeth to kerke:" (¶37)
    - *stated (Norton's schedule; few go beyond); interpreted ("the many" = instruments or persons, Open questions 14); ATWOOD; QUOTED: Norton.*

13. **Carry the work strongly from beginning to end despite opposition, and arrange everything in between gently, with grace.**
    - "Attingens a fine usque ad finem fortiter, Disponens omnia suaviter;" (¶17), glossed in the verse as "That is proceede mightily to th’ End From the beginning maugre the Fiend." (¶17)
    - Also: "All things disposing, in the meane space, With great suavity that cometh of grace," (¶17)
    - *stated (the book's own gloss); QUOTED: Norton (endorsed).*

14. **The ultimate Philosophic Work: kindling the Fire is trivial yet holds corruption and generation (Agent 7). The heat must stay within natural proportion and away from extremes.**

## Impediments and dangers

1. **Avarice, "this first and last defect".** Greedy seekers who worked on inert matter without understanding only lost their labour, and Atwood treats them as comparatively harmless. Geber's money-lovers approve the science but never reach the experience of it.
   - "this first and last defect of Avarice" (¶4)
   - Also: "who sought in ignorance, from inert matter, without a ray of light to guide their benighted hopes" (¶4); "yet to the experience of the work they attain not, through covetousness of money" (¶3, Geber); "Ye sons of Avarice and Ignorance, cries Geber, and ye of evil manners, avaunt and fly from this science" (¶18); "For covetous men yt findeth never Though they seek yt once and ever:" (¶21, Pierce)
   - *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Geber; Pierce.*

2. **The gravest failure is to succeed in raising the power and then turn it to selfish ends. Such people defile it and let an evil spirit in where the Divine should be.** Atwood identifies them with the prophet's discoverers of secrets who "are to be judged".
   - "having succeeded in inducing an exalted energy, have willfully denied the Light its true fulfillment, and substituting their own hasty purpose instead of the Divine, defiled it; compelling the Spirit to their private ends" (¶6)
   - Also: "basely entangling the clue of life, climbed by it into forbidden regions of self-sufficiency" (¶4); "who have turned the discovery of nature to an ill account" (¶6); "They have discovered secrets, and these are they who are to be judged" (¶6, the prophet)
   - *interpreted (what the energy, Light and Spirit are); ATWOOD.*

3. **Wickedness keeps the operator from approaching the material.** The Arabian Magician had to use a stolen boy to supply it.
   - "steal a little boy, to go with him to the mountain, in order to supply the material his own wickedness did not suffer him to approach" (¶6)
   - *interpreted; ATWOOD.* See Open questions 1.

4. **Even after the art is gained, pride or covetousness makes it vanish unnoticed. Any wrong against the right of nature makes Wisdom leave.**
   - "this art will vanish from under thy hands, insomuch that thou shalt not know even that thou hadst it" (¶7)
   - Also: "But if anything can be done against the right of nature, she forsakes the polluted tabernacle and is lost" (¶7)
   - *stated; QUOTED: the pious author of the Aquarium (endorsed); ATWOOD.*

5. **Mental defects (Geber):** a stiff neck, a mind clouded by its own fancies ("fumosities"), changeability, beast-like blindness to truth, and disbelief in the science, which the science itself repels.
   - "because there is replete with fumosities, it cannot receive the true intention of natural things" (¶3)
   - Also: "As there be many who have a stiff neck, void of ingenuity and every sort of perscrutination." (¶3); "a soul movable from opinion to opinion, and from will to will" (¶3); "others again, who condemn this science and believe it not to be" (¶3)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Geber.*

6. **Irrational skepticism ("infidelity") rejects anything new without reason and blocks others too.**
   - "by infidelity, we mean that fashionable kind of faithlessness, which, without rational foundation, denounces everything that is new" (¶12)
   - Also: "they push forward as so many stolid bolts before the gate of Truth" (¶13)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

7. **Learned pride: men who trust their own cleverness fail, then call the art a fable.** Those who have never seen it cannot judge it.
   - "Such men presume too much upon their minde, They weene theire wits sufficient this arte to finde;" (¶14)
   - Also: "How should a born blinde man be sure To write or make good portraiture?" (¶14)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Norton (endorsed).*

8. **A busy head, a faithless heart, impure hands and a wandering disposition each bar success.**
   - "neither will a busy head nor a faithless heart, by impure hands, be able; nor does a vagabond inclination enter in by the narrow way of life" (¶15)
   - Also: "opinionative, unstable in purpose, willful and dissimulating" (¶15)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

9. **The covetous spirit is too gross for the work's subtle depth and flees its fiery trial.**

## Principles (general laws of the work)

1. **In Alchemy, unlike ordinary arts, ends become visible in the act itself. The operator's intention returns on him exactly in kind. Subject, object and result agree, and the end is contained in the beginning, as a seed contains its plant.** In ordinary arts, by contrast, the product shows the Idea and not the maker's motive (Holbein).
   - "But in Alchemy, where the nature of things is altogether altered and ultimately reversed, Final Causes are of all things most manifestly revealed" (¶9)
   - Also: "the intention is received back according to its kind most exactly" (¶9); "where the subject, object, and result, through every phase of life agree together, where the end is determinate from the beginning" (¶9); "so in this philosophy the end is implied in the beginning, and the purpose is by the product made manifest" (¶45); "the motive, through the resulting action, by the metaphysical cause, into physical effect" (¶45)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

2. **The Law of Justice: as the agent grows, so does the accountability it lays on whoever wields it. This is why the work is secret, so that only experienced, upright people reach the Power.**
   - "An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth; so does the Law of Justice exact retribution in those spheres" (¶9)
   - Also: "hence so much caution and secrecy, that the Power might only be discovered through the long labour of an experienced and upright mind" (¶9)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

3. **The soul cannot stay neutral. It is filled either by a superior (divine) power or by an inferior (evil) one.**
   - "it must either be filled with a superior or inferior power" (¶6)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

4. **No impurity can enter Wisdom.**
   - "No impure leaven (need we repeat it?) can enter into Wisdom" (¶7)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

5. **Moral, physical and organic laws are one at the root. A violation reaching the root through any of them decays the whole.**
   - "Let the virus but once reach this by either channel, the moral, the physical, or the organic vitally infringed, the whole structure sympathizing decays" (¶8)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

6. **The philosophers' secrecy ("envy") protects. It keeps power from the vicious, and the teacher answers for misuse.**
   - "For this reason are the philosophers said to be envious, declares Hermes, not that they grudged the truth to religious or just men, but to fools, ignorant and vicious" (¶9)
   - Also: "If one evil man had hereof all his will, All Christian peace he might easily spill;" (¶10, Norton)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; Norton (endorsed).*

7. **The science is found only by grace, which is why it is called holy.**
   - "And forasmuch as no man may her find But only by grace, she is holy in her kind." (¶10)
   - Also: "For God’s conjunctions man my not undoe, But if His grace fully consent thereto," (¶10); "For this treasure is not to be bought with money; and as it cannot be bought, so neither can it be sold" (¶18, the royal artist)
   - *stated; QUOTED: Norton; the royal artist (endorsed).*

8. **The records' inner sense is held apart from the literal, and even plain statements are disbelieved. The many books all point to one "Royal Road", but they are useful only once that road is found.**
   - "In almost all the records of Alchemy, the inner sense is held aloof from the literal" (¶16)
   - Also: "all directing on the same Royal Road when this is found; but useless to most wayfarers" (¶15); "not believing, as he quaintly expresses it, that there was any water in our sea" (¶16, Sendivogius)
   - *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Sendivogius.*

9. **Success comes in degrees, and every step taken under reason is progress.**
   - "there are many degrees of success in the legitimate path, and every step is progressive where the Rule of Reason is pursued" (¶11)
   - *stated; ATWOOD.*

10. **Man can only set up the conditions. Blessing, spiritual union and increase come from God. The operator co-operates with God's work of generation and must not hinder it.**
    - "he cannot compel, much less impart, the Divine blessing on his handiwork, neither cement the spiritual union or give it increase" (¶20)
    - Also: "Thou hast resolved with thyself to be a co-operator with the living God, and to minister to Him in his work of generation." (¶44, Vaughan); "the Lesser is blessed by the Greater" (¶20)
    - *stated; ATWOOD; Vaughan.*

11. **Every desire is a kind of prayer. Concentrated desire gathers the mind so that the means suggest themselves. To desire wisdom is to seek to share in the divine, which happens only by willingly becoming like it.**
    - "every desire of the mind is in its kind a prayer and preparative for the acquirement of its object" (¶22)
    - Also: "for when the thoughts are concentered, means and adjuncts suggest themselves" (¶22); "For to desire and covet after wisdom is to seek to be a partaker of that Divinity to which we aspire, and no otherwise can we be made partakers, it is taught, but by a voluntary assimilation" (¶20)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

12. **No sacred operation succeeds without prayer. Prayer enlarges the soul's capacity by enlarging desire.**
    - "No operation, however, in sacred concerns, can succeed without the intervention of prayer." (¶24)
    - Also: "renders, the receptacles of the soul far more capacious (by enlarging the desire) for the communications of the gods" (¶24)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Iamblicus (endorsed; voice of the parenthesis unclear, Open questions 12).*

13. **Love of God holds the strength of the undertaking and the "bond" of whatever is to be loosed.**
    - "I admonish thee to love God, in whom is the strength of thy undertaking and the bond of whatsoever thou meditatest to unloose" (¶20)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Hermes.* What is to be unloosed is not said.

14. **There is no increase of the Spirit without a medium and a bond.**
    - "neither is there any increase of the Spirit, as we have already shown, without a medium and a bond" (¶30)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

15. **Matter by nature reaches toward the Good and takes in as much good as it can.**
    - "even Matter itself is said to be extended to the desirable, i.e., to the Good" (¶28)
    - *stated; voice UNCLEAR.*

16. **Sensible objects attract only through a virtue the idea lends them. Possessed without it, they disappoint.**
    - "the desire of images which lose ultimately the magnetic virtue imparted to them by the idea" (¶26)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

17. **Art and instrument must fully agree.**
    - "Noe man is sure to have his Intent Without full concord of arte with hys instrument." (¶39)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Norton.*

18. **Place affects the result. The same thing produces opposite effects in places opposite under the heavens, as the magnet shows.**
    - "But contrarie wonders be of one thinge, In contrarie countries wrought without leasing;" (¶41)
    - *stated; QUOTED: Norton.*

19. **Higher things dissolve and lower things coagulate, as circumferences spread and centres contract. A day is a contracted year.**
    - "Circumferences spread, but centers contract; so superiors dissolve and inferiors coagulate" (¶44)
    - Also: "every day is a contracted year, and that each year is an extended day. Anticipate the year in the day, and lose not a day in the year." (¶44)
    - *stated (dissolve/coagulate); interpreted (day/year, Open questions 17); QUOTED: Vaughan.*

20. **Reasoned study of effects leads to their causes.**
    - "effects rationally investigated lead into their causes" (¶45)
    - *stated; ATWOOD.*

## Open questions

1. **What is "the material"?** One reading: something within human life itself. Basis: "all men have the material and live by it" (¶2); the agent "Springing directly from ourselves" (¶9); the "sperme owte of a body" (¶21). The other reading: simply something universally available (¶2 alone). This bears on the Arabian Magician (¶6): does the boy fetch the material for him, or is the boy himself its source? ¶2 may favour the second reading, but the chapter does not decide.

2. **Who or what are the Subject (¶29), the "entranced Subject" (¶42), the "spiritual guide" (¶42), the "allied mind" (¶27) and the "one only" who "loves" (¶44)?** One reading: a second person, an entranced partner or assistant working with an operator who acts as guide. Basis: "suitable assistance", "reciprocate the design" (¶29); "two are better than one" and "mutually assist each other" (¶30); "entranced Subject" paired with "spiritual guide" and "communion" (¶42); Mesmerism as the Door-keeper to antique science (¶13); "Avoid the multitude, as well of passions as of persons." next to "one only loves" (¶44). The other reading: the material or patient (marble, ¶29), and a single mind whose faculties are brought into concord (¶28 "deny any faculty of the mind its due exercise"; "these diversified parts of the Spirit are in the renascent harmony made one"). The chapter never says that these terms refer to the same thing.

3. **Is Mesmerism part of the method or only Atwood's personal way in?** "gave us first introduction to the vestibule" (¶13) supports the second. "offer a new guide to knowledge and felicity" (¶13), and the entranced Subject and imaginative impressures (¶42), are compatible with the first.

4. **"Subject soul" (¶6):** the soul oppressed by the abuser's evil, or the soul as the subject of the work, defiled by misuse? Basis for the second: "the subject, object, and result" (¶9); "defile the divine part of their essence" (¶6).

5. **"a second to a third of concentration" (¶9):** successive degrees of intensifying the agent, or passages through a second and a third subject or operation? Related questions: is the Conjunction of ¶9 the same as "in conjunction" in ¶20, which may be a stage of the practical work ("a period") or the "spiritual union" just mentioned? And is "the Power" (¶9) the agent or the whole art?

6. **"Wisdom’s fiery ordeal" and "vaporous essence in Her pure Light" (¶11):** a moral test, or an actual fiery stage of the work? The chapter says only that the covetous spirit is too gross to enter it.

7. **"the Light" (¶6, ¶15):** an experience reached within the work ("living experience", ¶15; "Her pure Light", ¶11), or understanding of the abstruse doctrine ("the abstruse light of Alchemy", ¶16)?

8. **Eirenaeus "in the outward application" (¶16):** does this imply an inner, operative meaning that Atwood leaves unstated (compare "the inner sense is held aloof from the literal", ¶16), or does it simply mean applying the story to how the public receives the art?

9. **"the polluted tabernacle" (¶7):** the operator's own person (¶6 "divine part of their essence"), or the vessel or matter of the work (¶7 "man’s finite hand")?

10. **"That, which in the hands of a philosopher becomes most precious" (¶18):** the knowledge, or the matter transformed by philosophic handling?

11. **The Rational Light and the "siege of life" (¶19).** One reading: the student's reason freeing the meaning from the emblems. The other: an operative agent, "in practice strengthening", raised into life to overcome evil there. "Siege" may mean a seat ("raised to") or an assault ("overcome the stronghold"). The text grammatically identifies the "she" who is delivered with the one spirit and intention of the writings, but the second half of the sentence describes a process.

12. **Voice boundaries.** Does Kirchringius's quotation run from "they prayed, says Kirchringius" to the end of ¶22, or is the riddle passage Atwood's own? Is "(by enlarging the desire)" (¶24) Atwood's gloss on Iamblicus? Is "(that is of the ultimate Philosophic Work)" (¶44) Atwood's or Vaughan's? There are no quotation marks in any of these places.

13. **The Philosophic Vessel (¶30–¶32).** Is it partly a living body? Basis: "nerves, veins, and alembics"; "The Spirit finally constructs its own vessel and vitrifies it". Or is it laboratory ware? Basis: Atwood calls the literature "sophistication"; Norton's account of glass, clay and stone (¶31); Charnocke's potter (¶33).

14. **"Indeterminate" and "determinate" (¶40, ¶44); "of the many that may be called together, at first, few, it will be understood, are chosen" (¶32).** Are these instruments and agents in the operation, or persons? The case for instruments: ¶32 follows straight on from the choice of instruments, and ¶40 names instruments outright. The case for persons: "called together"/"chosen", the surrounding paragraphs on assistants and ministers, and "the many wish well, but one only loves" (¶44).

15. **Labour (¶37 vs ¶38).** Norton's eight ministers working night and day may belong to the Gross Work only (¶36 "about the Gross Work"; ¶32 "preliminary Gross Work"), with Vaughan's ease ("no way troublesome") applying to a later work. Or Atwood may simply be placing disagreeing authorities side by side without reconciling them.

16. **"heat" (¶44):** literal heat in an operation (fire, moist natures, central fire), or the operator's vehemence ("thy violent destructive Hands", "a few violent knocks", "not destructive to thyself")?

17. **"every day is a contracted year" (¶44):** a rule of diligence ("lose not a day in the year"), or a correspondence within the process, a short cycle reproducing the long one ("Anticipate the year in the day")? The second is favoured by the parallel "centers contract" / "Circumferences spread".

18. **Pierce's "Leave worke, take your beades and pray" (¶21):** give up labour for prayer alone, as the words literally say? Or add prayer to labour, as Sendivogius's "pray; but work", the Lucerna Salis's "mediate dispositions" and Kirchringius's "tempting of God" (¶20, ¶22) suggest?

19. **Undecoded keys the chapter names but does not explain:** Causal Light (¶2); clue of life (¶4); Wheel of Life (¶9); the Radix (¶3); Dagon (¶20; probably ignorance, from its position in the sentence); the bond and the "unloosing" (¶20); the Water of the Wood (¶21); the Living Temple (¶27); the vessel of nature (¶31); the planks of Argo and the "self-same ground" (¶32); locks nyne (¶41); Virgil's Bees (¶42); communion (¶42); the Door (¶44); complexions (¶44); the Master Key (¶47); Portus Explicit (¶48, our translation only).
