# Method material — I.1 A Preliminary Account of the Hermetic Philosophy, with the more Salient Points of its Public History

The chapter is a history of the art. Most of its method material comes from authorities Atwood quotes or reports: the Smaragdine Table, Norton, Flamel, Artephius, Sendivogius, Ripley, Bacon, Agrippa, Khunrath, Paracelsus, Van Helmont. Her own statements mostly concern the operator's motives, the deliberate veiling of the texts, and the rule that theory comes before practice. She defers explaining the subject and the "conditions of success" to later chapters (I.1 ¶24, ¶39, ¶86).

## The subject (what the work is done on)

1. **The subject is one thing.** The Table names a single "One Thing" from which all things arose "by adaptation", calling it the father of all perfection. Agrippa likewise speaks of one stone, "besides which there is no other thing", as the subject of the stone. Neither passage says what the thing is. Atwood says only that the Table "is said to comprehend the working principle and total subject of the art".
   - "as all things were from one, by the mediation of one, so all things arose from this one thing by adaptation" (I.1 ¶9)
   - also: "for performing the miracles of the One Thing" (I.1 ¶9); "This is the father of all perfection, or consummation of the whole world." (I.1 ¶9); "of the one blessed stone alone, besides which there is no other thing, the subject of the most holy stone of the philosophers" (I.1 ¶64); "is said to comprehend the working principle and total subject of the art" (I.1 ¶7)
   - *interpreted* (that the One Thing is the subject: ¶7 + ¶9). Voices: QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table); QUOTED: Cornelius Agrippa. ATWOOD for ¶7, which she reports as "said".

2. **The subject is universal and paradoxical.** The Bononian epitaphs describe something that is neither man, woman nor androgyne "but all", lies nowhere in particular "but everywhere", and is both corpse and tomb. The alchemists say these contradictions describe the properties of their universal subject. Atwood accepts the reading and promises to explain it later.
   - "All these contradictory claims are said by the alchemists to relate to the properties of their universal subject, as we shall hereafter endeavor to explain" (I.1 ¶24)
   - also: "the adepts claim it as having exclusive reference to the occult material of their art" (I.1 ¶20); "Not in heaven, earth, or water broad It lies, but everywhere!" (I.1 ¶23); "Both corpse and sepulcher the same!" (I.1 ¶23); "Nec vir, nec mulier, nec androgyna" (I.1 ¶21)
   - *stated* (the relation to the subject is stated; the properties themselves are not explained here). Voices: QUOTED: the alchemists / adepts; Atwood endorses ("as we shall hereafter endeavor to explain").

3. **The subject is not an ordinary chemical substance.** The philosophers did not experiment on common metals, salts, sulphurs or mercuries but "in a far different nature". Books that put the truth in salts, niters, boraxes or plants are deceptive. Ripley worked on metals, salts, vitriol, arsenic, urine, eggs, hair and blood and got nothing from any of them.
   - "Not with our gross elements, our mercuries, sulfurs, and our lifeless salts; but in a far different nature, with stranger arts" (I.1 ¶33)
   - also: "some indeed affirming, that the truth was to be found in salts, or niters, or boraxes; but others, in all vegetable bodies indiscriminately" (I.1 ¶53); "I proved uryns, eggs, here, and blod," (I.1 ¶65); "But profytt found I right none therein." (I.1 ¶65); "left mankind to imagine, as they did, all that was erroneous concerning it, as of their salts, sulfurs, mercuries, magic elements, and occult confections" (I.1 ¶82)
   - *stated*. ATWOOD (¶33, ¶53, ¶82); QUOTED: Ripley (¶65), used by Atwood as an example of error.

4. **The subject is always concealed.** Even the most candid author (Norton) hides the Subject Matter and certain preliminaries. No adept ever openly declared even the "common Subject".
   - "with the exception of the Subject Matter and certain preliminaries, which are constantly concealed" (I.1 ¶64)
   - also: "they would not, not have they anywhere openly declared, even the common Subject of their Art" (I.1 ¶82)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

5. **Some authors point to it.** Albert describes "the first matter of the adepts" from personal observation. Boehme's works are "full of explicit indications" about the occult material of the stone. Neither passage identifies it.
   - "full of explicit indications concerning the physical basis of magic and occult material of the philosophers’ stone" (I.1 ¶68)
   - also: "the first matter of the adepts" (I.1 ¶39)
   - *stated* (that these authors indicate it); ATWOOD.

6. **Having the first matter is not the same as knowing how to prepare it.** Flamel had the prima materia, "the first principles". He lacked their preparation, which he calls the hardest thing of all.
   - "I had now the prima material, the first principles, yet not their preparation, which is a thing most difficult above all things in the world" (I.1 ¶57)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel. Atwood presents his account without dissent.

7. **The divine water.** There is a "celestial, or rather a divine water", distinct from every other water. It is a spirit of the nature of the ether, and potable gold and the stone take their beginning from it. Its ether burns perpetually, without being consumed, if outside air is kept from it (see Principles 7).
   - "There is a celestial, or rather a divine water of the chemists, with which both Democritus and Trismegistus were acquainted" (I.1 ¶20)
   - also: "which is a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things; whence potable gold, and the stone of philosophers, takes its beginning" (I.1 ¶20); "a particular kind that is distinct from every other water or liquor" (I.1 ¶20)
   - *stated* (the gloss is in the text). QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus, cited by Atwood without dissent. Whether this water is the subject, the agent or both is not said (see Open questions).

## The agent (what does the work: fire, spirit, light, intellect…)

1. **The parents of the One Thing.** The Table calls the Sun its father and the Moon its mother; the wind carries it and the Earth nurses it. None of these names is explained in this chapter.
   - "the father of it is the Sun, the mother of it is the Moon; the wind carries it in its belly; the nurse thereof is the Earth" (I.1 ¶9)
   - *interpreted* (that these name generating principles); QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).

2. **The universal spirit of nature, which ascends and descends.** Bacon drew ("fill his lamp") on a "reviviscent spirit" and worked with it "ascending and descending experimentally". Working with "the universal spirit of nature", he proved that light can be multiplied and produced gold. One of Vaughan's treatises has as its subject the universal spirit of nature, with its "ascent and descent". The Table uses the same ascend/descend language (Procedure 3). What the spirit is, the chapter does not say.
   - "fill his lamp from her reviviscent spirit; and with this ascending and descending experimentally" (I.1 ¶38)
   - also: "miraculous multiplicability of light by the universal spirit of nature" (I.1 ¶38); "the universal spirit of nature, with its strange, abstruse, and miraculous ascent and descent" (I.1 ¶74); "that secret fountain whose water flows from fire" (I.1 ¶74)
   - *interpreted*; ATWOOD. The "her" in ¶38 refers back to "our Phoenix" (¶37); see Open questions.

3. **The fire of nature and nature's central source.** The medieval "Magians" were led by the fire of nature into contact with nature's central source.
   - "drawn about the fire of nature, as it were, into communication with her central source" (I.1 ¶39)
   - *interpreted* (figurative, marked "as it were"); ATWOOD.

4. **Light.** The perpetual lamps show knowledge of "the occult laws of light". The best of them burned for an estimated 1500 years or more on "a very pure liquor" held in gold and silver vessels.
   - "the Romans understood something of chemistry and the occult laws of light" (I.1 ¶18)
   - also: "a very pure liquor, by whose virtue it is probable these lamps had continued to shine for upwards of fifteen hundred years" (I.1 ¶18); "some superior skill in adjusting the gaseous elements, or other ethereal adaptations than is known at this day" (I.1 ¶18)
   - *stated* (as Atwood's judgment of the lamps); ATWOOD.

5. **The tincture acts by an inner astral fire that turns other things into its own nature.**
   - "has within it an astral fire which conquers all things and changes them into a nature like itself" (I.1 ¶76)
   - also: "by its transmuting force all imperfect metals are changed" (I.1 ¶76)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Paracelsus. Atwood offers it as testimony.

6. **Azoth, the Universal Mercury, is enough by itself.** With its own internal and external fire it suffices, and fire transforms it into whatever it will.
   - "Azoth, viz., the Universal Mercury, which alone with its internal and external fire is sufficient for thee" (I.1 ¶81)
   - also: "which transforms itself into what it will by the fire" (I.1 ¶81)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: H. Khunrath. Atwood offers it as testimony.

7. **A dissolvent known only to philosophers.** Cleopatra dissolved her earring in a "vinegar" that is no common vinegar. The chapter does not explain it.
   - "such a sharp vinegar as is only known to philosophers on the ground of nature" (I.1 ¶18)
   - *interpreted* (its dissolving role is inferred from the anecdote); ATWOOD.

8. **An original virtue behind the sacramental signs.** The Church's types (water, oil, salt, light) work only through understanding of, and cooperation with, an "original virtue". Without it they merely imitate an effect. The virtue is not identified.
   - "but from a true understanding and cooperation of that original virtue, apart from which they do but mimic an efficacy, and gather unwholesome fruits" (I.1 ¶26)
   - *interpreted*; ATWOOD.

9. **Divine grace.** Flamel attributes his knowledge to the grace of God. Artephius attributes his long life to God's grace together with the quintessence.
   - "through the grace of God it was, that I attained the perfect knowledge of all that I desired" (I.1 ¶57)
   - also: "by the blessing of God, I have not wanted an understanding of the books of the philosophers" (I.1 ¶56); "by the grace of God alone, and the use of this admirable quintessence" (I.1 ¶36)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel; QUOTED: Artephius.

## The operator (requirements: knowledge, moral state, will, guidance, secrecy…)

1. **Seek wisdom for its own sake, not for gold.** Those who seek gain never find the stone. Wisdom teaches only philosophers to make gold. Success has "peculiar" conditions, to be shown later.
   - "Wisdom must be sought for her own sake, neither for gold or silver or any intermediate benefit, lest these all should be denied together without the discovery of their source." (I.1 ¶17)
   - also: "It is philosophers only that she teaches to make gold." (I.1 ¶39); "Avarice is of all motives the least likely to be gratified by the discovery of wisdom." (I.1 ¶39); "The conditions of success are peculiar, as will be shown." (I.1 ¶39)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

2. **Pure, moral and pious motives mark the true adepts.**
   - "the motives actuating them were of the purest possible kind; truthful, moral, always pious and intelligent" (I.1 ¶41)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

3. **Real powers come from holiness of life and well-grounded faith.**
   - "the powers which sanctify of life and a well-grounded faith had gotten her" (I.1 ¶26)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD. (Its negative, power sought without conscience, is Impediment 2.)

4. **Faith and freedom of thought beyond things seen.** Ordinary minds stop at limits that great minds pass almost without noticing.
   - "Faith was the beacon light that led them on to conviction, by a free perspicuity of thought beyond things seen" (I.1 ¶39)
   - also: "Medium minds set limits to nature, halting continually, and returning, before barriers which those others over-leaped almost without perceiving them" (I.1 ¶39)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

5. **Learning and wisdom.** Only a great scholar should attempt the work, because it is "most profounde philosophye". The art is for the wise.
   - "For trewly he that is not a great clerke, Is nice and lewd to medle with this werke;" (I.1 ¶52)
   - also: "this Art is for the wise, not for the ignorant" (I.1 ¶84)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Norton's Ordinal (endorsed by Atwood); QUOTED: Sendivogius (endorsed).

6. **Ability, aspiration and labour.** The adepts opened the inner knowledge only to those able to aspire and willing to labour. The White Stone was won by "patient and wise" men, truly taught, through great labour. Bacon's results were fruits of labour and "long interior study". Geber credits God's prompting together with his own unceasing work.
   - "disclosing the interior lights of science to such as were able to aspire, and willing to follow their great example, laboring in the way" (I.1 ¶39)
   - also: "After that they were trewly taught, With great labor, that stone they caught," (I.1 ¶60); "final fruits of labor and long interior study" (I.1 ¶38); "By the goodness of God’s instigation and by our own incessant labor" (I.1 ¶76); "according as we found them by experiment and reason" (I.1 ¶76)
   - *stated*. ATWOOD (¶38, ¶39); QUOTED: Norton; QUOTED: Geber.

7. **A teacher, or God, is needed for one point.** Artephius withholds one thing that only God or a master can truly reveal, though the teachable may learn it from his book. Aquinas gives the decisive instruction by mouth. Flamel understood his book's figures only when a stranger explained them. Cremer, misled for thirty years, sought out Lully. Agrippa received the principles from Trithemius. Morienus became the disciple of the adept Adfar.
   - "I except one thing only, which it is not lawful that I should write, because it can be revealed truly only by God, or by a master" (I.1 ¶36)
   - also: "Fac sicut te ore tenens docui, ut scis quod tibi non scribo" (I.1 ¶42; ours: "Do as I taught you by mouth, as you know I do not write it to you"); "their meaning was explained to him by a Jew stranger" (I.1 ¶57); "Desirous of becoming still more intimately enlightened than was convenient in that place" (I.1 ¶46); "having possessed himself of the means and principles of the Occult Science from his friend, the wary and learned Abbot Trithemius, set to work" (I.1 ¶64)
   - *stated* (Artephius). The Aquinas sense rests on our translation. QUOTED: Artephius; QUOTED: Aquinas (endorsed); ATWOOD for the narratives.

8. **Teachability and some experience.** Artephius' withheld point can be learned from his book by one who is not stubborn and has a little experience.
   - "provided one be not stiff- necked, and have a little experience" (I.1 ¶36)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Artephius.

9. **Prove what you receive by your own practice; do not teach before full experience.** Agrippa tested his received principles "in a self-sufficient order". His early writings predate full experience and the stone. Ashmole will not speak without attested practice.
   - "without the confident attestations of practice" (I.1 ¶62)
   - also: "I know enough to hold my tongue, but not enough to speak" (I.1 ¶62); "proving them in a self-sufficient order" (I.1 ¶64); "he had not, when he wrote them, arrived at a full experience, nor was he able to make the philosophers’ stone" (I.1 ¶64)
   - *stated*. QUOTED: Ashmole; QUOTED: Cornelius Agrippa ("as he declares"); ATWOOD (¶64 narrative).

10. **A kindred light, the "true Light".** A person sees what the philosophers really did only with a light akin to their own intellect. Those who practised without "the true Light" failed. Bacon promises that one who grasps his meaning will find, "being a lighted candle". None of these lights is defined.
    - "they would observe the philosophers themselves, by a kindred light made visible, on their own ground" (I.1 ¶33)
    - also: "had they one ray even of the antique intellect they deride" (I.1 ¶33); "they who have thus fallen to practice, without the true Light or heeding their injunctions" (I.1 ¶86); "apprehend my meaning, and you will find, believe me, being a lighted candle" (I.1 ¶86)
    - *interpreted*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Roger Bacon.

11. **Preparation and approval before initiation.** Synesius and his brethren swore to initiate only those who were worthily prepared and approved by the whole conclave.
    - "to initiate none but such as had been worthily prepared and duly approved by the whole conclave" (I.1 ¶27)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD.

12. **Secrecy and godly use.** Adepts are bound by oath to silence. Ripley would reveal the whole work to his king only if the king kept it secret and used it only as God pleased. Aquinas enjoins guarding one's mouth.
    - "That to yourself ye shall keep yt secretly; And only yt use, as may be God’s pleasure," (I.1 ¶80)
    - also: "I could tell many tings of this art, if I had not sworn to keep silence" (I.1 ¶64); "ne sis garrulous sed pone ori tuo custodiam" (I.1 ¶42); "Els in tyme comynge of God I shoulde abye For my discoveringe of hys secret treasurye" (I.1 ¶80)
    - *stated*. QUOTED: Ripley; QUOTED: Cornelius Agrippa (Atwood: "he was fettered by oaths"); QUOTED: Aquinas.

13. **Sobriety and prudence (Flamel's household).** Perenelle was sober, chaste, wise and prudent, and kept the secret. The couple gave themselves to charity. The text presents these as their qualities, not explicitly as requirements.
    - "a sober chaste wife; but she was also a wise prudent woman" (I.1 ¶57)
    - also: "lest Perenelle, by reason of extreme joy, should not hide her felicity" (I.1 ¶57)
    - *interpreted* (as a requirement); QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

14. **A woman can work the art fully.** Perenelle understood it as well as Flamel and could have perfected it alone.
    - "I have made three times with the help of Perenelle, who understands it as well as myself" (I.1 ¶57)
    - also: "if she would have done it alone, she would have brought the work to the same, or full as great perfection as I had done" (I.1 ¶57)
    - *stated*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

## The procedure and its stages (in the order the chapter gives, if it gives one)

The chapter gives no single ordered procedure. Two partial sequences appear: the Table (items 1–3) and Flamel's narrative together with Norton (items 6–9).

1. **Turn it into earth.** The power of the One Thing becomes complete ("Integral") when it is turned into earth. Not explained.
   - "The power of it is Integral, if it be turned into earth." (I.1 ¶9)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).

2. **Separate, gently and with judgment.** Earth is separated from fire, and subtle from gross. Which term corresponds to which is not said.
   - "Thou shalt separate the earth from the fire, the subtle from the gross, gently with much sagacity" (I.1 ¶9)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).

3. **Circulation: ascent and descent.** The thing rises from earth to heaven and comes down again, taking in the strength of the higher and the lower. This gives "the glory of the whole world". Bacon's working of the spirit "ascending and descending experimentally" (Agent 2) uses the same terms. By it he is said to have found "the secret ligature of natures, and their magical dissolution".
   - "it ascends from earth to heaven, and again descends to earth: and receives the strength of the superiors and of the inferiors" (I.1 ¶9)
   - also: "so thou hast the glory of the whole world; therefore let all obscurity flee before thee" (I.1 ¶9); "the secret ligature of natures, and their magical dissolution" (I.1 ¶38)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table); ATWOOD (¶38, reported as "is said").

4. **Digestion.** The lamp inscription says the elements were "Digested With laborious art" and sealed in a small vessel. Not explained.
   - "Namque elementa gravi clausit digesta labore" (I.1 ¶19; book: "The elements has chained. Digested With laborious art, From secret science gained.")
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: inscription of Maximus Olybius.

5. **A self-contained "laboratory" and a manual technique.** The philosophers' laboratory is "compact in one": vessels, fuel, furnaces and material united. Ashmole alludes to a "manual artifice" fairly openly. Neither is described.
   - "most complete with vessels, fuel, furnaces, and every material requisite, well adapted together and compact in one" (I.1 ¶33)
   - also: "alludes to the manual artifice without much disguise" (I.1 ¶62)
   - *interpreted*; ATWOOD.

6. **Finding the preparation of the first principles: the key step.** Flamel spent three years in continual prayer, close reading and pondering of the philosophers' words, and trial of the operations they might mean.
   - "praying also continuously unto God, and reading attentively in my book, pondering the words of the philosophers, and then trying and proving the various operations which I thought they might mean by their words" (I.1 ¶57)
   - also: "after a long aberration and wandering in the labyrinth of errors, for the space of three years" (I.1 ¶57)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

7. **After that, the magistery follows the written directions.** Once the preparation of the "prime agents" is known, following the book literally cannot fail.
   - "For knowing the preparations of the prime agents, and then literally following the directions in my book, I could not then miss the work if I would." (I.1 ¶57)
   - also: "Having this, I easily accomplished the magistery." (I.1 ¶57); "Again following the same direction in my book, word by word" (I.1 ¶57)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

8. **White before Red, then multiplication.** The white medicine must be fully made before the Red Stone is sought. The red work is hard and rare; the adepts credit Basil Valentine with an easier method for it. A further "multiplication" increases the Red Stone; Norton says even Albertus and Bacon did not understand it.
   - "Wheom to seeke it availeth right noughte, Till the white medicine be fully wrought;" (I.1 ¶60)
   - also: "Found our White Stone with exercise;" (I.1 ¶60); "Him to increase in multiplication" (I.1 ¶60); "discovered a new method of working the Red Elixir, and facilitated the process materially, which had been hitherto laborious and a rare effect" (I.1 ¶59)
   - *stated* (the order white before red); *interpreted* (multiplication, unexplained). QUOTED: Norton; QUOTED: Basil Valentine's brethren ("as they say"), reported by Atwood.

9. **Projection.** A tiny amount of the finished medicine is cast onto a base metal, usually mercury, which turns into silver or gold. Flamel's first projection gave silver; his projection of the Red Stone gave gold. Van Helmont cast a quarter grain onto eight ounces of boiling quicksilver. In Ashmole's account, a piece of a pan was merely warmed, not melted. The stated potencies are 1 to 19,186, 1 to 120,000 and 1 to 272,330. Misjudging the medicine's strength wastes it.
   - "projected the fourth part of one grain, wrapped in paper, upon eight ounces of quicksilver boiling in a crucible" (I.1 ¶76)
   - also: "the first time I made projection, was upon mercury; a pound and a half whereof, or thereabouts, I turned into pure silver, better than that of the mine" (I.1 ¶57); "I made projection of the Red Stone, on a like quantity of mercury" (I.1 ¶57); "the elixir being projected thereon, it was transmuted into pure silver" (I.1 ¶66); "being 1 upon 272,330" (I.1 ¶66); "they lost a great portion in trial before they found out the true height of the medicine" (I.1 ¶66)
   - *stated* (as narrated). QUOTED: Van Helmont; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel; QUOTED: Ashmole; ATWOOD (¶66).

## Signs (what shows the work is proceeding)

1. **Scent.** Flamel recognized what he had sought by its scent. The text does not settle which product (see Open questions).
   - "At length, I found that which I desired; which I also soon knew, by the scent and odor thereof." (I.1 ¶57)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

2. **Marks of the finished tincture.** It is deep red (saffron to ruby) and very heavy. It is fixed and unchanging, yet "liquid like resin, transparent like crystal, frangible like glass".
   - "It is of a ruby color of the greatest weight; and this is a true sign of the tincture of the philosophers" (I.1 ¶76)
   - also: "a powder having the reddest color, almost like saffron" (I.1 ¶76); "liquid like resin, transparent like crystal, frangible like glass" (I.1 ¶76); "it is a most fixed substance and immutable in the multiplication" (I.1 ¶76)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Paracelsus.

3. **Projection taking effect.** The boiling quicksilver makes a small sound, stops flowing, and sets like yellow wax.
   - "the quicksilver, with a small voice, presently stood in its flux, and was congealed like to yellow wax" (I.1 ¶76)
   - *stated* (as narrated); QUOTED: Van Helmont.

4. **False colours.** Red and white tinctures made from common materials are untrue. Montebanks deceived the world with their "Whites and Reds".
   - "Of which gey tinctures I made to shew, Both red and white which were untrew." (I.1 ¶65)
   - also: "have with their Whites and Reds deceived almost the whole world" (I.1 ¶84)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Ripley; QUOTED: Sendivogius.

5. **The final proof is tangible.** Transmutation is evidence addressed to the senses: one handles and sees the thing.
   - "the transmutation of metallic bodies was a proof addressed to the senses" (I.1 ¶76)
   - also: "with thine own hands thou shalt handle and with thine own eyes thou shalt see Azoth" (I.1 ¶81)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: H. Khunrath.

6. **Lavish spending marks a possessor who found the stone by chance, never one who made it.**
   - "This kind of profusion has been frequently exhibited by such as are reported to have come by the treasure casually, never by those who have themselves confected it." (I.1 ¶66)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

## Impediments and dangers

1. **Exposing the mysteries to the multitude.** Sacred mysteries exposed too openly became perverted. Egypt fell when she neglected the law by which she governed and held off the profane.
   - "sacred mysteries, too openly exposed to the multitude, became perverted and vilified by their abuse" (I.1 ¶11)
   - also: "holding in strong abeyance the ignorant, profane, vulgar, until the evil day of desolation came with self-abuse, when, neglecting to obey the law, by which she governed, all fell" (I.1 ¶10)
   - *interpreted* (that "sacred mysteries" means the Art); ATWOOD.

2. **Power sought without conscience.** Priests who abandoned conscience tried to use magic power; confusion followed and the Mysteries were abandoned.
   - "having forsaken the law of conscience, attempted to wield without it the rod of magic power" (I.1 ¶30)
   - *interpreted* (conscience as precondition); ATWOOD.

3. **Two errors about signs.** One error treats the material sign as holy in itself (idolatry). The other discards signs as superstition. Both lose the thing signified.
   - "by placing inherent holiness in those material signs, apart from the spirit and only thing signified" (I.1 ¶26)
   - also: "mistaking these things for superstitions" (I.1 ¶26)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

4. **Deliberately obscure and disordered texts.** The masters wrote "under coverts", and readers lose their money. Texts are put out of order, padded with repetition, and each master gives only a part. Some were scrambled by the cabalistic method. Some lead on the initiated but disgust beginners. Obscurity is why men failed to perfect the magistery, and Cremer was misled for thirty years. (For the reasons behind the veiling, see Principles 11–13.)
   - "the Hermetic discourses are not infrequently found introverted in their order, and dispersed with repetitions, to prevent the truth from becoming openly obvious" (I.1 ¶82)
   - also: "Wrote under coverts and Aristotle alsoe." (I.1 ¶15); "They leefe their costs, as men see alle daye." (I.1 ¶15); "Every and each of them disclosed but a parte" (I.1 ¶83); "spoliation and displacement from the order in which they were originally written, according to the mischievous cabalistic method in vogue at that time" (I.1 ¶64); "may serve to lead on the initiated, yet will appear infamously sophistical and inevitably disgust a beginner" (I.1 ¶64); "that men have been unable to perfect the same magistery on account of the obscurity of the words of the philosophers" (I.1 ¶36); "The enigmas of the old adepts had sadly perplexed and led him astray" (I.1 ¶46)
   - *stated*. ATWOOD; QUOTED: Norton; QUOTED: Artephius.

5. **False and corrupted books.** Pretenders who did not know even the preliminaries wrote deceptive books. Old masters' texts were mutilated. Mercenaries spread recipes "for lucre". Most of Paracelsus' writings mislead on purpose. The adepts charge that Aristotle led people away from true experiment; Atwood doubts this was deliberate. Ripley was "dysceivyd with many falce books". Error comes more from sophists than from the genuine tradition.
   - "introduce mutilated editions of the old masters, filled with inconsistencies, and the wicked inventions of designing fraud" (I.1 ¶53)
   - also: "Many usurped the title of the adepts, who had no knowledge even of the preliminaries of the Art" (I.1 ¶53); "a difficulty yet remains to distinguish them, and segregate, from so great an interspersion of darkness, the true light" (I.1 ¶53); "wrote and practiced for lucre, leading mankind into error and the commitment even of egregious crimes by their receipts" (I.1 ¶64); "leading them far away, through alluring sophisms, from the straight way of truth" (I.1 ¶64); "And oft times I was dysceivyd with many falce books" (I.1 ¶65); "more frequently owing to the deceit of sophists than to the genuine tradition of Hermetic science" (I.1 ¶86); "led mankind astray from the path of true experiment" (I.1 ¶16)
   - *stated*. ATWOOD; QUOTED: Ripley; QUOTED: the adepts (¶16, qualified by Atwood).

6. **Literal practice with lifeless apparatus.** Readers who took Geber's, Basil's and Glauber's recipes literally used stills, coals and furnaces, and salt, sulphur, mercury and antimony. They hoped to sublime the Spirit of Nature or extract the Form of gold. Atwood blames their failure on their own misunderstanding, not on the adepts.
   - "surrounding many a literal soul with stills, coals, and furnaces, in hope by such lifeless instruments to sublime the Spirit of Nature" (I.1 ¶86)
   - also: "or by salt, sulfur and mercury, or the three combined with antimony, to extract the Form of gold" (I.1 ¶86); "Thus I rostyd and boyled as one of Geber’s cooks," (I.1 ¶65)
   - *interpreted* (that lifeless apparatus cannot reach the goal); ATWOOD; QUOTED: Ripley.

7. **Avarice, haste and vague, selfish desire.** Hasty belief wastes the seeker's means in lifelong hope. Starkey wasted his master's medicine through ignorance and covetousness. Men stay ignorant chiefly because their desires are vague and selfish.
   - "on account mainly of the indefiniteness and selfishness of their desires" (I.1 ¶64)
   - also: "But manie Artificers have byn over swifte, With hastie credence to sume away their thrift;" (I.1 ¶52); "the spirit of avarice, bursting forth expectant, absolutely raged" (I.1 ¶49); "with this treasure I went to work ignorantly and was caught in the trap of my own covetousness" (I.1 ¶73)
   - *stated*. ATWOOD (¶49, ¶64; the ¶64 sentence follows a remark of Ashmole's); QUOTED: Norton; QUOTED: Starkey.

8. **Danger to the operator: curiosity and forbidden self-sufficiency.** Bacon's curiosity reportedly led him into "forbidden realms of self-sufficiency and unlawful peace of mind". His conscience told him to withhold a gift obtained too rashly and dangerously, and he gave up the research to make amends.
   - "led him by a fatal curiosity, as it is suggested, into forbidden realms of self-sufficiency and unlawful peace of mind" (I.1 ¶38)
   - also: "a gift somewhat over rashly and dangerously obtained" (I.1 ¶38)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Roger Bacon ("as he declares") and an unnamed report ("as it is suggested"), relayed by Atwood without dissent.

9. **Danger to the operator: the elixir itself.** Vaughan reportedly died of an overdose of the elixir, his "subtle light of life" swallowed by "a greater flame". Agrippa tells a similar story of Alexander's preceptor. For Paracelsus' early death, a cited view holds that intemperance is especially fatal to one taking "a potent spiritual medicine", which heightens the effects of vice. The operator's way of life therefore matters.
   - "the subtle light of life should in these instances have been swallowed up in the superior attraction of a greater flame" (I.1 ¶74)
   - also: "an overdose of the elixir" (I.1 ¶74); "administering the venom of the waters of the Styx" (I.1 ¶74); "a potent spiritual medicine, which would heighten the spiritual consequences of depravity and habitual excess, and accelerate dissolution in the conflict of opposite principles" (I.1 ¶64)
   - *interpreted*. UNCLEAR voice: ¶74 is given "as the narrative runs"; ¶64 is a view that "has also been considered".

10. **Persecution from outside.** A public that could not tell adepts from frauds exiled, arrested and tortured them. Adepts therefore hid their names and the evidence of the art.
    - "even the true adepts --- for the public knew not to distinguish --- suffered equal cruelty and abundant inconvenience" (I.1 ¶66)
    - also: "I am constrained even from the works of mercy, for fear of suspicion and arrest" (I.1 ¶66)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Alexander Seton / Philalethes (see Open questions).

## The result

1. **The completed thing is the strongest power.** It overcomes every subtle thing and penetrates every solid. It is "the glory of the whole world".
   - "This is the strong fortitude of all fortitudes, overcoming every subtle and penetrating every solid thing." (I.1 ¶9)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).

2. **The three kingdoms can be perfected to a quintessence.** This is Hermes' reputed discovery, and the reason for his title "Thrice Great". The kingdoms are not named.
   - "the quintessential perfectibility of the three kingdoms in their homogeneal unity" (I.1 ¶6)
   - also: "whence he is called the Thrice Great Hermes, having the spiritual intelligence of all things in their universal law" (I.1 ¶6)
   - *interpreted*; ATWOOD.

3. **Transmutation of metals into silver and gold better than mined gold.** Witnesses report gold and silver made by Flamel, Lully, Arnold, Van Helmont and others. Artificial gems are also credited (Democritus; St John). The White medicine gives silver and the Red Stone gives gold.
   - "This mercury I truly transmuted into almost as much gold, much better indeed than common gold, more soft also, and more pliable." (I.1 ¶57)
   - also: "this gold is better than the gold of the mines" (I.1 ¶76); "he gave me upwards of two ounces of the White medicine of sufficient virtue to convert 120,000 times its weight into the purest silver" (I.1 ¶73); "Seneca also mentions his artificial confection of precious stones" (I.1 ¶12); "not only in healing the sick, but also confecting gold, silver and precious stones for their benefit" (I.1 ¶24)
   - *stated* (as testimony). QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel; QUOTED: Paracelsus; QUOTED: Starkey; ATWOOD.

4. **A medicine.** It heals the sick, including cases the physicians had given up on. Potable gold and the stone begin from the divine water. Artephius claims a life of a thousand years by the quintessence; Atwood leaves that open.
   - "used it efficaciously towards my poor neighbors in most desperate cases" (I.1 ¶81)
   - also: "having ventured to administer the medicine to sufferers given over by physicians" (I.1 ¶66); "out of it may be prepared better medicines and arcane" (I.1 ¶76); "by the use of the elixir, is reported to have lived throughout the period of a thousand years" (I.1 ¶36)
   - *stated* (as testimony). QUOTED: H. Khunrath; QUOTED: Seton; QUOTED: Paracelsus; QUOTED: Artephius (credibility left open by Atwood).

5. **Moral transformation of the maker.** The perfected Stone removes the root of sin from its maker and turns evil into good, provided the work is carried through to its proper end. From then on he is "ravished with the goodness of God".
   - "which being perfected by anyone, takes away from him the root of all sin and evil; changing his evil into good" (I.1 ¶57)
   - also: "provided only he carries through the work to its legitimate end" (I.1 ¶57); "For from thenceforward he is continually ravished with the goodness of God" (I.1 ¶57)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

6. **Regeneration as a real, living operation.** Early faith rested on "reality and living operation", and regeneration was rationally understood. The Art is "the one Art and medium of vital perfectibility".
   - "faith was then grounded in reality and living operation, and the mystery of human regeneration, so zealously proclaimed, was also rationally understood" (I.1 ¶11)
   - also: "the one Art and medium of vital perfectibility" (I.1 ¶74); "by its open efficacy, and the power of works, in healing and purifying the lives of men" (I.1 ¶26)
   - *stated* (¶11); *interpreted* (¶74, "vital perfectibility" not explained). ATWOOD; QUOTED: Thomas Vaughan (¶26).

7. **The higher aim is theosophy, not gold.** The art's power is to quicken and perfect. Using it in the mineral kingdom is the lower use; its higher purpose is "universal theosophy". The philosophers professed power over all nature, not over minerals only.
   - "the higher purposes and application of the Divine Art, and universal theosophy, rather than to rest its capabilities of quickening and perfection in the mineral kingdom" (I.1 ¶42)
   - also: "the powers they professed, not in the advancement of the mineral kingdom only, but over all nature" (I.1 ¶86); "sacrificing their whole life’s hope to the multiplication of gold" (I.1 ¶42)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

8. **Conviction and contentment in truth.** Proving the Mystery gave Agrippa a "final and convictive faith" above ordinary science. From the top of "Celsus’ ladder" the particular sciences look inferior. Seton takes satisfaction only in truth. Flamel's delight after success was contemplating nature's works in the vessels.
   - "that final and convictive faith, which is as much above ordinary science as the vulgar credulity of mankind is below it" (I.1 ¶64)
   - also: "as it were from the top of Celsus’ ladder, looking down upon the steps by which he had climbed, and whereon he had successively rested" (I.1 ¶64); "though I possess all things, I have no rest or enjoyment of any, except in the truth, which is my whole satisfaction" (I.1 ¶66); "I found exceeding great pleasure and delight in seeing and contemplating the admirable works of nature, within the vessels" (I.1 ¶57)
   - *stated* (the ladder image is *interpreted* as stages of knowledge). ATWOOD; QUOTED: Seton; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

## Principles (general laws of the work)

1. **As above, so below.** Higher and lower correspond, and the correspondence makes the work on the One Thing possible.
   - "that which is above is as that which is below, and that which is below is as that which is above, for performing the miracles of the One Thing" (I.1 ¶9)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).

2. **The work repeats creation.** The operation on the One Thing is the way the world was made.
   - "So the world was created. Hence were all wonderful adaptations of which this is the manner." (I.1 ¶9)
   - *interpreted*; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).

3. **Theosophic books and physical ground.** The Poimander and Asclepian books describe our divine capacities and the promise of regeneration. The physical ground of these, and the practical method, are said to be in the Golden Treatise.
   - "denote rather our divine capabilities and promise of regeneration than the physical ground of either" (I.1 ¶7)
   - also: "this, with the practical method of alchemy being further given in the Tractatus Aureus, or Golden Treatise" (I.1 ¶7)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD.

4. **One hidden discovery underlies ancient philosophy.** The theosophic schools rested on an experimental ground. The Alexandrians joined Theurgy with Hermetic experimental physics. Alchemy is the deepest metaphysics and "most profounde philosophye", and Hermetic philosophy is "universal, rational, and divine".
   - "that occult discovery on which the systems of ancient philosophy appear to have been very uniformly based" (I.1 ¶12)
   - also: "the most famous schools of theosophy have in all ages been based on a similar experimental ground and profound science of truth in their leaders" (I.1 ¶17); "each professing a genuine knowledge of the Theurgic art, and experimental physics on the Hermetic ground" (I.1 ¶27); "connects Alchemy with the most profound metaphysical science" (I.1 ¶64); "the ground of the whole Hermetic philosophy laid out, and the relative vanity of worldly science to that, which is universal, rational, and divine" (I.1 ¶64); "For it is most profounde philosophye This subtill science of holy Alkimy" (I.1 ¶52)
   - *stated* (as Atwood's hedged thesis: "appear to have been"; ¶17 is a forward promise). ATWOOD; QUOTED: Norton.

5. **The mystery is entered by experience, and can be revealed theosophically or intellectually.** Boehme entered experimentally and gives a "ground of experience" for decoding the adepts. He revealed the ancient wisdom anew "theosophically, but not intellectually". The Greeks' doctrine of Wisdom remains the best scientific explanation of the divine source. Plato's fear of writing the truth suggests his science was practical.
   - "of all who have hitherto entered experimentally into the mystery, the plainest, simplest, and most confidential exponent" (I.1 ¶68)
   - also: "such a ground of experience and principles of the Divine Art in his writings, as may help the student to conceive profoundly and lead him to the means of understanding the enigmas of the old adepts" (I.1 ¶68); "Revealed anew, it will be observed, theosophically, but not intellectually." (I.1 ¶70); "the science which drew the tyrant to the philosopher was more probably practical and profitably interesting than abstracts would appear to be to such a mind" (I.1 ¶17)
   - *stated* (the distinction); *interpreted* ("theosophically" read as inner experience). ATWOOD.

6. **One matter of metals.** Metals can be transmuted because they share one matter. Alchemy is the surest form of inquiry because it knows each thing through its proper cause.
   - "Metals can be transmuted one into another, since they are of one and the same matter" (I.1 ¶42)
   - also: "recommends alchemy as the best and most easy means of rational investigation" (I.1 ¶39)
   - *stated*; QUOTED: Aquinas; QUOTED: Albert (both endorsed by Atwood).

7. **Ether that burns without consuming itself.** The ether in the divine water burns perpetually, without loss, if outside air is kept out.
   - "the ether in this praeter-perfect aqueous body will burn perpetually, without diminution or consumption of itself, if the external air only be restrained" (I.1 ¶20)
   - *stated*; UNCLEAR voice: Khunrath or the ancient author he cites ("he devoutly affirms").

8. **Theory before practice.** Investigation and thought must come before experiment. Bacon says to stop experimenting, grasp his meaning and work "according to the blood", which he glosses as the understanding. Most adepts forbid practice before full understanding of the matter. Atwood adopts the rule and will next examine the theoretic ground of transmutation.
   - "strict investigation and thought which should always precede experiment" (I.1 ¶82)
   - also: "operate according to the blood; i.e., according to the understanding" (I.1 ¶86); "leave off experiments, apprehend my meaning" (I.1 ¶86); "leave off experiments until the ground of wisdom is properly conceived" (I.1 ¶86); "warn their readers against running into the practice upon vague premises, and before they have attained to a full understanding of the matter to be taken in hand" (I.1 ¶86); "they unanimously recommend a studious examination, in order to conceive rightly of the promises held out, before atemnpting to judge them" (I.1 ¶86)
   - *stated*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Roger Bacon (endorsed).

9. **Do not read literally; read from what nature makes possible.** A literal reading loses "the clue to Ariadne". The writings contradict one another only in their literal sense; their true meaning agrees. Read "from the possibility of nature", not the surface of the words. The New Light claims to rest on nature and manual experience, though its claims conflict with common ideas of what nature can do.
   - "he that takes the philosophers’ saying according to the outward sense and signification has already lost the clue to Ariadne, and wanders up and down the labyrinth" (I.1 ¶84)
   - also: "understand my writings, not so much from the outside of my words as from the possibility of nature" (I.1 ¶84); "apparently contradictory, as respects their language when taken in a literal sense; for he never quarrels with their true meaning" (I.1 ¶16); "professedly drawn from the fountain of nature, and grounded in manual experience" (I.1 ¶71)
   - *stated* (Artephius); *interpreted* (Sendivogius, ¶16). QUOTED: Artephius; QUOTED: Sendivogius; ATWOOD.

10. **Words need a guiding idea.** Words convey true ideas only to someone who already holds the guiding idea. Word-study confirms a truth but does not discover it.
    - "words do not convey true ideas unless their proper leader be previously entertained" (I.1 ¶6)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD.

11. **Theory is stated openly; the practice is hidden.** Adepts set out the roots of the science but conceal the practice, because truth is not to be shown "to every ribald". Aquinas obscures points on purpose to screen the doctrine.
    - "the roots of the Hermetic science are fairly stated; but the practice most carefully concealed" (I.1 ¶39)
    - also: "truth ought not to be shown to every ribald, for then that would become most vile, which, in the hands of a philosopher, is the most precious of all things" (I.1 ¶39); "he slurs over points and sophisticates also occasionally in order to screen the doctrine from superficial detection" (I.1 ¶42)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Roger Bacon.

12. **The veil is protective and purposeful.** Absurd recipes are "wisdom’s envelope". They guard the magistery, help those already on the right road, and mislead only the credulous. Only the art itself can lift the veil. A plain art is hidden in horrid metaphors against thieves, villains and fools. Adepts teach indirectly, "by reasons and examples". Contradictions are customary and the reader needs them. Lully's writings open only to those with "the key of his cabalistic mind". Repeated study and some prior knowledge are needed.
    - "For they are a part of wisdom’s envelope, to guard her universal magistery from an incapable and dreaming world" (I.1 ¶33)
    - also: "calculated they are, nevertheless, though closely sealed, to awaken rational curiosity, and lend a helping hand to those who have already entered on the right road" (I.1 ¶33); "Right skillfully has old Geber veiled a fair discovery, by his own art alone to be unmasked" (I.1 ¶33); "In horrid metaphors veiled are an art most plain" (I.1 ¶85); "we are willing to guide into the knowledge of nature indirectly, by reasons and examples" (I.1 ¶84); "Be not displeased if thou meetest sometimes with contradictions in my treatises, it being the custom of philosophers to use them; thou hast need of them" (I.1 ¶84); "the key of his cabalistic mind" (I.1 ¶48); "repeated perusal and a certain knowledge are requisite, in order to gather its recondite drift" (I.1 ¶71)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Ripley; QUOTED: Sendivogius.

13. **Adepts accept public disbelief.** The crowd cannot grasp the truth and cares little for it, so the philosophers were content to leave it sceptical. Gold-making and recipes are not the valuable part of the subject; Vaughan leads instead "to the true and only valuable speculation".
    - "But philosophers were content to have it so; observing the incapability of the common herd, and how little they cared for the truth, or the witness of nature’s greatest miracles" (I.1 ¶66)
    - also: "repelling impertinent inquiry, he leads at once to the true and only valuable speculation of the subject" (I.1 ¶74)
    - *stated* (¶66); *interpreted* (¶74). ATWOOD.

14. **The art is carried in allegory, fable, prophecy and images.** Du Parfait Amour allegorizes the art. Merlin's Prophecies reportedly concern only Hermetic secrets. Maier explains fable "by the Hermetic key". Flamel painted the true signs of the art under hieroglyphic veils. The books' title pages and engravings repay reflection.
    - "shows him to have been practically conversant with the art he allegorizes" (I.1 ¶29)
    - also: "the Prophecies of Merlin, which are reported to have sole reference to the arcane of the Hermetic Art" (I.1 ¶45); "of all those who have connected ancient fable with philosophy, and explained them by the Hermetic key, Michael Maier ranks first" (I.1 ¶70); "the most true and essential marks and signs of this art, yet under veils and types and hieroglyphical characters" (I.1 ¶57); "even the title pages of many of them convey more idea and food for reflection than other modern tomes" (I.1 ¶70)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.

15. **Sacramental signs stood for real powers.** The Church's types of water, oil, salt and light signified real efficacies.
    - "certain ordinances and elementary types, as of water, oil, salt and light, signified some real and notable efficacies" (I.1 ¶26)
    - *stated*; ATWOOD.

16. **The beginning decides the end.** A faulty beginning produces a fraudulent result.
    - "In delibi principio was myne inception: Therefore was frawde in fine the conclusion:" (I.1 ¶65)
    - *interpreted* (the Latin is uncertain); QUOTED: Ripley.

## Identities asserted

- Divine water = Scythian latex — "calling it divine water, Scythian latex, &c." (I.1 ¶20) — QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus.
- Divine water = a spirit of the nature of the ether (and quintessence of things) — "which is a spirit of the nature of the ether and quintessence of things" (I.1 ¶20) — QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus.
- Divine water = "praeter-perfect aqueous body" (which holds the perpetually burning ether) — "concerning this water; and he devoutly affirms, that the ether in this praeter-perfect aqueous body will burn perpetually" (I.1 ¶20) — Khunrath or his ancient author.
- Divine water = source of potable gold and the stone — "whence potable gold, and the stone of philosophers, takes its beginning" (I.1 ¶20) — QUOTED: Hermolaus Barbarus.
- Bononian Enigma / epitaphs = descriptions of the occult material (universal subject) — "the adepts claim it as having exclusive reference to the occult material of their art" (I.1 ¶20); "said by the alchemists to relate to the properties of their universal subject" (I.1 ¶24) — QUOTED: adepts/alchemists; Atwood endorses.
- Smaragdine Table = the working principle and total subject of the art — "is said to comprehend the working principle and total subject of the art" (I.1 ¶7) — ATWOOD (reporting).
- Smaragdine (Emerald) Table = what remains of Egypt's Sacred Art — "This Emerald Table, unique and authentic as it may be regarded, is all that remains to us from Egypt of her Sacred Art" (I.1 ¶10) — ATWOOD.
- Prima materia = the first principles — "I had now the prima material, the first principles" (I.1 ¶57) — QUOTED: Nicholas Flamel.
- One blessed stone = the subject of the most holy stone of the philosophers — "of the one blessed stone alone, besides which there is no other thing, the subject of the most holy stone of the philosophers" (I.1 ¶64) — QUOTED: Cornelius Agrippa (apposition; see Open questions).
- Azoth = the Universal Mercury — "Azoth, viz., the Universal Mercury" (I.1 ¶81) — QUOTED: H. Khunrath.
- Blood of the Lion = the gold of the philosophers (possibly also the universal tincture) — "the universal tincture, and the blood of the Lion, which is the gold of the philosophers" (I.1 ¶81) — QUOTED: H. Khunrath.
- Philosophers' gold = the transmuting powder (Van Helmont's usage) — "one grain of the philosophers’ gold upon some thousands of grains of boiling quicksilver" (I.1 ¶76) — QUOTED: Van Helmont.
- Blood = the understanding — "operate according to the blood; i.e., according to the understanding" (I.1 ¶86) — QUOTED: Roger Bacon (gloss inside the quotation).
- Alchemy = most profound philosophy — "For it is most profounde philosophye This subtill science of holy Alkimy" (I.1 ¶52) — QUOTED: Norton's Ordinal (endorsed).
- Green Lion ≠ vitriol (fools' naming) — "vitriall, Which folys doe call the Grene Lyon" (I.1 ¶65) — QUOTED: Ripley. A negative key: common vitriol is not the true Green Lion, if "folys" marks the naming as wrong.
- Medicine = tincture (Starkey uses both of the same White medicine) — "I expended or wasted nearly all of this tincture" (I.1 ¶73) — QUOTED: Starkey.
- Thrice Great = having the spiritual intelligence of all things / the three parts of the philosophy of the world — "whence he is called the Thrice Great Hermes, having the spiritual intelligence of all things in their universal law" (I.1 ¶6); "Therefore I am I called Thrice Great Hermes, having the Three Parts of the philosophy of the whole world" (I.1 ¶9) — ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes (Smaragdine Table).
- Chemia = Egypt (Cham), also the pupil of the eye and "other black matters", hence "Black Art" — "by the word Chemia, says Plutarch, the seeing pupil of the human eye was also designated, and other black matters, whence in part perhaps Alchemy, so obscurely descended, has been likewise stigmatized as a Black Art" (I.1 ¶5) — QUOTED: Plutarch; the "Black Art" inference is ATWOOD's ("perhaps"). Bears on the method only through "black matters".

## Open questions

1. **Earth/fire and subtle/gross (¶9).** Does "earth" pair with "subtle" (by word order) or with "gross"? Or are these two separate separations? The Table gives no gloss.
2. **"the operation of the Sun" (¶9).** Does it mean the operation of the Sun named as the One Thing's father, or the whole work called the operation of the Sun? Unexplained.
3. **Three kingdoms vs "Three Parts of the philosophy" (¶6, ¶9).** Both explain the title "Thrice Great"; the text does not say whether they are the same triad.
4. **Divine water: subject, agent or both (¶20)?** It is the "beginning" of the stone (subject-like) and holds a perpetually burning ether (agent-like). "and quintessence of things" may describe the water's nature or say that it is the quintessence. Atwood does not say whether the lamp liquor of ¶18 is this water; she sets the two side by side.
5. **Phoenix and "reviviscent spirit" (¶37–38).** Is "our Phoenix" the art itself, which passed from East to West (figurative), or something within the work whose spirit Bacon worked "ascending and descending" (operative)? ¶37's historical framing supports the first reading. The stated results in ¶38 (ligature and dissolution of natures, gold) support the second.
6. **Bacon's "forbidden realms of self-sufficiency" (¶38).** Is this a moral fault of the operator (curiosity, pride), or a state the work itself produces that is forbidden? The text says both "fatal curiosity" and "a gift somewhat over rashly and dangerously obtained".
7. **The laboratory "compact in one" and the "kindred light" (¶33).** Reading A: a single self-contained thing, not an external laboratory, seen only by a light akin to the philosophers' intellect. Reading B: a well-equipped laboratory of another kind ("of common fittings"), with the "light" belonging to the philosophers' art. ¶33's contrasts ("gross elements", "far different nature") favour A.
8. **Flamel's scent (¶57).** Does it identify the prepared first principles (immediately followed by "Having this, I easily accomplished the magistery") or the completed product?
9. **Moral change from the Stone (¶57).** Does the work itself transform the maker, or does the change come through the contemplation of God's goodness that follows ("For from thenceforward he is continually ravished with the goodness of God")?
10. **Red Elixir, Red Stone, Red Powder, Powder of Projection, philosophers' gold, blood of the Lion.** Several names appear near each other (¶59–60, ¶66, ¶76, ¶81). No identity among them is stated, except blood of the Lion = gold of the philosophers (Khunrath) and Van Helmont's calling his powder "the philosophers’ gold". Likewise "quintessence" (Artephius) and "elixir" (Atwood) are used of the same claim in ¶36, and "the elixir" and "a potent spiritual medicine" of Paracelsus in ¶64. These are co-references, not asserted identities.
11. **Agrippa's "one blessed stone" (¶64).** Possible readings: (a) the subject is one single thing; (b) the subject is itself a "stone" of the same nature as the product; (c) "no other thing" means the subject comprehends everything. The apposition has no verb.
12. **The Green Lion (¶65).** Is Ripley saying fools wrongly take vitriol for the Green Lion (implying a true Green Lion elsewhere), or just reporting usage? Does "Which" cover sulphur as well?
13. **Blood of the Lion (¶81).** Does "which is the gold of the philosophers" gloss only "the blood of the Lion", or the universal tincture as well?
14. **"as they did" (¶82).** Did mankind imagine the salts, sulphurs and mercuries, or did the adepts themselves speak of them, so that readers took the names literally ("their salts")?
15. **"the true key; but only of the vestibule" (¶82).** Is the truth hidden even from holders of the true key, or only from those who have just the "vestibule and entrance rights"?
16. **Deaths by the elixir (¶64, ¶74).** "a greater flame", "venom of the waters of the Styx" and "dissolution in the conflict of opposite principles": literal bodily death, or a broader dissolution of the person? Does Atwood adopt these explanations ("as the narrative runs"; "has also been considered")?
17. **Plato's "parturition respecting this ingenerated in the soul" (¶17).** Is it an inward process that must be completed before truth is reached, or cited only as a sign of a secret practical science? Atwood does not connect the two.
18. **Lully's contradictions (¶48).** Deliberate concealment from greedy chemists, or the result of his learning the art late? Atwood leaves it undecided.
19. **Aquinas, "Noli te, charissime, cum majori opere occupare" (¶42).** Does it mean, in our rendering, do not busy yourself with the greater work (subordinating alchemy to spiritual duty), or is it a general warning against distracting labour? This rests on our translation of corrupt Latin.
20. **Bacon's "being a lighted candle" (¶86).** Is the reader who grasps the meaning the lit candle, or is Bacon's meaning itself the candle? The relation to "the true Light" of ¶86 is unexplained.
21. **"Whites and Reds" (¶84).** Spurious white and red products, or misused talk of white and red stages? Compare Norton's White and Red Stone (¶60) and Ripley's untrue tinctures (¶65).
22. **"In delibi principio" (¶65).** Probably, in our rendering, in a weak (debili) beginning; but the Latin is corrupt.
23. **Boehme's attainment (¶68, ¶70).** "something better even than a view of the Promised Land": did he reach the goal of the art, or a different spiritual attainment ("theosophically, but not intellectually")?
24. **"without the Lodge" (¶64).** An organized fraternity of adepts, or simply anyone not initiated?
25. **The persecution complaint (¶66).** Is it Seton's or Philalethes' (the bracketed correction)? This affects the voice of Impediment 10 and Result 8.
