# Method material — I.3 The Golden Treatise of Hermes Trismegistus

Voice convention: the main text (¶) is Hermes' treatise (QUOTED: Hermes). The footnotes (n) are Atwood's commentary. In them she often adopts the glosses of "our Scholiast" and other "adepts", and those glosses are labelled with both voices. Labels give the status of the plain meaning: stated (the text itself glosses or identifies it) or interpreted (inferred, with the basis given).

## The subject (what the work is done on)

- **One matter only.** The material is a single thing, and so are the art and the Stone. "the whole matter I know to be one only thing" (I.3 ¶38). Also: "even as the hermetic material is one the art is one; and the stone is also one mineral spirit" (I.3 n55). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, repeated by ATWOOD*

- **The subject is within the enquirer: the Universal Nature.** The work is to be sought inwardly, not by ordinary experiment. Its subject is the substance of the Universal Nature, in which all beings live without knowing it and which stays hidden in ordinary generation. "the work is both with you and about you" (I.3 ¶8). Also: "seek to know intrinsical within themselves the substance of that Universal Nature in which they, with all beings in common, as it were, unconsciously live" (I.3 n8). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The philosophic matter lies in the "caverns of the metals".** The caverns mean the central motion of mineral life, where spirit first coagulates and takes concrete form. The matter is called a stone, a "true mineral petrification", and yet it is also spirit, soul and body. "In the caverns of the metals there is hidden the stone that is venerable, splendid in colour, a mind sublime, and an open sea" (I.3 ¶10). Also: "in the central motion of the mineral life, where the spirit is first coagulated and conceives itself into a concrete form" (I.3 n13); "Ii is a stone and no stone, spirit, soul, and body" (I.3 n13, Arnold). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD (citing Arnold)*

- **Many cover names, one thing.** The names are meant to hide the matter from the unworthy. In concrete form it is properly "the Stone". In its fluid state it is "the sea of the wise", passive to the light. "it is also named the feminine of magnesia, or the hen, or the white spittle, or the volatile milk, the incombustible ash in order that it may be hidden from the inept" (I.3 ¶11). Also: "In its flowing, humid state it is called the sea of the wise, passive to all impression and influences of the light" (I.3 n16). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The philosophic water and its four "elements".** The Water is divided into four parts, and these parts are "hypostatic relations" called elements. One divides into two (agent and patient). From their conjunction three appear (body, soul, spirit), which together beget all things. "the division that was made upon the Water, by the ancient philosophers, separates it into four substances; one to two, and three to one" (I.3 ¶6). Also: "The philosophic water then, being divided into four parts or hypostatic relations, they are called elements" (I.3 n5); "the one third part of which is colour, that is to say --- a coagulating moisture" (I.3 ¶6). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD (see Open questions on "one to two, and three to one")*

- **The subject holds its own active principle.** The water, or mercury, contains its own sulfur ("vital flame"), so the whole work proceeds from it. The true unguent is likewise already inside the matter's own body and needs no preparation from outside. The matter is "simple". "this water or mercury of the adepts has, within itself, its own good sulfur, or vital flame, thou mayest perfect all things out of mercury" (I.3 n5). Also: "It dwells in his own body, as fire in trees and stones" (I.3 ¶54); "Here again we are reminded of the simplicity of the matter worked with, and its formal light" (I.3 n62). — *stated; ATWOOD (following the Scholiast); QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The Argent vive (philosophic Mercury) is the residue of the separation.** It is a fiery, vital, mobile spirit. It is prepared and coagulated in "the innermost chamber of life", at the deepest centre of each created life, and is what remains of the philosophic earth after separation. It must be kept. "Keep, therefore, thy Argent vive, which is prepared in the innermost chamber in which it is coagulated; for that is Mercury which is spoken of concerning the residual earth" (I.3 ¶9). Also: "this argent vive, then, which is the residuum of the philosophic earth after the separation" (I.3 n9); "even in the ultimate axle of each created life" (I.3 n9); repeated at I.3 n48. — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; QUOTED: the Scholiast (called "wise", adopted by ATWOOD)*

- **A universal nature that people despise.** In its exalted state the subject is most precious. In the common world it is defiled, present everywhere in decay and the lowest forms of life, and unrecognized. "Our most precious stone cast forth upon the dunghill, being most dear is made altogether vile" (I.3 ¶32). Also: "The same catholic nature, which in its preternatural exaltation appears so very precious in the eyes of the philosopher, is in the common world defiled; abiding everywhere in putrefactions and the vilest forms of life" (I.3 n40). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The three in the matter: spirit, soul, body.** In Hermes' figure, the water is the passive spirit, the redness is its soul, and the earth produced between them is the body of both. More generally, every nature holds "three from two": the needful water, the oily tincture, and the faeces (earth). The notes treat this triad as agent, patient and offspring, the adepts' Salt, Sulfur and Mercury, or body, spirit and occult soul. These are hidden natures, not literal water, oil and earth. "The water spoken of by Hermes is the passive spirit, the redness is its soul, and the earth begot betwixt them is the substance or body of both" (I.3 n21). Also: "In every nature there are three from two, first the needful water, then the oily tincture, and lastly, the faeces or earth which remains below" (I.3 ¶23); "agent, patient and offspring universal and co-equal" (I.3 n30); "a body, a spirit, and an occult soul" (I.3 n30, Sendivogius); "not of kitchen-stuff, those three pot-principles of water, oil, and earth" (I.3 n30, Vaughan). — *n21 gloss stated (voice UNCLEAR: Scholiast or ATWOOD). The mapping of ¶23's water/oily tincture/faeces onto Salt/Sulfur/Mercury is interpreted: it rests on where n30 is placed, and no item-by-item mapping is given*

- **The bodies house the spirits.** The "bodies" are the homes of their spirits. The text explains them as the sensible and vegetable media of life. The life in them sleeps until they are dissolved. "The bodies of the metals, explains our Scholiast, are the domiciles of their spirits" (I.3 n38). Also: "the bodies (i.e., the sensible and vegetable media of life)" (I.3 n38); "the spirits in the washed bodies desire to dwell therein and rejoice in them" (I.3 ¶29). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast (adopted by ATWOOD); QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The red root: Terra Adamica, Magnesia, Salt.** The root material is Adamic earth. The wise also call it Magnesia, and once purified it is called Salt. "The red root is the Terra Adamica, called sometimes Magnesia by the wise, and Salt after the purification" (I.3 n52). Also: "so our Magnesia, continues he, being sown in the Philosophic Earth, dies and corrupts, that it may conceive itself anew" (I.3 n21). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **Man's origin as the ground of the mystery.** Atwood treats Hermes' saying as perhaps the whole ground of the mystery. An adept glosses man's making from "the dust of the earth" as made from "the arising quintessence of the universal nature". "Man is generated from the principle of Nature whose inward substance is fleshy, and not from anything else" (I.3 ¶40). Also: "With what force and earnestness does the master here speak, as if the whole ground of the mystery lay in these words" (I.3 n51); "Man, it is said, was created of the dust of the earth; that is, interprets the adept, of the arising quintessence of the universal nature" (I.3 n51). — *interpreted: the adept's gloss is on "dust of the earth", not on ¶40's words; QUOTED: Hermes, ATWOOD, QUOTED: the adept*

- **The egg as analogue.** The disposition sought is found in "our egg", not in the hen's egg. Still, the hen's egg corresponds in every respect to the art's "compound simple", except that it lacks universality ("catholicity"). "The disposition sought after by the philosophers, O son, is but one in our egg; but this in the hen's egg can, by no means be found" (I.3 ¶22). Also: "comparing also its vital composition to that of a hen’s egg, which answers in all respects, excepting the catholicity, to the compound simple of this art" (I.3 n26); "in the hen's egg is the greatest proximity and relationship in nature" (I.3 ¶22). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Where the matter is most easily found.** Maria says the science may be found in all bodies, but most easily in the matter that most plainly contains the four philosophic elements. "I say to you, writes Maria, laconically, that this science may be found in all bodies" (I.3 n48). Also: "They found it most easily in that matter which most evidently contains the four philosophic elements" (I.3 n48). — *stated as Maria's view, cited by ATWOOD. It is interpreted that this answers the concealed vessel of n48, since the note does not say whether vessel and matter are the same*

## The agent (what does the work: fire, spirit, light, intellect…)

- **The Spirit of life: vulture and crow.** One Spirit of life does the work, appearing in different states. Active and devouring, it is the vulture: the newly born quintessential spirit, or Proteus, the first sublimed quintessence not yet perfected. Darkened and passive, it is the crow, "the chief principle of the art". The crow stands at the early stage where the revived spirit is united with its "solar ferment". "The vulture and the crow are interpreted to be one and the same thing, only differing somewhat in estate" (I.3 n12). Also: "Whilst the Spirit of life appears active and devouring in the process, it has been called the vulture, and when it lies in a more obscured and passive condition, the crow" (I.3 n12); "The vulture, according to our Scholiast, is the new born quintessential spirit or Proteus" (I.3 n11); "the chief principle of the art is the Crow" (I.3 ¶10). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD and QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **"Our Mercury", the single Protean agent.** A single shape-changing agent runs through the whole work. It looks different in each element but stays one, and the artist performs every operation with it. It is fiery, vital and running. "this art beginning in our Mercury, likewise finishes in the same" (I.3 n55). Also: "yet is, not withstanding, One throughout the multiformity of nature. With this the artist works, and with it he transacts all the necessary operations of our Stone" (I.3 n55); "Our Mercury, says the wise Scholiast, is philosophic, fiery, vital, running" (I.3 n9). — *stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **The philosophers' fire is not common fire.** The fire of the work is magical, creative and vital, very subtle and volatile. The essence of life is a pure, celestial, generating fire, which is also the first matter of metals. Hermes' "moist fire" is not explained here, and Atwood defers the nature, origin and "artificial excitation" of the philosophic fire. "The philosophers uniformly distinguish their own special fire as magical, creative, vital; whereas the common element is without sagacity or discrimination" (I.3 n41). Also: "Our fire is a most subtle fire, inhabiting in himself an infernal secret fire, and in its kind extremely volatile" (I.3 n41, the adept); "the essence of life, says the scholiast, is nothing else than a pure, naked, unmingled Fire" (I.3 n22); "Concerning the peculiar nature, origin, and artificial excitation of the philosophic fire, we may more effectively inquire hereafter" (I.3 n14). — *stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: the adept, QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **The temperate unguent, the Perscrutinator, the Rational Ferment.** A tempered fire, which Hermes calls the unguent and identifies with sulphur, is the medium between the faeces and the water and the "Perscrutinator" (searcher) of the water. It stirs up the whole power of the matter and purifies it. To know how to prepare and use it is the whole art. Atwood says the name marks the "Rational Ferment" and defers that topic. "the temperate unguent, which is fire, is the medium between the faeces and the water and is the Perscrutinator of the water" (I.3 ¶24). Also: "It is the stirrer-up of the whole power and efficacy and purifier of the matter; hence Hermes calls it the Perscrutinator, eminently distinguishing the Rational Ferment" (I.3 n32); "The knowledge of this secret sulfur, says the Scholiast, and how to prepare it and use it in this work includes the whole art of perfection" (I.3 n32). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD and QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **Sulfur is Light, the seed of gold.** Sulphur, auripigment and similar names stand for the hidden luminous principle that gives life. The philosophic sulfur is Light, in states that change as the work develops. It is the seed of gold and the concentrating power of the Stone. As sulphur it tinges and fixes, and it holds the volatile "fugitive". "The occult luminous principle of vitalization he calls sulfur, auripigment, etc., hiding it also under a variety of other covertures" (I.3 n24). Also: "the philosophic sulfur, or Light, as it becomes developed in the Hermetic work" (I.3 n25); "light or sulfur, as they call it, is the true form or seed of gold, and the concentering virtue of their philosophic stone" (I.3 n40); "this sulphur doth tinge and fix, and is the connection of the tinctures" (I.3 ¶21). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **One spirit, many grades.** The "sulphurs" or lights are not all of one quality. Some are heavenly and some terrestrial. The spirit is one in essence but varies with its conception and with how far the art has rectified it. "certain of them are heavenly, and some are terrestrial" (I.3 ¶22). Also: "the spirit, though one is essence, is extremely diversified, in its conception, as also according to the degree and order of its rectification by art" (I.3 n28). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Two agents, outer and inner.** The fire and the heavenly bird stand for the external and internal agents. Both must be kept in the matter to the end, since either one preserves and feeds it. "Include, therefore, and conserve in this sea, the fire, and the heavenly Flyer, to the latest moment of his exit" (I.3 ¶11). Also: "By the fire and heavenly bird are dignified, says the scholiast, the external and internal agents in the Hermetic work, by either of which it is conserved and nourished to the end" (I.3 n16). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by QUOTED: the Scholiast (which agent is which is not said; see Open questions)*

- **The agent outranks the body.** Body and spirit come from one source. Of the two, the agent is superior in operation: it gives life, holds the matter together, and forces nature's shifting form into its true form. "the agent, because it vivifies and holds the particles of the matter together, is repesentatively superior in operation, to compel the Protean Hypostasis of Nature to enter into his true Form" (I.3 n37). Also: "they both proceed form one fountain" (I.3 n37). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **The ruling, framing Spirit at nature's centre.** The magistery is reached through communion with the ruling Spirit of nature. The operator must first enter the Spirit that frames all things and dwells at the central root. That Spirit then carries him, as a vehicle, into the root and shows him the whole work. "the way to the attainment of the magistery is by communion with the ruling Spirit of nature" (I.3 n19). Also: "But thou must first enter into that Spirit which is the Framer of all things, which pierces through and dwells in that central root" (I.3 n19); "it will, as a vehicle, carry thee into the same root where all things are hidden" (I.3 n19). — *stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: the Scholiast (endorsed)*

- **Venus: the central light of nature.** Venus stands for nature's hidden central light, a moist spirit. She liquefies the bodies held down by earthiness, wipes away their rust, extracts their substance and gives them life. "Venus personifies the central light of nature, which is occultated in her generations" (I.3 n49). Also: "I beget light, nor is the darkness of my nature" (I.3 ¶39); "I liquefy them and wipe away their rust, and I extract their substance" (I.3 ¶39); "they gladly adhere to this moist spirit, that it may vivify them" (I.3 n49). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The King: the Rational Efficient.** The crowned King stands for the rational efficient principle. His "brethren" are the lower degrees of illumination in the spirit, which are finally gathered back into accord with their source. "By the king, the Rational Efficient is signified; by the brethren, the inferior degrees of illumination in the spirit" (I.3 n50). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **Luna: the passive ethereal light.** "Luna" is an ethereal light, the passive luminary of the "Philosophic Heaven". Its waning and waxing in the work parallel the moon's. "Understand here the diminution and increase of that ethereal light, which is the passive luminary in the Philosophic Heaven" (I.3 n7). Also: "My own Luna, also, is my light, exceeding every other light" (I.3 ¶37). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The dissolving water, which is really fire.** A pure water comes from the crow. It is the viscous moisture made by the dissolution, which dissolves metals at the root and reduces them to their "first ens", which is water. The "watery pontic form" (Aqua Philosophica) dissolves the elements. It cleanses inwardly as common water cleanses outwardly and purifies itself. Joined "in consciousness" with the central Efficient, it becomes all-powerful. The water kept within the Stone is not truly water but fire. "the thin water is the viscous humidity made by the dissolution which radically dissolves all metals, and reduces them into their first ens, or water" (I.3 n12). Also: "O, blessed watery pontic form, that dissolvest the elements" (I.3 ¶27); "as common water washes and cleanses things outwardly, so this inwardly effects the same, even itself purifying itself from its inbred defilements" (I.3 n36); "being conjoined in consciousness with the central Efficient, it becomes all-powerful, and the key of every magic art" (I.3 n36); "this, however, is not water in its true form, but fire, containing in a pure vessel the ascending waters, lest the spirits should fly away from the bodies" (I.3 ¶26). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The Water as medium between the two poles.** Heaven and Earth are joined through a middle nature, the Water. Atwood reads Heaven and Earth as the two invisible poles of the Spirit, and the Water as the mercurial quintessence. That quintessence is born humid and vaporous, becomes golden as the central light informs it, and converts nature into itself. "the Heaven is to be joined mediately with the Earth; but the middle nature, which is the Water" (I.3 ¶55). Also: "The two invisible poles of the Spirit are here especially signalized by Hermes, and that consummate medium which brings them forth into manifestation" (I.3 n63); "The water alluded to is the mercurial quintessence, as it is first born in a humid and vaprous consistency" (I.3 n63). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The permanent watery Form, a matured stage.** The fixed watery Form is the same watery form praised earlier, now more mature (Trevisan's "fountain"). As "Framer of the royal elements", it draws the reddened light of its internal agent through its whole substance. "O, permanent watery Form, creatrix of the regal elements!" (I.3 ¶31). Also: "The fixed watery Form of the philosophic matter, which Hermes here apostrophies, is the same as was before celebrated only more mature" (I.3 n39); "it draws to itself the rubified light of its internal agent permeating the same throughout the whole essentiality" (I.3 n39). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The well, the source of the multiplying virtue.** The well is the inexhaustible universal ("catholic") spirit of life, with the hidden Causality of all things at its centre. The "auripigment" drawn from it is the multiplying power of the Stone. "the well is, as the catholic spirit of life, inexhaustible; at the bottom, or center rather, of which subsists the occult Causality of all" (I.3 n22). Also: "resting over the fountain as known to thee, and drawing from thence the Auripigment dry, without moisture" (I.3 ¶18); "that auripigment of philosophers which is the multiplicative virtue of their stone" (I.3 n22). — *stated for well and auripigment; interpreted that Hermes' "fountain" is n22's "well", which rests on note placement; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Living gold: the ferment Ixir.** Hermes' gold is the philosophers' living gold, a luminous concrete, not the dead metal. Its soul, the true aurific principle, acts as a medium that perfects the "seven bodies", which here mean the lower spheres of vitality. It is the Elixir, Ixir or true Ferment, which tinges and fixes. "so gold, which is the ferment Ixir, vivifies and contains every metallic body" (I.3 ¶58). Also: "The gold of the philosophers, or living gold, as they sometimes call their luminous concrete, is here alluded to throughout" (I.3 n65); "so does our soul of gold, writes the Scholiast, which is the true aurific principle, even as a medium, perfect all the other seven bodies" (I.3 n65); "Our gold is not common gold, says the adept, but a depurated substance, in the highest degree perfected and brought to an astral or heavenly complexion" (I.3 n63). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD and QUOTED: the Scholiast / the adept*

- **Kibric and Zibeth: soul and spirit.** Soul and spirit are two "fumes" from one root, and they are called the root of the Hermetic science. They are rectified on the white body and repeatedly separated, dissolved and reunited until their fermenting power can no longer be broken down. "it is necessary to rectify Kibric and Zibeth (the soul and spirit) upon this body" (I.3 n67). Also: "these two fumes are the root of the Hermetic science; which, being of one root, are separated, dissolved, and reunited so often until their fermentive virtue survives the utmost efforts of art or nature any more to decompose" (I.3 n67). — *stated; QUOTED: Maria (cited by ATWOOD)*

## The operator (requirements: knowledge, moral state, will, guidance, secrecy…)

- **Study, experience, divine blessing.** Three requirements depend on each other. Study gives theory. Theory admits one to the "central experience", which cannot be had in the Universal Spirit without God. Hermes claims both long labour and inspiration. "There are three things said to be necessary for the attainment of the Hermetic science: viz., study, experience, and the divine benediction" (I.3 n1). Also: "study is required for the theory, and this for entering into the central experience which, in the Universal Spirit, is not found without God" (I.3 n1); "this science and art I have obtained by the inspiration of the living God alone" (I.3 ¶3). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Theory and a right starting principle first.** Experiment without correct theory and a right principle to begin from wears people out for nothing. Freeing the radical element is impossible without the adepts' theory. "Without theoretic knowledge and a right principle to begin with, many have wearied themselves in experimenting" (I.3 n2). Also: "he who knows the principles and the way of generation, which consists according to the intention of nature, is but a very short way from the completement" (I.3 n2, Geber); "which, say the adepts, is impossible without the theory of their arcanum" (I.3 n4). — *stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Geber*

- **Moral fitness.** The operator must be free of arrogance, just, good, of deep reason, ready to help mankind, serene, diligent, and self-controlled. Philosophers are called "envious" only because they withhold the truth from the ignorant and vicious. "to free himself from the vice of arrogance; and to be just and good, and of a profound reason, ready at hand to help mankind" (I.3 ¶25). Also: "not that they grudged the truth to religious or just men, or to the wise; but to the ignorant and vicious, who are without self-control and benevolence" (I.3 ¶11); "Evil men are unworthy of this wisdom" (I.3 ¶11). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes (Atwood leaves ¶25 "unexplained", I.3 n33)*

- **Secrecy.** The art is to be guarded and never opened to the foolish, ignorant or wicked, who would use nature's power selfishly and overturn its order. The danger falls on the teacher. "that to no fatuous, ignorant, or inept person ye open this Stone" (I.3 ¶11). Also: "be himself a guardian of the secrets of philosophy open to him" (I.3 ¶25); "These secrets, son, it behooves us to guard and conceal from the wicked and foolish world" (I.3 ¶26); "these are unfit to be admitted to the Causal Knowledge, lest, handling the powerful machine of nature recklessly or unjustly for selfish ends, they subvert the order of final causes" (I.3 n10); "Wherefore the sentence of peril and jeopardy Upon the teacher reseth dreadfully" (I.3 n15, Norton). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, endorsed by ATWOOD; QUOTED: Norton, Lully*

- **Fear of God as obedience to the Law of the work.** The operator must fear and obey the Law of the ruling Spirit of nature. Breaking it in any part turns the work to evil. Only those who know the "Fontal Nature" by experience understand why God is to be feared. "fear God, in whom is the strength of thy undertaking; and the bond of each separated element" (I.3 ¶15). Also: "Hermes admonishes the student earnestly to fear and obey its Law; lest, being transgressed in any part, man should work evil instead of good through its means" (I.3 n19). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Withdrawal inward to a higher consciousness.** The rational operator shuts himself in, away from sense and the ignorance of ordinary life, and opens "the door of a higher consciousness". Otherwise he will be misled by the outward, literal sense of words and things. "he who is rational shuts himself within from the threshold of ignorance; lest supinely he should be deceived" (I.3 ¶15). Also: "to shut himself within, away from the distractions of sense and this life’s ignorance, and learn to open to himself the door of a higher consciousness" (I.3 n19). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Entering the centre with one's whole spirit.** The operator must enter with his whole spirit into the centre of nature and see there how all things begin, continue and are perfected. "Thou must enter with thy whole spirit into the center of nature, and there behold how all things are begun, continued, and perfected" (I.3 n19). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast (adopted by ATWOOD)*

- **Reason, judgment, meditation.** The teaching must be weighed rationally and meditated on, as if the student were its author. Reason is needed above all because Hermes deliberately hides the philosophic vessel. "Lay hold, therefore, of my instructions and meditate upon them, and so let thy heart be fitted, as if thou wast thyself the author of that which I now teach" (I.3 ¶15). Also: "then let us use our judgment" (I.3 ¶38); "The profound significance of the first monition to use our reason may be better appreciated on inquiry; for Hermes has chosen to conceal the philosophic vessel" (I.3 n48); "Meditate on this letter, and reject superfluities" (I.3 ¶40). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; ATWOOD*

- **Self-investigation and experiential knowledge of Life.** The teaching comes from experimental knowledge of Life. Without self-investigation its reality cannot be perceived. "who, without self-investigation, are unable to perceive the reality of those things which are spoken out of an experimental knowledge of Life" (I.3 n51). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **Not alone: fellowship and reciprocity.** No one can work the art alone. Friendship, gratitude and mutual benefit are called the chief art of working the matter, and no man may "Mercury mortify" without "his brother’s knowledging". The reasons are deferred. "In friendship, gratitude, and reciprocity of benefaction, say the adepts, consists the chief art of operating with their matter; and no man, for reasons hereafter explicable, can operate the Hermetic artifice alone" (I.3 n17). Also: "There may noe man Mercury mortify, But it be with his brother’s knowledging" (I.3 n17, verse citing Arnold); "I have received nothing from any, to whom I have not returned that which had been given me" (I.3 ¶12). — *stated; QUOTED: the adepts, endorsed by ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Learned by practice and patient search, not from a master.** The preparation of the philosophic water cannot truly be taught. Practice reveals it "by the instinct of nature". Hermes leaves the Mystery to unfold through study and faithful experiment, and the elements are to be discovered "through patience". "Nor can this secret be truly learned either from a master at all, but practice reveals it by the instinct of nature" (I.3 n36). Also: "Hermes leaves the Mystery thus, therefore, to unfold itself through study and faithful experiment, that the mind by searching and patient investigation may be prepared and able to appreciate the truth when found" (I.3 n10); "which are through patience to be discovered according to their causes and their occult operation" (I.3 ¶5); "the reward of ardent and continual toil" (I.3 n70). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Readiness: the already initiated.** Hermes writes only for the studious, the "predestined sons of wisdom" who are already initiated. "the predestined sons of wisdom, to guide them, already initiated, further into the practice of his high Art" (I.3 n3). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **After attainment: gratitude, vigilance, humility.** Whoever gains the Stone must be grateful and on guard against misusing it, even without meaning to. He must give up self-love for adoration of the divine goodness, since God is met only in humility. "has need not only to be grateful but to be watchful of every temptation, lest he should be drawn, even unwittingly, to abuse it" (I.3 n60). Also: "he should humble himself and sink away from every appetite of self love into the single adoration of the divine goodness" (I.3 n60); "It behooves us to give thanks to God, who bestows liberally to the wise" (I.3 ¶52). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

## The procedure and its stages (in the order the chapter gives, if it gives one)

The chapter gives no single linear recipe. Atwood's notes supply frames (A–C). The operations follow, grouped in the order the treatise and notes develop them (1–16).

- **A. Two works: decoction, then the Second Work.** The first part, decoction, dissolves the matter and purges it until it is almost annihilated and stripped of every outward form. It then revives by its own inborn force. The Second Work continues the first: it perfects the "newly informed embryo" and multiplies its light. Atwood says these few words cover the whole process. "Decoction lessens the matter, but the tincture augments it" (I.3 ¶7). Also: "the former Hermes calls decoction, which dissolving the matter discharges also its impurities by a proper rule; until, being at length on the verge of annihilation; i.e., freed from every exteriorly attracting form" (I.3 n7); "Then follows what is called the Second Work, which is only, in continuation of the First, to perfect the newly informed embryo and multiply its vivific light" (I.3 n7); "In such few words, Hermes professes to comprehend the whole of the artificial process of working the Spirit" (I.3 n7); "This is the beginning and the end" (I.3 ¶7). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **B. Gross work, then the philosophic work after dissolution.** A preliminary preparation of the matter (the "gross work") comes first. The philosophic work proper begins only after the dissolution. Hermes places the beginning of the Art in "that which is born of the crow". "The philosophic work is not considered to begin until after the dissolution; the preliminary preparation of the matter being very generally termed the gross work" (I.3 n56). Also: "My Son, that which is born of the crow is the beginning of this Art" (I.3 ¶46). — *stated (n56); that the crow-born thing is the product of the dissolution is interpreted, resting on n56's attachment to ¶46; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Hermes*

- **C. Repetition: many cycles, seven in all.** The whole process is repeated many times. Hermes gives seven repetitions before the Quintessence rises into a permanent form. In a verse the book leaves untranslated, the cycle is summed up as dissolve, coagulate, fix. "The entire process is repeated many times before perfection is arrived at" (I.3 n20). Also: "this process , according to Hermes, is repeated seven times before the final resurrection of the Quintessence into a permanent form of life" (I.3 n40); "Solve, Coagula, Fige" (I.3 n43). — *stated (n20, n40); the meaning of "Solve, Coagula, Fige" with its verse (our rendering: if you dissolve the fixed, and make the dissolved fly, and fix the flying) is interpreted from our own translation; ATWOOD*

- **1. Decompound: discharge the superfluities.** First the matter must be broken down. The many foreign "images and superfluities" that cling to it in its natural state must be fully discharged, so that the "radical element" can be found and set free. "nothing is done except that the matter be decompounded" (I.3 n4). Also: "there are many heterogeneous images and superfluities adhering to this subject in its natural state, which render it unfit for progress; these therefore must be entirely discharged" (I.3 n4); "the medium by which the radical element is discovered and set free to the accomplishment of its inclusive law" (I.3 n4). — *stated; ATWOOD (Hermes' text reads "compounded"; see Open questions)*

- **2. On the person: draw out and fix the inner light, make the outer life pliant.** Two operations are named. The inner "causal light" is drawn out by art and fixed. The outer "sensual spirit of life" is made flexible and hidden. Only then can reason enter the "Identity" in which nature's powers are understood from within. "That which is within, viz., the causal light, must be drawn forth by art and fixed; and that which is without, viz., the sensual spirit of life must be made flexible and occultated" (I.3 n8). Also: "taking what is within and fixed, thou canst have it either in earth or sea" (I.3 ¶8). — *stated; ATWOOD glossing QUOTED: Hermes*

- **3. Separate into the essential relations, then rejoin in proportion.** Divide the matter into its essential relations, purify them, and join them again in harmonious proportion. The Stone is divided and mortified "by its proper nature", which is already in it. "separate the matter into its essential relationships, and join them again together in harmonious proportion" (I.3 n23). Also: "which we ought to divide and dissever in pieces, and segregate in the limbs; and mortifying the same by its proper nature, which is also in it" (I.3 ¶26); "if thou shalt join them together, having been previously separated and well purified, will without fail, by imitating nature, yield most pure fruits" (I.3 n30, Sendivogius); "restore thou it also, to the superiors by its proper windings, and altering skillfully before collecting; then to its former red spirit let it be carefully conjoined" (I.3 ¶19). — *stated; ATWOOD glossing QUOTED: Arnold; QUOTED: Hermes, Sendivogius*

- **4. Bind the volatile so it cannot escape.** The matter is held fast so it can withstand the fire. The binding works through the affinity between the body and its own spirit. The flying, volatile part is drowned and separated from what holds it in death, so that it lives without flying off and can be governed by reason. "the philosophers bind up their matter with a strong chain, that it may contend with the Fire" (I.3 ¶29). Also: "this constriction, according to the scholiast’s teaching, is not made by chance, but by means of the affinity which is between the body and its spirit" (I.3 n37); "Take the flying volatile and drown it flying and divide and separate it from its rust, which yet holds it in death" (I.3 ¶16); "not by flying away into the regions above, but by truly forbearing to fly" (I.3 ¶16); "make that which is volatile so that it cannot fly from that which flies not" (I.3 ¶42). — *binding and affinity stated (n37). ¶16 is stated only generally ("These images, indicating the mode of rational operation with the freed spirit and its soul, will appear inevitably obscure", I.3 n20). ¶42 fixing is interpreted from its wording; QUOTED: Hermes, ATWOOD*

- **5. Constrain reason and turn it back on its own life.** Reason, the King, is held by art so that it cannot escape the "fiery ordeal". It then turns back upon the wheel of its own life, reversing the natural direction of generation, and a door opens into life's innermost recess. "This same reason, being artificially constrained, lest it should escape the fiery ordeal, returns, fastening, as Hermes says, upon the wheel of its proper life, as it were, introverting the natural channel and order of generation" (I.3 n50). Also: "being chained to the arms and breast of my mother, and to her substance, I cause my substance to keep together" (I.3 ¶39); "whereby a door is marvelously opened into the most intimate recess of life" (I.3 n50). — *n50 stated (ATWOOD); linking it to "chained" in ¶39 is interpreted; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **6. Seal the vessel, keep a continual fire.** Decoct the matter in the furnace (Athanor) with a continual fire. Seal the vessel exactly with the "Hermetic seal" so that the penetrating vapour, the spirit, cannot escape and leave a dead body. The "mountain" of the vulture is the vessel set in a furnace walled with fire. The vessel itself is presupposed and not disclosed. "let it be put into a furnace, which lute diligently, and observe that none of the spirit may escape" (I.3 ¶47). Also: "The matter, he says, is to be decocted in the philosophic furnace called Athanor, with a continual fire" (I.3 n57); "And the vessel which holds the matter must be exactly sealed, lest the penetrative mineral vapor should expire and leave the dead body" (I.3 n57); "a fit vessel placed in a well-built philosophic furnace encompassed with a wall of fire" (I.3 n11); "the master presupposes not only a knowledge of the Matter, but of the Vessel also in which it is scientifically concocted" (I.3 n22). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by QUOTED: the Scholiast; ATWOOD*

- **7. Heat and repeated "Hermetic sublimation".** The matter is boiled in a "moist fire" until the radix appears. The redness and the light part are then extracted until a third remains. The note on this passage gathers all the named operations (calcination, inceration, fixation and so on) into one Hermetic sublimation, repeated over and over. Unlike chemical sublimation, it changes and improves the matter each time. Hermes also gives periods of boiling: seven, fourteen or twenty-one days in "that which comes from the horse's belly". "Put the matter into a moist fire, therefore, and cause it to boil in order that its heat may be augmented, which destroys the siccity of the incombustible nature, until the radix shall appear" (I.3 ¶10). Also: "then extract the redness and the light part, till the third part remains" (I.3 ¶10); "the Hermetic sublimation, repeatedly operated over and over again, is the occasion of many changes in the matter and effects, which, though differently designated, are in their source the same" (I.3 n14); "the Hermetic sublimation is said to change the matte, qualifying and meliorating each time that it succeeds" (I.3 n14); "boil them in that which comes from the horse's belly for seven, fourteen, or twenty-one days" (I.3 ¶47). — *the sublimation doctrine is stated (ATWOOD). Its link to "moist fire" is interpreted (by note placement only). Reading the horse's belly as continual decoction is interpreted (n57); QUOTED: Hermes*

- **8. Dissolution: drive out vapour, blackness and death.** A Dragon lives in all three components: water, oily tincture or unguent, and faeces. Each must be purified by dissolution: vapour from the water, blackness from the oily part, death from the faeces. This radical dissolution of the spirit exposes the evil of original sin, and without that exposure the spirit cannot return to its first purity. The life in the bodies sleeps until they are dissolved into their root source. "Take away therefore the vapour from the water, and the blackness from the oily tincture, and death from the faeces: and by dissolution thou shalt achieve a triumphant reward" (I.3 ¶23). Also: "It behooves us, therefore, to drive away the vapour from the water, the blackness from the unguent, and death from the feces, and this by dissolution" (I.3 ¶56); "the evil of the original sin is said to be discovered by a radical dissolution of the spirit" (I.3 n31); "nor can it exert its powers, or show itself, unless the bodies (i.e., the sensible and vegetable media of life) be first dissolved and turned into their radical source" (I.3 n38); "by the power of the water, the composition is dissolved, it is the key of the restoration" (I.3 ¶27). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD and QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **9. Darken the higher life: death and revival by withdrawal and return of spirit.** The "brain" is ground in sharp vinegar until it is darkened. The note glosses this as the spirit's higher ("cerebral") life being darkened during purification, so that its true mineral root shows. The matter then lives again in putrefaction, its old dark clouds changed. This is repeated: it dies, and then it lives. Central heat turns water into air, which turns back into a far subtler water. "take the brain thereof and triturate it in most sharp vinegar till it becomes obscured" (I.3 ¶48). Also: "The cerebral, or superior life of the Spirit, is obscured during the purification, and for the revealment of its true mineral radix or source" (I.3 n58); "it lives in the putrefaction; the dark clouds which were in it before it died in its own body will be changed" (I.3 ¶48); "This process being repeated, as I have described; it dies again as I said, thence it lives" (I.3 ¶48); "that the central heat may change the water into air" (I.3 n31); "the air will be turned into water, far more subtle than the first water was" (I.3 n31). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD; QUOTED: an unnamed adept (n31)*

- **10. Purify by repetition until clear and white.** The redness, corrupted with water like a live coal, is withdrawn again and again until pure. The body is repeatedly opened, thinned, dissolved and putrefied. Like sown wheat, the matter ("our Magnesia") dies in the Philosophic Earth so that it can conceive itself anew. It is worked by its own nature acting on itself, as agent and patient in turn. Light and darkness, as contraries, fight unceasingly until the foreign life dies and the matter whitens. Then the internal agent returns to give life to the whole. Separating light from darkness seven times completes the philosophic mercury. "Take, my Son, this redness, corrupted with the water, which is as a live coal holding the fire, which if thou shalt withdraw so often until the redness is made pure" (I.3 ¶17). Also: "the body must be repeatedly opened and made thin after its fixation and dissolved and putrefied" (I.3 n21); "It is purifies by separation, and is dissolved, digested, and coagulated, sublimed, incerated, and fixed by the reciprocated action of its own proper Identity, as agent and patient, alternating to improve" (I.3 n21); "except one understandeth how to mortify and induce generation, to vivify the Spirit, to cleanse and introduce Light, until they fight with each other and grow white and freed from their defilements" (I.3 ¶26); "The two contrary natures of light and darkness must contend together, as it were, in mortal strife, and the war must be waged unceasingly for the destruction of the foreign life until it succumbs" (I.3 n34); "in order that the internal agent may return to vivify the whole, and yield the abundant tincture of its light" (I.3 n34); "Separate, says Eiraneaus, the light from the darkness seven times, and the creation of the philosophic mercury will be complete" (I.3 n39). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD and QUOTED: the Scholiast, Eiraneaus*

- **11. Extract the hidden unguent and the soul without destroying the life.** The true unguent is extracted by the most subtle art, without burning it. The artist removes the external forms the spirit has taken on, dissolving them without destroying the life that contains them. The soul is drawn out of the "death of the first life" as the red living tincture. "which by most subtle art and ingenuity it behooves us to extract without combustion" (I.3 ¶54). Also: "it behooves the artist to extract these, therefore, and to dissolve without destroying the continental life" (I.3 n62); "the bitterness existing in the throat occultly indicates the death of the first life, whence the soul is ediced; which is also the red and living tincture taken from the body" (I.3 n12). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **12. Distil seven times; reduce the spirit to a fixed ash.** Hermes' recipe of weights is not literal. It means the spirit is distilled seven times, and at the eighth distillation the fire turns it into an ash or very fine powder that resists fire. When the mercurial spirit has been reduced to ash "by its own fire", it generates the Stone. The extinct "coal", a dead body whose life was fire, is returned to the water for thirty days. "the spirit should be seven times distilled, which after the eighth distillation is converted by force of the fire into ashes" (I.3 n6, the Scholiast). Also: "Take of the humidity an ounce and a half, and of the Meridian Redness, that is the soul of gold, a fourth part" (I.3 ¶6); "If you have brought your mercurial spirit to ashes or burnt it by its own fire, you have, continues our informant, an incomparable treasure" (I.3 n9); "Return, then, O my son, the extinct coal to the water for thirty days" (I.3 ¶18); "The life of the coal is fire, which being extinct, becomes a dead body" (I.3 n22). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast (reported by ATWOOD); QUOTED: Hermes. The thirty days are unexplained*

- **13. Reunion: spirit, soul and body conjoined.** After separation and purification, spirit penetrates body and body fixes spirit, and the joined soul colours the whole white or red. The newly born quintessences are reunited so that they bear fruit (the king married to the "red daughter" in a gentle fire). What once nourished is now itself nourished, by a gentle heat like the heavenly fire, not ordinary boiling. Active and passive are conjoined to reproduce light. The whitened water turns red by conjunction with its own prepared spirit. "the spirit thereafter penetrates the body, and the body fixes the spirit --- the soul being conjoined, tinges the whole of its proper color, whether white or red" (I.3 n21). Also: "when we marry the crowned king to our red daughter, and in a gentle fire, not hurtful, she doth conceive a son" (I.3 ¶34); "The new-born Quintessences are here shown to be reunited for fructification and to be further promoted" (I.3 n41); "It is nourished with a gentle heat, not in the vulgar way of decocting, but conformable to the heavenly fire" (I.3 n41); "join the son to Buba, which is Jupiter and a hidden secret" (I.3 ¶37) with "the conjunction of active and passive principles for the reproduction of light out of the whole" (I.3 n47); "By a conjunction with its own permanent prepared spirit, the albified water is made red" (I.3 n54). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD (n21 voice UNCLEAR)*

- **14. Sow the seed in its matrix (the farming analogy).** The seed of gold, which is light, must be planted in its own prepared ethereal medium. In the New Light's account, "our lune" (not common silver) receives the seed. It is kept under continual fire for seven, sometimes ten, philosophical months until the water consumes three parts and leaves one. It is then fed with "the milk of the earth" and kept from rotting by "the salt of nature". The seed of the new generation, put back into its matrix, makes it a thousand times fitter. "needs only to be planted in its proper ethereal vehicle, well prepared and fallow to bring forth its virtue in manifold increase" (I.3 n40). Also: "our lune or silver, not common silver, is that which receives the seed of the gold, afterwards it is governed by our continual fire for seven months (philosophical), and sometimes ten" (I.3 n40); "Then it is nourished with the milk of the earth, or the fatness thereof, which is bred in the bowels of the earth and is governed and preserved from putrefaction by the salt of nature" (I.3 n40). — *the roles are stated by the New Light author, cited by ATWOOD. What lune, milk and salt are is unexplained (interpreted only as roles)*

- **15. Ferment.** Sublime and purify the body, separate the impurity from the dregs, rejoin and mix the parts, add the ferment, and work earth and water together until the Ixir ferments like dough. The ferment must be of a kindred nature. The fermenting light grows by constant addition of spirit. The gold of the wise is digested with a "fiery water" to make Ixir, in a "temperate", not "equal", composition. "when thou hast sublimed the body and purified it, separating the uncleanness from the faeces" (I.3 ¶58). Also: "thou wilt then conjoin and mix them together, and put in them the ferment confecting the earth with the water until the Ixir ferment even as dough ferments" (I.3 ¶58); "The fermenting light by constant addition of the spirit, leavens more and more" (I.3 n45); "The gold of the wise, concocted and well digested, with a fiery water, makes Ixir" (I.3 ¶61); "in a temperate composition is the ferment Ixir, and contrariwise, becomes distempered by an equal composition" (I.3 ¶61). — *stated (¶58, n45); the "fiery water" and the contrast of "temperate" with "equal" are unexplained; QUOTED: Hermes, ATWOOD*

- **16. A new death, then multiplication.** Once the internal light is manifest, it must be led into another life, suffer and die again, so as to convert the gross material into itself. Hermes' "Burn then the Brazen Body" is set beside the doctrine that nature's self-willed hypostasis must die. Then comes multiplication: dissolving and coagulating the medicine again in the mercurial water it was made from increases its power each time. For projection on metals, it must first be fermented with pure gold (for red) or silver (for white). "The internal light, once made manifest to sense, so far is ready for the perscrutination of another life into which it must be induced toenter, to suffer again, and die in order to transmute the foul material into itself" (I.3 n44). Also: "Burn then the Brazen Body with an exceeding great fire" (I.3 ¶42); "The self-willed hypostasis of nature must die, as we before explained, in order to evolve her universal being" (I.3 n53); "And by how much the oftener the medicine is dissolved, by so much more it increases in virtue" (I.3 n38); "if this medicine be multiplied and be again dissolved and coagulated by the water of its mercury, white or red, of which it was prepared, then the tinging virtue will be augmented each time by ten degrees" (I.3 n70); "if the intention be to change metals into gold, it is requisite they should be first fermented with the most pure gold" (I.3 n70). — *n44, n38 and n70 are stated (ATWOOD; QUOTED: the Scholiast; QUOTED: the adept). Equating the Brazen Body with the self-willed hypostasis is interpreted (note placement only)*

- **Unexplained operative instructions (recorded, not decoded in this chapter).** "it behooves us to mortify two Argent vives together, and to venerate the Argent vive of Auripigment, and the oriental Argent vive of Magnesia" (I.3 ¶32). "with this watery soul, in order to possess ourselves of the sulphurous Form, to mingle the same with our Acetum" (I.3 ¶27). "when thou shalt send forth fire upon the foliated sulphur, the boundary of hearts doth enter in above it, let it be washed from the same, and the refined matter thereof be extracted" (I.3 ¶34). The image of the old man who swallows gold or silver, is burned, and has his ashes boiled into "a medicine to cure the leprosy (of life)" (I.3 n31). — *QUOTED: Hermes; QUOTED: an unnamed adept. Unexplained here*

## Signs (what shows the work is proceeding)

- **The colours must be fully passed.** The work is not perfected until it has gone completely through its colours. The vulture's cry gives the order black → white → red → citrine. Hermes' "seven letters" are the necessary phases of the material, by colour and quality, which some call planets and others metals. The colours are also given as three red and three white spirits growing from one water. "it is not perfected unless the colours be thoroughly passed and accomplished" (I.3 ¶5). Also: "I am the White of the Black, and the Red of the White, and the Citrine of the Red" (I.3 ¶9); "The seven letters are taken to signify the necessary phases which the philosophic material passes in order of color and qualitative virtue" (I.3 n47); "into three red spirits and three white, which have their growth all from the same identical water" (I.3 n5); "making the light manifest without refraction in every variety of its coloring and creative imagination" (I.3 n11). — *stated (n47); the colour sequence is the vulture's cry in ¶9, and reading it as an order of stages is interpreted; QUOTED: Hermes, ATWOOD, QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **Blackness: putrefaction, the gate.** The black "night" is the putrefaction of the spirit, and the "day" is its resurrection to relative purity. Blackness is the necessary passage to the change: to know it is to know everything, because putrefaction comes before every new form. The darkness that follows the light is "the mother of all things". The crow "flies without wings" because the fixed spirit carries it. "O happy gate of blackness, cries the sage, which art the passage to this so glorious a change" (I.3 n42). Also: "The blackness of the night is the putrefaction of the same, and the clearness of the day signifies its resurrection to a state of comparative purity" (I.3 n12); "for to know this is to know all, but to be ignorant of this is to be ignorant of all" (I.3 n42); "For putrefaction precedes the generation of every new form into existence" (I.3 n42); "then it generates and brings forth the black clouds or darkness, which is the mother of all things" (I.3 ¶33); "IT flies without wings, being borne and carried by the fixed spirit" (I.3 n12). — *stated; QUOTED: the sage (endorsed by ATWOOD); ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The dissolving Dragon.** The matter takes on the image of a self-devouring dragon at the dissolution. "Then it becomes the Dragon eating his own wings and destroying himself" (I.3 ¶47). — *interpreted (n57 says the paragraph goes "over again" the dissolution; the dragon image itself is not glossed here); QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Luna wanes and waxes.** The passive ethereal light shrinks and then grows in the work, as the moon does. "Luna after fifteen days is diminished; and in the third, she is augmented" (I.3 ¶7). — *stated for Luna (n7); the numbers are unexplained; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The crowned king and unseen colours: the white elixir.** The "crowned king" is the first visible radiance of the vital tincture. When the exaltations return and continue on the body, colours appear such as were never seen. The king has triumphed over death, and the operator may rest and enjoy the white elixir. "By the crowned king, Hermes signifies the first manifested resplendence of the vital tincture" (I.3 n22). Also: "light shall begin to appear with such admirable colors as never were seen by the eye of man in so little a room before" (I.3 n22, the adept); "Here thou mayest rest and wait, and enjoy the glory of thy white elixir" (I.3 n22). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: an unnamed adept*

- **Whitening before reddening.** The contraries "grow white". The "virgin's milk is whitened". The ferment whitens the mixture. The fermenting light is white, and the silvery spirit shows itself, before the solar red. "the fermentive light is indeed white before the multiplication of its internal form has rubified it, and the silvery spirit is made manifest before the solar ray" (I.3 n67). Also: "the virgin's milk is whitened" (I.3 ¶35); "the ferment whitens the confection and hinders it from combustion" (I.3 ¶58). — *stated (n67); the virgin's milk is interpreted (unglossed); ATWOOD, QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The Dragon withdraws as the inner light rises.** "The Dragon, who watches the crevices, shuns the sunbeams" (I.3 ¶35). Also: "The nature and origin of this Dragon was before discussed, which becomes so occultated in the rising of the internal light to manifestation" (I.3 n43). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Inner evil exposed: corruption, then improvement.** At this point the innate evil becomes visible, and the will then carries out its own dissolution. Corruption appears first and improvement after, and both are marks that the art is working. "The destroyers are the renovators, and hence the corruption is made manifest in the matter to be renewed, and hence the melioration will appear and each side is a signal of Art" (I.3 ¶44). Also: "when the innate evil being made manifest, the will proceeds to operate its proper solution in life" (I.3 n53). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; ATWOOD*

- **The new light at first terrifies.** When the new light appears to the outer life of the spirit, it is not welcome or sweet. The outer life's wrathful properties try to swallow it, until they are weakened and lose their own will. "The colour of the golden matter points to redness, and the nature thereof, is not sweetness" (I.3 ¶59). Also: "The apparition of the new light to the outward qualifications of the spirit is not welcome or sweet at first, but causes a terrification of the whole circumferential life" (I.3 n69); "so that they lose at length their own will, strength and predominance" (I.3 n69, the theosophist). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD and QUOTED: the theosophist*

- **The decisive sign: the masculine tincture rises out of the black, united with the white.** When the masculine tincture rises from death and comes out of the black darkness joined with the white virginal spirit, the artist knows he has the great Arcanum. Under the fire the material, pictured as Venus or Paphia, throws aside a "glaucus veil" and appears golden. "come forth out of the black darkness together in union with the white virginal spirit, he will then know that he hath the great Arcanum of the world" (I.3 n53). Also: "throwing the glaucus veil aside, she shall appear entirely golden" (I.3 n53, Democritus). — *stated; QUOTED: a more modern experimentalist; the Democritus image is interpreted (given as an operative image of "this juncture", with no further gloss)*

- **Red as flesh: the tincture as coagulated blood.** The fire purges its carrier, and a pure red tincture the colour of flesh and blood appears in the residue. This tincture is coagulated blood, the "boundary of hearts": the thing sought, which satisfies once attained. Blackness changes into true red. The son is clothed in red and purple, and death's reign is over. "our tincture is of blood coagulated, which blood is the boundary or satisfaction of hearts, as Hermes alludes, the object sought for, and which satisfies when attained" (I.3 n42). Also: "there appears at length in the faeces, a most pure and rubicund tincture of the color of flesh and blood" (I.3 n42); "his tincture by help of the fire remains red, as flesh" (I.3 ¶34); "the colourings of its tincture and blackness is then changed into the true red" (I.3 ¶43); "now he is invested with the red garment, and the purple is put on" (I.3 ¶35). — *stated (n42, ¶43); reading the red garment as the final reddening is interpreted; QUOTED: the adept, ATWOOD, QUOTED: Hermes*

- **"joyful signs": what fixes its own body.** When the revival is reached, what was sought becomes visible. "I have even related to thee the joyful signs, that which doth fix its own body" (I.3 ¶49). — *interpreted (the signs are not described here); QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Rapid colour change under moderate fire (Maria).** With only a moderate fire, the composition passes quickly from colour to colour to the perfect red or white. It leaves a clear body like a pearl, or like wild poppy mixed with white, which melts, penetrates and transmutes. "it is wonderful to see how, in less than an hour, the composition will pass from one color to another, till it comes to the perfect red or white" (I.3 n67). Also: "there will appear in it a body clear, shining like a pearl or the color of wild poppy mingled with white" (I.3 n67). — *stated; QUOTED: Maria*

## Impediments and dangers

- **The Dragon: the self-willed spirit, a deadly poison.** A Dragon lives in the components and rises by their blackness. It is the self-willed spirit drawn outward into nature by the fall into generation. In operation it is the blackness of the matter on its first ascent, a thick, sticky body that is hard to resolve into vapour and that, like poison, destroys whatever it touches. While the "fume" remains, the components are not immortal. "The dragon is the self-willed spirit, which is externally derived into nature, by the fall into generation" (I.3 n31). Also: "But a Dragon inhabits all these, and are his habitation; and the blackness is in them, and by it he ascends into the air" (I.3 ¶23); "But, whilst the fume remains in them, they are not immortal" (I.3 ¶23); "Hermes especially signifies the blackness of the matter on its first ascension, which is operated with difficulty on account of its thick glutinous body" (I.3 n31); "for the root of this science is a deadly poison" (I.3 n31). — *stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: the Scholiast; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Clinging superfluities and the shadow that goes with the body.** Foreign images and superfluities cling to the subject in its natural state and make it unfit for progress. A shadowy darkness travels with the philosophic body, clouding its own light, until it is cleansed of sensual defilement. "A shadowy darkness passes always along with the philosophic body, moving in its own light until it is thoroughly purified from sensual defilements" (I.3 n21). Also: "Extract from the ray its shadow and impurity by which the clouds hang over it, defile and keep away the light" (I.3 ¶17); "which render it unfit for progress" (I.3 n4). — *stated (n4, n21). Linking n21 to ¶17 is by note placement; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Literalism: taking fire, sulfur, mercury and unguent as common substances.** The chief error is believing there is no fire but common fire and no sulfur or mercury but the ordinary ones. Recipes using fats, hair, verdigris, tragacanth and bones are to be thrown out. The triad is not "kitchen-stuff". "men neither see nor do they believe there is any other fire but the common fire, nor any other sulfur or mercury --- thus they are deceived by their own opinions" (I.3 n41). Also: "Away the, O sons of Science, with the unguents extracted from fats, hair, verdigrease, tragacanth, and bones" (I.3 ¶53); "lest in the outward acceptation of words or things he should be deceived" (I.3 n19); "not of kitchen-stuff, those three pot-principles of water, oil, and earth" (I.3 n30). — *stated; QUOTED: the adept; QUOTED: Hermes; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Vaughan*

- **Experiment without theory.** Without theoretic knowledge and a right starting principle, people wear themselves out in experiments (see Operator). "many have wearied themselves in experimenting" (I.3 n2). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **The false finish: Venus's light alone.** When Venus, the light, first appears, the artist may think the work is done. But that light is imperfect, alone and passing until the masculine tincture fixes it. "the artist is rejoiced, and thinks perhaps his work is finished, and that he has the treasure of the world in hand; but it is not so" (I.3 n49). Also: "the light still be found imperfect, alone, and transient, without the masculine tincture to fix it in manifestation" (I.3 n49). — *stated; QUOTED: a no less experienced adept (cited by ATWOOD)*

- **Escape of the spirit leaves a dead body.** If the penetrating vapour escapes the vessel, only the dead body remains. The water held "in a pure vessel" keeps the spirits from flying off the bodies. "lest the penetrative mineral vapor should expire and leave the dead body" (I.3 n57). Also: "lest the spirits should fly away from the bodies" (I.3 ¶26). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Destroying instead of dissolving.** The unguent must be extracted "without combustion". The external forms must be dissolved "without destroying the continental life". "to extract without combustion" (I.3 ¶54). Also: "to dissolve without destroying the continental life" (I.3 n62). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, ATWOOD*

- **Negligence and a false idea of the matter.** Carelessness and a wrong opinion of what the matter is spoil the operation, like bad leaven, through not understanding fermentation. "through negligence and a false opinion of the matter, the operation is perverted, as bad leaven on the dough" (I.3 ¶58). Also: "without understanding the proper method and matter of fermentation" (I.3 n68). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; ATWOOD*

- **The unworthy operator, and abuse of power.** In the vicious, the art becomes power for evil. Breaking the Law of the work turns good to evil. Even the successful adept is tempted to misuse it (see Operator). "lest they should be made powerful in evil for the perpetuation of sinful things" (I.3 ¶11). Also: "If one evil man had hereof his will, All Christian peace he might easily spill" (I.3 n15, Norton). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; QUOTED: Norton*

- **Deliberate concealment in the texts.** Hermes hides the matter by circumlocution and gives one thing opposite names. The alchemists everywhere veil by repetition. The treatise never says where, when or how the Matter is taken, and it hides the vessel and apparatus. "I have termed this dissolved, and this joined, this nearest I have termed furthest off" (I.3 ¶46). Also: "The manner of obscuring the truth, by repetitions and circumlocutions, has been everywhere adopted by the Alchemists" (I.3 n56); "neither the does Hermes discover the true Art, either whence, when or how the Matter is to be taken" (I.3 n71); "the philosophic vessel, with the whole apparatus for working the Spirit to perfection, is wrapped up under an ambiguous disguise" (I.3 n71). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; ATWOOD*

- **Projection without prior fermentation.** Imperfect metals cannot bear the medicine's subtlety unless it is first fermented with pure gold. Otherwise there is loss in projection. "otherwise the imperfect metals would not be able to support its too great and supreme subtility; but there would ensue loss and damage in the projection" (I.3 n70). — *stated; QUOTED: the adept*

## The result

- **The many-coloured Stone: the elixir of Light.** The final result is the complete union of the purified spirit "with its course". It is a tincture that renews itself through dissolution and survives its own death: the "elixir of Light" freed from the central essence. It is said to lengthen life and to cure disease, moral excess and bodily defects. It leads from darkness into light. "This, O son, is the concealed Stone of many colours; which is born in one colour" (I.3 ¶13). Also: "The consummate union of the purified spirit with its course is thus covertly indicated by Hermes as the true cornerstone of his philosophy" (I.3 n18); "that tincture of many dyes which, being dissolved renews itself, and dying survives itself" (I.3 n18); "This is the elixir of Light from the central essence, so set free, that it is said to prolong life, and cure disease and moral indulgence and physical defects" (I.3 n18); "It leads from darkness into light, from this desert wilderness to a secure habitation" (I.3 ¶13). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Spotless gold, the Universal Ferment; perhaps Light given substance.** The Stone is perfectly tempered, incorruptible gold, the citrine Universal Ferment that sets all things right. Atwood asks whether it is Light given its own substance and raised by fermentation into an unchanging magnet that draws nature into accord with itself. "The Stone, therefore, is the most precious gold without spots --- evenly tempered, which neither fire nor air, nor water, nor earth is able to corrupt" (I.3 ¶60). Also: "the Universal Ferment rectifying all things by its composition, which is of the yellow or true citrine colour" (I.3 ¶60); "is Light essentialized in its proper substance, and exalted by fermentation into an immutable magnet, able to draw and convert the radical homogeneity of nature into its own assimilative accord?" (I.3 n70). — *¶60 stated (QUOTED: Hermes). n70 is ATWOOD's proposal, phrased as a question ("an ultimate promise only")*

- **Botri: the Stone from the citrine of the red.** "Botri is made from the Citrine, which is extracted out of the Red, and from nothing else" (I.3 ¶41). Also: "By the term Botri is here signified the Philosopher’s Stone" (I.3 n52). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD (the Citrine is not explained)*

- **The son: quintessential nature in person.** The "son" born of the conjunction is the whole quintessential nature gathered into one, as if personified. He carries the golden light of life to make it universal, and he is himself the treasury. "For this son is himself the treasury, even himself bearing the Philosophic Matter" (I.3 ¶35). Also: "is he not in truth the whole quintessential nature concentrated, as it were personified, bearing in hand the golden light of life to perpetualize it universally" (I.3 n43); "for the reign of death is finished, and the son doth rule" (I.3 ¶35). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **The mercurial ash that generates the Stone.** The mercurial spirit, burnt to ash by its own fire, generates the Stone and is born from it. It converts other metals and puts tincture and fixity on them. "for this is that which generates the Stone, and is born of it" (I.3 n9). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **Tingeing and endless multiplication.** Revived bodies pass their tinging power to imperfect bodies and make them permanent. The medicine tinges 10, then 100, then 1000 parts, gaining power with each dissolution: an inexhaustible fountain. The auripigment drawn from the well is the Stone's multiplying power. "the dead elements are revived, the composed bodies tinge and are altered, and operate wonderful works which are permanent" (I.3 ¶30). Also: "by the abundance of their internal light, they communicate their tinging property to other imperfect bodies, transmuting them into a fixed and permanent substantive" (I.3 n38); "one part thereof will tinge ten parts of an imperfect body, then one hundred, then a thousand, and so infinitely on" (I.3 n38); "Here there is a celestial and divine fountain set open, which no man is able to draw dry" (I.3 n38). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes; QUOTED: the Scholiast (adopted by ATWOOD)*

- **The King's seal: universal reason stamped on regenerated life.** "with the king's seal we tinge the clay" (I.3 ¶59). Also: "By the King’s seal, Hermes signifies the great Law of Light or universal reason, which is finally impressed upon the regenerated vitality of nature" (I.3 n69). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD*

- **Inner knowledge: the causal principle recalled and experienced.** Through the art's deliberate loosening of the bond of life, the causal principle of nature is brought back into memory ("reminiscence") and experienced in the renewed life. Reason enters the "Identity" where nature's powers are understood from within. A door opens into life's innermost recess. The Stone gives "divine things" to the intelligent. Possessing it brings "the sovereign philosophy and secret of all hidden things". "By an artificial dissolution of the vital bond, by means of Alchemy, the Causal principle of nature is said to be developed into reminiscence and to arise in the experience of the recreated life" (I.3 n64). Also: "before reason can become into that Identity by which the powers of the Universal Nature are made manifest and intrinsically understood" (I.3 n8); "whereby a door is marvelously opened into the most intimate recess of life" (I.3 n50); "and what they ask about it I make to know and understand and to possess divine things" (I.3 ¶37); "Which being done we have the sovereign philosophy and secret of all hidden things" (I.3 ¶56); "I compose the invisible from the visible, making the occult matter to appear" (I.3 ¶39). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The Key: what the ferment does.** The ferment whitens, prevents burning, holds the tincture so it does not fly, and makes the bodies penetrate and join one another. This is called the Key and the end of the work. "and makes them intimately to be joined and to enter one into another, and this is the Key of the philosophers and the end of their works" (I.3 ¶58). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Benefits along the way: health, knowledge, riches.** The art gives intermediate benefits. The Stone cures illness and keeps health to the end of life, tinges metals into silver and gold, and turns crystals into gems. The universal medicine is concreted from things rich in the "balsam of nature" (fixed sulfur). "the art offers many intermediate benefits by the way, alluring health, science and riches, of her mineral stores" (I.3 n70). Also: "Our stone, says the adept, drives away and cures all sorts of maladies whatever, and preserves any one in good health to the last term of his life" (I.3 n70); "From things, therefore, abounding with a balsam of this kind, the universal medicine is concreted" (I.3 n61); "By this, the Almighty favouring, the greatest diseases are escaped" (I.3 ¶13). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: the adept; QUOTED: the Scholiast; QUOTED: Hermes*

## Principles (general laws of the work)

- **Read spiritually ("ethereally"), not bodily.** The elements are not physical bodies but properties of the Universal Spirit hidden in all existence. They are known only by the Spirit's own inner analysis and "introverted light". Everything is to be understood ethereally. "the knowledge of the four elements of the ancient philosophers was not corporally or imprudently sought after" (I.3 ¶5). Also: "non corporaliter, as the Scholiast explains, sed spiritualiter et sapienter, --- not corporally but spiritually and wisely" (I.3 n4); "the properties of the Universal Spirit are abstrusely included in all existence, and to be understood only by its own intimate analysis and introverted light" (I.3 n4); "All here is to be understood ethereally, according to the principles before laid down" (I.3 n49). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes, glossed by ATWOOD (book's own translation)*

- **Death before life: reduce a thing to its origin to reveal it.** A thing's true original shows only when the thing is reduced back into it. The "catholic germ" in man comes alive only after it dies. Putrefaction comes before every new form, and the destroyers are the renewers. "the true original cannot be made manifest except they be reduced into it" (I.3 n8). Also: "is not quickened except it die" (I.3 n8); "The destroyers are the renovators" (I.3 ¶44); "For putrefaction precedes the generation of every new form into existence" (I.3 n42). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Death is the withdrawal of spirit; life is its return.** The work is done "with the spirits". The matter dies when the spirit is taken away and lives when it returns. "for as it dies by the taking away of the spirit, so it lives in the return and is revived and rejoices in them" (I.3 ¶49). Also: "In the life and death thereof we work with the spirits" (I.3 ¶49). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Like from like; the ferment must be kindred.** The product is of the same kind as its source. A thing is raised only by the ferment of its own more advanced virtue; otherwise it corrupts. The Stone grows by fermentation within its own kind, as leaven makes leaven. "There is not from man anything but what is like him; nor from the ox and bullock" (I.3 ¶38). Also: "observe, also, that there is no ferment otherwise than from a kindred nature" (I.3 ¶58); "as leaven makes leaven, and every ferment begets its own exaltation" (I.3 n55); "except the past of flour be leavened, or the liquor receive the ferment of its own advanced virtue, it will not be exalted; but die and corrupt in the inferior elements of its nature" (I.3 n66). — *stated (¶58, n55, n66). Applying ¶38 to the material is interpreted; QUOTED: Hermes, ATWOOD, QUOTED: the Scholiast*

- **Self-action: the matter works on itself.** The matter is mortified "by its proper nature, which is also in it". It is purified by its own Identity acting in turn as agent and patient. Its own sulfur suffices (see Subject). "fixed by the reciprocated action of its own proper Identity, as agent and patient, alternating to improve" (I.3 n21). Also: "mortifying the same by its proper nature, which is also in it" (I.3 ¶26). — *stated; QUOTED: the Scholiast; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Repetition improves rather than exhausts.** Each Hermetic sublimation improves the matter and pushes its life to the utmost. Unlike ordinary farming, each return of the ethereal seed enriches its soil. Each dissolution strengthens the medicine. "the ethereal seed, on the other hand, tends always to improve its generation, fertilizing by the return of each successive growth, and enriching its maternal soil" (I.3 n40). Also: "urging on life, as it were, to the utmost exercise of vivacity, to save itself from death and a total disseveration" (I.3 n14). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **Active and passive need a medium.** Masculine and feminine, heaven and earth, need each other, and the precepts require a middle term between them (fire or unguent between faeces and water; Water between Heaven and Earth). "Each needs the other; for the precepts demand a medium" (I.3 ¶23). Also: "the masculine truly is the heaven of the feminine, and the feminine is the earth of the masculine" (I.3 ¶22). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Sulfur as seed, mercury as the earth that conceives.** The purified, fixed sulfur is the generating seed. Mercury, the renewed passive body of the spirit, is "the earth of the wise", which conceives, nourishes and brings the seed to visible birth. "The purified sulfur, fixed and incombustible, is the generating seed of the universal nature, according to the adepts" (I.3 n29). Also: "the mercury (which is the recreated body of the spirit, passive and pure) is sometimes called the earth of the wise" (I.3 n29). — *stated as the adepts' doctrine. Applying it to ¶22's masculine/feminine is interpreted (n29 calls the son's allusion "particular"); ATWOOD*

- **The threefold law of life.** Universal nature necessarily advances under a threefold law: ingress, egress and alternating action, with first one principle dominant and then another. These make up all the phenomena of the process. Agent, patient and offspring are three in one and found in everything. "she operates her proper progress necessitously under the threefold law of life; the ingress, egress, and alternatig action of which, under dominance of either of its principles, constitutes the whole phenomena of the Hermetic process" (I.3 n35). Also: "so these three are found to be in all created things imitatively, the paternal, maternal, and proceeding ens of life" (I.3 n30). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **The art is a scientific "association of the Spirit".** The art is summed up as the skilled joining together of the Spirit. "In a scientific association of the Spirit the Hermetic Art has been said summarily to consist" (I.3 n59). Also: "even I have associated the Spirit" (I.3 ¶50). — *stated; ATWOOD; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **Tinging and fixing come from holding spirit in body.** Sulphur fixes and holds the volatile. When the spirits are kept with the bodies they become tinging and fixed. Spirits joined to washed bodies are never separated from them again. "for, by this means they are made tinging and fixed" (I.3 ¶26). Also: "a conjunction of fugitives with sulphurs and albumninous bodies, which hold also and detain the fugitive Ens" (I.3 ¶21); "nor are they separated any more from them" (I.3 ¶29). — *stated; QUOTED: Hermes*

- **The operator's weights added to nature's.** The "Weights of the Wise" are the true philosophic proportions. If the operator knows how to add his own weights to nature's ("double mercury and triple sulfur"), the work ends quickly in good, then better, then best. "if thou shalt know to add thy weights to the weights of nature, to double mercury and triple sulfur, it will quickly be terminated in good, then in better, until into best of all" (I.3 n5). Also: "the two third waters are the Weights of the Wise" (I.3 ¶6); "the true philosophic proportions called also by Hermes the Weights of the Wise" (I.3 n6). — *stated; ATWOOD (following the Scholiast); QUOTED: Hermes (the text does not say whether the two "weights" are the same)*

- **Learn the method from nature by analogy, above all farming.** Watching nature lets one judge the right method by analogy and cooperate with nature's Spirit to regenerate it. The comparison with agriculture almost points out the method. "The Alchemists uniformly recommend us to observe nature, that from analogy we may be better able to imagine and judge of the proper method of experimenting, and learn to cooperate with her Spirit effectually to regenerate it" (I.3 n27). Also: "the analogy, indeed, appears to bear throughout so intimately as to suggest and, almost without deviation, point out the method of its application" (I.3 n40). — *stated; ATWOOD*

- **Self-Knowledge as the method's aim.** The vital light lies hidden at nature's centre until it is drawn out. Freed and turned back inward, it may "meet itself" in a deeper experience. This is the Hermetic method of Self-Knowledge, the only way to know divine things, and Atwood postpones discussing it. "The vital light, as we have before explained, is centrally hidden in nature until it is drawn forth; but re-entering from without inwards, when freed again, will it not then probably meet itself in a yet more profound experience?" (I.3 n46). Also: "inquire into the Hermetic method of Self-Knowledge, which alone can enable man to know and understand and possess divine things" (I.3 n46). — *stated; ATWOOD*

## Open questions

- **Compounded or decompounded?** Hermes' text reads "nothing is done except it be compounded" (I.3 ¶5). Atwood's note on the passage reads "nothing is done except that the matter be decompounded" (I.3 n4). (a) The work requires composition of the parts. (b) The matter must first be broken down and freed of superfluities. Basis: the two passages differ, and the chapter does not say whether this is a variant or a misprint. Reading (b) fits n4's whole argument, while the later conjunction stages fit (a) as a later step.

- **"one to two, and three to one"** (I.3 ¶6). (a) One divides into two (agent and patient), and their union yields three (body, soul, spirit), per I.3 n5. (b) One part is divided into two and three parts are added to each, giving the philosophic proportions, per I.3 n6 ("by the former section the one part is divided into two, to each of which there are added three parts"). The notes do not reconcile the two.

- **Is the work done on the operator or on a material?** n8, n19, n46, n50, n51 and n64 frame the work as inward: seeking the Universal Nature "within themselves", the causal light drawn out and the sensual spirit made pliant, reason turned back on its own life, "Self-Knowledge", "reminiscence". Other passages speak in laboratory terms: furnace, sealed vessel, continual fire (n57), projection on metals with gold ferment and drachm quantities (n70), Maria's hour-long colour change (n67). In n65 Atwood admits both readings of the "seven bodies" but leans to the vital ("these things are not so much denoted of the paragraph which refers to the spontaneous operations in the divine Law in life"). In n71 she says she was led by "a certain experimental clue", which she does not name. The chapter does not settle how the two registers relate.

- **The vessel and its relation to the matter.** Hermes "has chosen to conceal the philosophic vessel" (I.3 n48). The master "presupposes" knowledge of it (I.3 n22). It is "wrapped up under an ambiguous disguise" (I.3 n71). n48 answers with Maria's "found in all bodies" and the Scholiast's "innermost chamber of life", which are the same words n9 uses of the Argent vive. Whether vessel and matter are one is not stated.

- **The "moist fire" and the "horse's belly".** The moist fire (I.3 ¶10) is not explained, and the philosophic fire is deferred (I.3 n14). For "boil them in that which comes from the horse's belly" (I.3 ¶47): (a) a source of steady heat, since n57 summarises the instruction as decoction "with a continual fire"; (b) a liquid medium, from the grammar "boil them in". Not resolved.

- **Which agent is external, which internal?** "the fire, and the heavenly Flyer" (I.3 ¶11) are called "the external and internal agents" (I.3 n16) without a one-to-one assignment. Word order suggests fire = external and bird = internal, but this is not stated.

- **"The consummate union of the purified spirit with its course"** (I.3 n18). (a) Union with its path or process. (b) Union with its body or matter, which fits "the revivified spirit is united with its solar ferment" (I.3 n12) and "spirit, soul, and body" (I.3 n13) only indirectly. "course" may be a misprint; the text gives no gloss.

- **Masculine/feminine = sulfur/mercury?** I.3 n29, the note to ¶22, gives the doctrine of sulfur as seed and mercury as "earth of the wise". But it adds "the son’s allusion is intimate to the art, and particular", which suggests a more specific operative sense that is not disclosed.

- **Mapping the "three from two".** Water, oily tincture and faeces (I.3 ¶23) are linked to Salt, Sulfur and Mercury, or body, spirit and soul, only by n30's placement. No item-by-item correspondence is given. n21's water = spirit, redness = soul, earth = body is a separate triad from a different figure.

- **Venus's "brother" and the King's "mother"** (I.3 ¶39). The brother may be (a) the masculine tincture that fixes her light (n49), (b) the King who speaks next, or (c) Mars of the fable (n49). The mother may be (a) Venus, the preceding speaker, or (b) the spirit's "first source" (n50). Neither is identified.

- **"Buba, which is Jupiter"; Alpha, Yda, Liber, Sol** (I.3 ¶37). The grammar leaves open whether Buba or the joining is "Jupiter and a hidden secret". n47 glosses only the general sense (active–passive conjunction; seven colour phases). The names are not decoded.

- **The numbers.** "Luna after fifteen days is diminished; and in the third, she is augmented" (I.3 ¶7), "the vine of the wise is drawn forth in three, and the wine thereof is perfected in thirty" (I.3 ¶6), "thirty days" (I.3 ¶18), "seven, fourteen, or twenty-one days" (I.3 ¶47), "seven months (philosophical), and sometimes ten" (I.3 n40). They may be literal durations or "variously perplexed forms and measures" (I.3 n6). Only "seven" gets a gloss: seven distillations (n6) and seven repetitions (n39, n40).

- **"temperate" versus "equal" composition** (I.3 ¶61). (a) The right proportion versus mere equal quantities. (b) A verbal clash with gold's "equalised" complexion (I.3 ¶58) and the Stone "evenly tempered" (I.3 ¶60). No gloss.

- **"the work begins from the vegetable, next from the animal, as in the egg of a hen"** (I.3 ¶62). (a) A sequence of starting materials. (b) Stages of growth by analogy, in line with the seed, dough and egg analogies (n26, n40, n63, ¶58). Unglossed.

- **The brother in "his brother’s knowledging"** (I.3 n17). No one can work alone "for reasons hereafter explicable". Whether the "brother" is a human companion or a principle of the work is not said.

- **Unexplained operative names.** Acetum (I.3 ¶27). The two Argent vives to be mortified, of Auripigment and of Magnesia (I.3 ¶32). Foliated sulphur (I.3 ¶34). Cambar (I.3 ¶42). The Citrine from which Botri is made (I.3 ¶41). The encaustic (I.3 ¶59). The "fiery water" (I.3 ¶61). "earth or sea" (I.3 ¶8). The "radix" (I.3 ¶10), possibly n4's "radical element", though the chapter does not link them. "our lune", the "milk of the earth" and the "salt of nature" (I.3 n40). The "rust" (I.3 ¶16). The "fountain" of ¶18, identified with n22's "well" only by note placement.

- **The status of Atwood's definition of the Stone** (I.3 n70: "is Light essentialized in its proper substance, and exalted by fermentation into an immutable magnet"). (a) Her own conclusion, phrased rhetorically. (b) A tentative proposal. Basis: it is phrased as a question and followed by "Yet this is an ultimate promise only".

- **Deferred topics that bear on the method.** The philosophic fire's "artificial excitation" (I.3 n14). The "Rational Ferment" (I.3 n32). The "Intellectual Ground" (I.3 n10). The reasons no one operates alone (I.3 n17). The "Hermetic method of Self-Knowledge" (I.3 n46). The fable of Mars and Venus "in the last extremity of life" (I.3 n49). Comment on the inner work of n8 ("we defer our comments"). Hermes' veil, like Moses', is "hardly expedient to look through at this stage" (I.3 n40).
