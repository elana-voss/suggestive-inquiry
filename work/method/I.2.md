# Method material — I.2 Of the Theory of Transmutation in General, and of the First Matter

## The subject (what the work is done on)

1. **One root substance underlies all things.** All natures, metals included, share one homogeneous root substance and can be reduced to it. The chapter calls it the mercurial first matter, the Universal Subject or Matter, and the radical moisture. Ordinary analysis cannot find it.
   - "the homogeneity of the radical substance of things" (I.2 ¶3)
   - also: "their common basis or mercurial first matter" (I.2 ¶3); "the subject-matter of these metals, the radical moisture of which they are uniformly composed" (I.2 ¶4); "nor does ordinary analysis at all discover this Universal Matter of the adepts" (I.2 ¶3)
   - stated; ATWOOD

2. **The subject is in every body, so nothing is borrowed from gold.** Every body already holds the passive principles the Light works on. No gold or silver has to be supplied as an ingredient. Atwood says this corrects the error that alchemists extracted the Form from metals.
   - "every body hath passive principles in itself for this Light to work upon, and therefore needs not to borrow any from gold or silver" (I.2 ¶8)
   - also: "that the alchemists extracted from the Form out of these metals to transmute and increase with" (I.2 ¶9), given as the error
   - stated; QUOTED: the adept, with ATWOOD's comment

3. **It is none of the four elements, yet it can become any of them.** It is one thing found everywhere and contains all nature and all Life. The Sages call it "their salt", meaning the salt of the world, not common salt. It is drawn from "their earth", which is not common earth.
   - "The band of Sages only have known it, And they call it their salt." (I.2 ¶10)
   - also: "It is not earth, nor fire, nor air, nor water" (I.2 ¶10); "Nay it can become to be fire, air, water, and earth" (I.2 ¶10); "But rather the salt of the world, Which contains in itself all Life" (I.2 ¶10); "It is extracted from their earth" (I.2 ¶10)
   - stated; QUOTED: Lucerna Solis. "Their earth" is not explained (see Open questions).

4. **The subject is a living spiritual water, which the masters describe in many ways.** Geber calls the Stone a spirit and "living water", also named "dry water". Albertus calls the mercury of the wise a cold, moist, oily, permanent water and the spirit of body. Lully calls it Hyle, a clear compounded water most like argent vive, formed in every compound out of the substance of the air.
   - "our Stone is no other than a foetant spirit and living water, which we have named dry water" (I.2 ¶11)
   - also: "the mercury of the wise to be a watery element, cold and moist, a permanent water, an unctuous vapor, and the spirit of body" (I.2 ¶29, Albertus); "the first material of the metals is an unctuous subtle humidity, forcibly incorporated with a subtle terrestreity" (I.2 ¶29, Albertus); "calls Hyle, saying, that it is a clear compounded water, most like in substance to argent vive" (I.2 ¶29, Lully); "it is found flowing upon earth, and is generated in every compound out of the substance of the air" (I.2 ¶29, Lully)
   - stated; QUOTED: Geber, Albertus Magnus, Lully (endorsed as descriptions of the same thing). "Foetant" is not explained.

5. **"A stone and no stone."** Arnold's argent vive is the whole subject of the Art: spirit, soul and body. It takes whatever state the operator gives it. Belus explains the riddle: the permanent water is the stone, but while it is still water it is no stone.
   - "this very permanent water is the stone, but whilst it is water it is no stone" (I.2 ¶29, Belus)
   - also: "it is a stone and no stone, in which the whole Art consists, spirit, soul and body" (I.2 ¶29, Arnold); "which if thou coagulatest, it will be coagulated; an if thou makest it fly, it will fly; for it is volatile, and clear as a tear" (I.2 ¶29); "if thou say it is water, thou dost say truth; and if thou sayest it is not water, thou dost lie" (I.2 ¶29); "It is a stone and no stone, In which the whole art consists;" (I.2 ¶30)
   - stated; QUOTED: Arnold, Belus

6. **The magi obtained it experimentally, in a compact form.** They describe it not only as it occurs mixed in air or water but as a compact, tangible essence with nothing foreign in it. In that pure state it is the Kabalists' Vehicle of Light and the Greeks' Free Ether.
   - "that elder Quintessence of the magi which they introduce, not as a being of speculation merely, but of experimental science" (I.2 ¶16)
   - also: "but as an essence compact and tangible without heterogeneity" (I.2 ¶16)
   - stated; ATWOOD

7. **It never exists free on earth.** The ethereal spirit is hidden, and defiled, inside the beings it sustains. The Mercury of the philosophers is not found by itself on earth and cannot be held or perfected without the Art.
   - "the ethereal spirit does not subsist of itself, separate or tangibly on earth; but, giving subsistence to other beings, is occultated even in their life, and defiled" (I.2 ¶20)
   - also: "Hoc vere nulibi est quod quaerimus." (I.2 ¶19); "the Mercury of the philosophers is not found of itself on earth, nor can be detained or perfected without this occult and needful Art assisting her" (I.2 ¶49)
   - stated; ATWOOD. Compare Lully's "found flowing upon earth" (item 4; see Open questions).

8. **"Elements" means properties of the one spirit, not the chemical elements.** When the alchemists speak of elements being converted, they mean the elements of the Mercury, which are properties of the universal spirit. They do not mean fire, air, water or gases. Paracelsus: what we see is only a container, and the true element is an invisible spirit of life.
   - "the elements they speak of, as being introverted and transformed are the elements of the Mercury, properties of the universal spirit" (I.2 ¶26)
   - also: "That which we see is only the receptacle; the true element is a spirit of life, and grows in all things, as the soul in the body of man." (I.2 ¶26, Paracelsus); "the first matter of the elements of the elements is nothing else but that life which the creatures have" (I.2 ¶26)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with QUOTED: Paracelsus (endorsed)

9. **A middle or "third" substance that is still one in nature.** It is called middle or third because it arises from the active and passive relations of the Universal Spirit. It remains of one nature with its root. Vaughan calls it a union of masculine and feminine spirits: a soft, formless, half-potential living mass.
   - "a middle substance" (I.2 ¶31), explained by Atwood: "in respect to its generation by active and passive relations of the Universal Spirit, whence it proceeds as a third, yet homogeneal from its radix" (I.2 ¶31)
   - also: "Lully also calls it tertium, and compounded in this sense" (I.2 ¶31); "the First Matter is indeed the union of masculine and feminine spirits" (I.2 ¶33, Vaughan); "a world without form, a divine animated mass of complexion like silver, neither mere power nor perfect action" (I.2 ¶33, Vaughan)
   - stated; ATWOOD (the gloss), QUOTED: Ripley, Lully, Vaughan

10. **Atwood's own definition: the simple substance of life and light.** It is an unseen substance of life and light running through nature, without which nothing can exist. She says this definition does not teach how to work it.
    - "the simple generated substance of life and light, immanifestly flowing throughout nature" (I.2 ¶34)
    - also: "that without which nothing that exists is able to be" (I.2 ¶34); "the alone Laelia Aelia latent in and about all" (I.2 ¶34); "it is the ethereal body of life and light which they profess to have discovered" (I.2 ¶43)
    - stated; ATWOOD

11. **It is no sensible thing.** The Matter is not water, earth, air, fire, gold, silver, mercury, antimony, alkali, gas or vitriol, nor any animal, vegetable or mineral particular. The adepts use such names, but they are deceptive.
    - "it is no one of the many things with which sense brings us acquainted" (I.2 ¶34)
    - also: "neither water, nor earth, nor air, nor fire, though it contains in principle the nature of all these" (I.2 ¶34); "comparable to no manifested particular whatever" (I.2 ¶31, Basil Valentine); "it is not the common elementated water to which he alludes" (I.2 ¶43)
    - stated; ATWOOD, with QUOTED: Basil Valentine

12. **It is in plain sight, cheap and unrecognised.** In its ordinary state it is everywhere, so the writers call it vile and cheap.
    - "Our subject is presented to the eyes of the whole world, and it is not known!" (I.2 ¶33)
    - also: "It is of small account, yet no body can be without it" (I.2 ¶33); "In the common estate, as the Spirit is in nature, said to be everywhere, it is called a thing vile and cheap" (I.2 ¶40)
    - stated; QUOTED: Synesius / the New Light; ATWOOD

13. **Liquid images stand for the spirit's fermenting principle.** Tears, rain, dew, milk, wine and oil in the texts mean the fermenting principle of the spirit and its distilled quintessence, not literal liquids. "Our water" is heavenly and does not wet the hands.
    - "by such familiar analogies as tears, rain, dew, milk, wine, and oil, the fermental principle of the spirit and her distilled quintessence are very ordinarily denoted" (I.2 ¶33)
    - also: "Our water is heavenly, not wetting the hands, not of the vulgar, but almost rain water" (I.2 ¶33, Sendivogius)
    - stated; ATWOOD

14. **The quintessence is a celestial soul drawn from its minera.** It is said "in plain terms" to be a celestial soul, drawn by the work (the "magistery") out of its minera. It is also called a clear Light, the bond of the elements.
    - "the quintessence is no other than our viscous celestial and glorious soul drawn from its minera by our magistery" (I.2 ¶33)
    - also: "a clear Light, which fills with true virtue every mind that has once perceived it" (I.2 ¶33)
    - stated; QUOTED: Synesius / the New Light. "Minera" is not glossed.

15. **The "Water" of Hermes is the ethereal body, tortured and made bodily by art.** The water on which Hermes, Thales and Moses ground everything is the ethereal body of life and light. Art alters it and makes it corporeal.
    - "a certain tortured water, having suffered alteration by art and becomes corporified" (I.2 ¶43)
    - stated; ATWOOD

16. **The thing to seek yields a moisture that dissolves gold gently.** Sendivogius says it is the thing from which nature produces gold, "like a mother" to it. He does not name it.
    - "you must seek for that hidden thing, out of which is made, after a wonderful manner, such a moisture or humidity which doth dissolve gold without violence or noise" (I.2 ¶48)
    - also: "it is even like a mother to it" (I.2 ¶48)
    - stated; QUOTED: Sendivogius

17. **Metals are used most because the metallic root appears first.** The metalline radix shows itself first in the experiment, is the easiest to apportion, and carries less vital risk. Atwood says this is not because the power is limited to metals, nor because adepts wanted gold above every other good.
    - "the metalline radix first presents itself in the experimental process, and is most easily apportioned" (I.2 ¶49)
    - also: "because the responsibility involved is less vital and consequential, it has been more freely exhibited and worked at large" (I.2 ¶49)
    - stated; ATWOOD

18. **Recap: one Homogeneal Subject.** One uniform substance in nature is the material basis of the Art and the only principle of transmutation, life, increase and perfection.
    - "the existence of a Homogeneal Subject in nature, showing that the same was the material basis of their philosophy, and the only principle of transmutation, life, increase, and perfection" (I.2 ¶49)
    - stated; ATWOOD

19. **Proteus and Pan: the whole of nature, taken as the masters found it.** The universal nature the alchemists "revolutionized" is to be considered whole and unaltered, before the art acts on it. Nature has produced it but left it unfinished. The verse says it grows "in the caverns of the mountains", not on earth; the image is not explained.
    - "Qui Proteum non novit, adeum Pana." (I.2 ¶27)
    - also: "whole, and singularly, and before any alteration had been induced in his uniform substance by their art" (I.2 ¶29); "Nature has made it such, But has not yet brought it to perfection." (I.2 ¶30, Lucerna Salis Phil.); "You will not find it on earth, since there it has no growth; It grows only in the caverns of the mountains." (I.2 ¶30)
    - interpreted (Proteus/Pan = the subject, from ¶27–29 context); stated for "not yet brought it to perfection". ATWOOD; QUOTED: Lucerna Salis Phil.

## The agent (what does the work: fire, spirit, light, intellect…)

1. **The freed subject does the transmuting.** The Universal Subject is both the thing transmuted and, once freed and separated in its pure essence, the thing that transmutes.
   - "the thing transmuting also, when set free and segregated in its proper essentiality" (I.2 ¶8)
   - stated; ATWOOD

2. **Light is the agent: it alone multiplies, and it acts as the ferment.** The Light is the only thing that can truly be multiplied. Once art has found and perfected it, it is the ferment that perfects the Ether. It is also the Form the matter lacks and the source of central heat.
   - "Light is the true fermental principle which perfects the Ether in its proper kind" (I.2 ¶44)
   - also: "It is the Light only that can be truly multiplied" (I.2 ¶8, the adept); "This Light (discovered and perfected by art)" (I.2 ¶8); "This Form is Light, the source of central heat, Which clothed with mater doth a seed beget." (I.2 ¶45, Eiranaeus); "No, there must be an inward agent granted, Else would a thing unchanged still remain;" (I.2 ¶45)
   - stated (¶44, ¶45); ATWOOD, QUOTED: the adept, Eiranaeus (endorsed). How the Light relates to the freed subject in ¶8 is unclear (see Open questions).

3. **The Light belongs to the subject itself.** The Mercury is multiplied by its own Light. The Free Ether works "of itself" by its own enclosed light. The Stone is the Ether condensed by multiplying "its proper Light".
   - "multiplying the Mercury by its proper Light" (I.2 ¶39)
   - also: "the Greeks, the Free Ether, that is to say, freed from the prison of gross matter, and able to work of itself intimately by the virtue of its own included light" (I.2 ¶16); "scientific multiplication of its proper Light" (I.2 ¶44); "the Formal Light, by multiplication in its Ethe" (I.2 ¶46; sic)
   - stated as wording; reading "proper Light" as the subject's own inherent light is interpreted (see Open questions). ATWOOD; QUOTED: the Greeks

4. **A stronger ferment or attraction frees the subject.** The subject-matter is drawn out of inferior forms by a superior fermenting or attracting force.
   - "being set free by the force of a superior ferment or attraction" (I.2 ¶4)
   - also: "withdrawn by art and transported from inferior Forms" (I.2 ¶4)
   - interpreted. ATWOOD. ¶4 does not name the ferment; ¶44 makes Light the fermental principle.

5. **The Ether as a fire always ready to act.** The ancients derived everything from a pure hidden fire, the Ether. The moderns Atwood cites held that it is always present and acts on subjects prepared for it.
   - "this mighty Ether moreover is always at hand, ready to break forth into action on predisposed subjects" (I.2 ¶14)
   - also: "a certain pure and hidden fire" (I.2 ¶12); "as a plastic fire, ever generating by rule" (I.2 ¶16, Zeno); "that most heavenly high ethereal igneous nature, which spontaneously begets all things" (I.2 ¶16, Cicero)
   - stated; QUOTED: Homberg, Boerhaave, Boyle (reported with approval), Zeno, Cicero

6. **The Universal Spirit, or Mercury, is the only means of growth.** Metals come from metalline seeds out of the Universal Spirit, or Mercury. Only that spirit can exalt and multiply them. Without it there is no growth, transmutation or increase.
   - "from metalline seeds out of the Universal Spirit or Mercury, by which also they may be exalted and multiplies, and by no other thing" (I.2 ¶49)
   - stated; ATWOOD

7. **The matter acts on gold as a solvent.** As a liquid ("Latex"), the Matter is the sharp vinegar that turns gold into pure spirit, and the water that engenders all things. Paracelsus names the matter of the metals "truphat", which gives everything its due form.
   - "this Latex is the sharp vinegar which makes gold a pure spirit, seeing she is even that blessed water which engenders al things" (I.2 ¶33)
   - also: "The truphat, or matter of the metals, brings everything into its due form" (I.2 ¶25, Paracelsus)
   - stated; QUOTED: Synesius / the New Light, Paracelsus

8. **Its own fire.** One stage happens when the matter is calcined "by its proper fire". The perfected seed is a "Stone of Fire". Sendivogius: art perfects gold by fire or heat alone, once the body has been opened.
   - "which afterwards appearing calcined by its proper fire, is called magnesia and lead of the wise" (I.2 ¶40)
   - also: "Lapis noster, hic est ignis, ex igne creatus, et in ignem veritur, et anima ejus in igna moratur" (I.2 ¶44; ours: "Our stone is fire, created from fire, and turned into fire, and its soul dwells in fire"); "by no other medium than fire or heat" (I.2 ¶49, Sendivogius)
   - stated as wording; that this fire is the subject's own Light/Ether rather than kitchen fire is interpreted (¶15: common fire is "an excitation only"; ¶24: fire-digesters destroy the nature). ATWOOD; QUOTED: the adept, Sendivogius

## The operator (requirements: knowledge, moral state, will, guidance, secrecy…)

1. **Skilled human work is required.** Nature alone does not bring the subject into action. It needs skilful handling by human hands.
   - "she is of another birth, and cannot be brought to knowledge without sagacious handling and human help" (I.2 ¶18)
   - also: "cum in actionem venire non posit per naturam, absque adjutorio ingenii et humanarum manuum operationibus" (I.2 ¶18, Lully)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Lully

2. **The required power is more than ordinary.** The reversal the alchemists propose is beyond ordinary art as much as beyond nature.
   - "The contrariation proposed by the proposed by the alchemists, indeed, is not the power of ordinary art, any more than of nature herself" (I.2 ¶24)
   - stated; ATWOOD

3. **Direct experience is needed, earned by labour.** Description cannot convey the Matter; only real experience can. That experience comes to the patient worker as the reward of personal labour and perseverance.
   - "needing actual experience to fix its idea" (I.2 ¶34)
   - also: "This the art promises to a patient and true philosopher, but as a reward of individual labor and perseverance only." (I.2 ¶34); "without the light of true experience" (I.2 ¶31, Basil Valentine); "which the Alchemists alone teach experimentally to expound" (I.2 ¶34)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Basil Valentine

4. **Early practice is needed to read the texts.** The descriptions are scattered and figurative, so practice at the start is needed to pick out the true light among them.
   - "Practice in the beginning is required, therefore, to interrogate and discern, from amongst so many shadowy representatives, the true light." (I.2 ¶42)
   - stated; ATWOOD

5. **Two prerequisites: the true matter and a fit artist.** One must know what the true matter is and where and how to take it, and must have a capable operator.
   - "to know what the true matter is, where and how it may be taken, and to find an artist able and fitted to perfect it" (I.2 ¶49)
   - stated; ATWOOD

6. **Prudence, patience, penetration and diligence.** These are needed to understand the Hermetic author, and more than these to discover the Art. The books are for diligent students of nature, not casual readers.
   - "Prudence, patience, and penetration, the author owns, are required to understand him, and more than these for the discovery of his Great Art." (I.2 ¶50)
   - also: "our books are made for those who have been or intend to become conversant about the search of nature" (I.2 ¶50); "For this is the first step towards the recovery of truth, to be diligent in the investigation" (I.2 ¶50)
   - stated; QUOTED: the author of the Tractatus Aureus (endorsed by ATWOOD)

7. **The operator's role is to unveil, under God and through nature.** Making the hidden thing appear is granted to an intelligent philosopher by God, through nature. The philosopher does not create it (see Principles 7).
   - "to make things that are not perceived, but which lie in the shadow to appear, and to take from them their veil, is granted to an intelligent philosopher by God, through nature" (I.2 ¶33)
   - stated; QUOTED: Synesius / the New Light

8. **The operator must capture the freed spirit and help it.** At the point of breakdown the operator must catch the bare spirit and assist it to act by its own will (Procedure 5). Atwood says Bacon knew neither the Great Art nor its subject.
   - "to entrap the bare spirit and help her on from thence to operate her own intrinsical freed will" (I.2 ¶24)
   - stated; ATWOOD

## The procedure and its stages (in the order the chapter gives, if it gives one)

The chapter gives no single numbered sequence. It gives an overall line (reduce, invert or break down, separate, ferment and multiply by Light, perfect) plus three partial lists of stages or colours (items 9–11 below). The order of points here follows the chapter.

1. **Reduction (Solution) to the first matter.** The first work, and the foundation of the art, is to reduce the body to "water", meaning mercury or argent vive. Metals transmute only once reduced to their beginning. Then one form can be expelled and another introduced, as glass is made from flints and ashes.
   - "the first work is to reduce the body into water, that is, into mercury, and this is called Solution, which is the foundation of the whole art" (I.2 ¶5)
   - also: "Solution and a reduction of the body into water, which is argent vive" (I.2 ¶5); "one form being expelled, another may be introduced" (I.2 ¶5, Rosarium); "unless they are reduced first into their first matter, and made free to pass from one into another form" (I.2 ¶5); "Without they be reduced to their beginning materiable" (I.2 ¶6, Ripley); "Mineral and metalline may be mercurizate" (I.2 ¶6); "the Subject be freed by an artificial reduction" (I.2 ¶5)
   - stated (¶5); "mercurizate" = turned into mercury is interpreted. QUOTED: Friar Bacon, Rosarium, Arnold, Ripley (endorsed)

2. **Dissolve and open the body; Hermetic dissolution.** No spirit shows in a solid metal, so the body must first be loosened and its pores opened so nature can work in it. Hermetic dissolution produces the right recipient, separates pure from impure and subtle from gross, and shows agent and patient to be one.
   - "by the Hermetic dissolution the right recipient is said to be obtained, the pure is separated from the impure, the subtle from the gross, and the agent and the patient are one identity" (I.2 ¶48)
   - also: "it is necessary that the body be loosened and dissolved, and the pores thereof opened, whereof nature may work" (I.2 ¶49, Sendivogius); "unless they be first purged from their terrestreity and their tincture set free in the subtle Origin of all life" (I.2 ¶49); "That which is below is as that which is above" (I.2 ¶48, Emerald Table)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Sendivogius, Emerald Table

3. **Add nothing; remove only superfluities.** The work uses one matter by and through itself. Nothing foreign is added or taken away; only superfluities are removed during preparation and separation.
   - "to which we add nothing nor take away anything, only in the preparation, removing superfluities" (I.2 ¶11, the monk)
   - also: "working, as it is said, the only universal living and occult nature by and through itself, scientifically" (I.2 ¶9); "if thou workest therefore with Mercury alone, without foreign intervention, thou obtainest thy desire" (I.2 ¶11, Aquinas); "to which we add nothing different or extract, only in the separation removing superfluities" (I.2 ¶43, the Arabian)
   - stated (¶11, ¶43); the ¶9 sense of "by and through itself" is interpreted. ATWOOD, QUOTED: the monk, Aquinas, the Arabian

4. **Reverse nature's order: make the fixed volatile and the volatile fixed.** Nature's ordinary course runs against her perfect law, so the operation must go the opposite way. The aim is to suppress the outward imposed form and develop the inner central form. Arnold calls this the conversion of the elements: reducing natures in the argent vive to their first root.
   - "that which is fixed becoming volatile and the volatile nature fixed" (I.2 ¶20)
   - also: "Si fixum solvas faciasqu volare solutum Et volucrem figas, facient te vivere tutum: Solve, Coagula, Fige." (I.2 ¶21; ours: "If you dissolve the fixed and make the dissolved fly, and fix the flying, they will make you live safely: Dissolve, Coagulate, Fix"); "to make light things heavy, and to make spirits no spirits" (I.2 ¶22, Arnold); "our operation is nothing else than a mutation of natures, and the method of conversion in our Argent vive is the reduction of natures to their first root" (I.2 ¶25, Arnold); "Converte elementa et quod quaeris invenies" (I.2 ¶22; ours: "Convert the elements and you will find what you seek"); "the order of natural procedure ought to be introverted for a true and perfect manifestation" (I.2 ¶27)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Arnold (endorsed)

5. **Break the matter down almost to nothing, catch the bare spirit, and help it re-form itself.** Following Democritus by way of Bacon: matter forced toward annihilation runs through all its shapes and, if the force is kept up, restores itself. The fastest way grips the matter to the utmost degree. Atwood's version: reduce the matter and its false forms to the edge of annihilation, trap the bare spirit there, and help it act by its own will until it wraps itself around a new form.
   - "to reduce the matter, with her false forms, to the verge of annihilation, but to entrap the bare spirit and help her on from thence to operate her own intrinsical freed will" (I.2 ¶24)
   - also: "torture and vex it in order to its annihilation" (I.2 ¶23); "running through the whole circle of transformations, and completing its period, it in some degree restores itself, if the force be continued" (I.2 ¶23); "which makes use of manacles and fetters; i.e., lays hold and works upon matter in the extremist degree" (I.2 ¶23); "wrapping herself spontaneously about it, even to a recreation" (I.2 ¶24)
   - stated; ATWOOD (¶24), QUOTED: Bacon on Democritus (endorsed in principle; Bacon's means rejected, see Impediments 4)

6. **Quick separation.** Paracelsus calls the Art a natural magic of unmatched speed of separation, in which things break apart into their elements and simple essence. Separation is the beginning of all generation.
   - "very excellent for quickness of separation, the like whereof nature can neither give nor express" (I.2 ¶25)
   - also: "all things fall apart into their elements, breaking forth into their act and simple essence" (I.2 ¶25)
   - stated; QUOTED: Paracelsus

7. **Separate the elements of the Mercury, then recombine them in equal proportion.** Ripley: this makes the elixir. Hermes divides the Water into four substances. "Elements" here are properties of the one spirit (Subject 8).
   - "The elements of Mercury being separated, says Ripley, and again commixed by equal weight or proportion, make the elixir complete" (I.2 ¶25)
   - also: "the separation of the ancient philosophers is made upon Water, dividing into four substances" (I.2 ¶43, Hermes); "Volatile et fixum simul colliga" (I.2 ¶32, Basil Valentine; ours: "bind the volatile and the fixed together")
   - stated (¶25, ¶43); ¶32 rests on our translation (interpreted). QUOTED: Ripley, Hermes, Basil Valentine. The four substances are not named.

8. **First whiteness, then the annihilate state, then three principles.** The Matter first appears pure and white out of the "philosophical contrition". After the artist brings everything to an annihilated state and presses out "the waters of her extreme life", nature reacts. The one Matter then shows three principles, Salt, Sulfur and Mercury, as agent, patient and offspring.
   - "when the wise artist has brought all into this annihilate condition, and pressed out the waters of her extreme life" (I.2 ¶36)
   - also: "the Matter only as it first appears, pure, as they say, and white, out of the philosophical contrition" (I.2 ¶36); "three great magnetic principles of being --- the Salt, Sulfur, and Mercury of adepts, in relation to each other of agent, patient, and offspring universal" (I.2 ¶36)
   - stated; ATWOOD. The contrition is not explained.

9. **Fermentation through every form, then a potential chaos.** In the artificial fermentation the subject becomes everything in turn: mineral, vegetable, animal, fire, vapour, aqua sicca, oil, vinegar, phoenix, dragon. It then becomes a formless "potential chaos and egg of philosophers", while remaining one unchanged Identity.
   - "In its artificial fermentation and progress towards perfection, the changes it undergoes are manifold" (I.2 ¶37)
   - also: "but a potential chaos and egg of philosophers; a precedential, nameless principle, always in mutation, becoming to be" (I.2 ¶37); "Proceeding homogeneal through each omniform variety, and returning into herself manifestly the life and all phenomena which she as constantly supplies, the great Identity is as herself unchanged" (I.2 ¶37)
   - stated; ATWOOD

10. **Atwood's ordered stage names.** This is the chapter's only explicit sequence of stages. Each name marks one stage:
    (a) common state: "vile and cheap";
    (b) first sublimed: serpent, dragon, green lion, "on account of its strength and crude vitality";
    (c) putrefying: a stronger poison, the "venomous toad";
    (d) calcined by its own fire: magnesia, lead of the wise;
    (e) dissolving again: "vitriolic solvent and most sharp acetum";
    (f) turned to an oil and whitening: milk, dew, quintessence;
    (g) final perfection: phoenix, salamander, royal essence, Red Stone.
    - "being sublimed at first, it is called a serpent, dragon, or green lion, on account of its strength and crude vitality" (I.2 ¶40)
    - also: "which putrefying, becomes a stronger poison, and their venomous toad" (I.2 ¶40); "which afterwards appearing calcined by its proper fire, is called magnesia and lead of the wise" (I.2 ¶40); "which again dissolving, becomes their vitriolic solvent and most sharp acetum" (I.2 ¶40); "this afterwards is changed into an oil, which, whitening, is called milk, dew, quintessence, and by may other names" (I.2 ¶40); "until raised to the final perfection, it is henceforth a phoenix, salamander, their royal essence and Red Stone" (I.2 ¶40); "Some call it a Toade for his great vehemence" (I.2 ¶41, Bloomfield)
    - stated; ATWOOD

11. **Arnold's sequence.** After its clear, volatile state the matter is made citrine, then salty without crystals. It is a deadly poison.
    - "it is made citrine, then saltish, but without crystals; and no man may touch it with his tongue, for it is a deadly poison" (I.2 ¶29)
    - stated; QUOTED: Arnold

12. **Aquinas: the matter works itself.** Mercury dissolves and coagulates itself and turns white, red, yellow and black. It unites with, conceives and brings forth itself until it is perfect. No order of colours is given.
    - "for it dissolves and coagulates itself ---whitens, rubifies, and makes itself to be both yellow and black" (I.2 ¶11)
    - also: "it unites with itself, conceives itself, and brings itself forth, to the full perfecting of its intention" (I.2 ¶11); "The White and Red proceed from one root" (I.2 ¶11); "when Sol is reduced to his first original, i.e., the mercury, then nature embraceth nature" (I.2 ¶11, Geber)
    - stated; QUOTED: Aquinas, Geber (endorsed)

13. **Purify and condense the Ether by multiplying its Light, in four phases.** The Stone is the Ether separated by art, purified, and made concrete by multiplying its own Light. This goes through preparation, generation, birth and specification, all resting on a first drawing-out ("eduction").
    - "the preparation, generation, birth, specification --- all proceeding, arte mirabili, on the hidden basis of its primal eduction" (I.2 ¶44)
    - also: "separated by artificial means, purified and made concrete by constriction and scientific multiplication of its proper Light" (I.2 ¶44)
    - phases stated; reading "primal eduction" as the first separation is interpreted. ATWOOD. The phases are not explained individually.

14. **Light joined to matter begets a seed; the matter comes alive and cooperates.** The Form (Light), clothed with matter, begets a seed. The seed stamps its character on the matter. The matter then lives and works with the Form toward the end.
    - "On it it stamps its character, which done, The Matter lives, and that which may seem strange, Co-worketh with the Form t’attain the end" (I.2 ¶45)
    - stated; QUOTED: Eiranaeus (endorsed)

15. **The Vital Spirit is raised by its own leaven, then acts back on its origin.** As juice turns to wine by its own ferment, with nothing different added, the Vital Spirit is raised to a higher form by a leaven prepared from itself. It passes through many stages from imperfect to perfect, then acts back on the elements it came from.
    - "the Vital Spirit said to be, by the art of Alchemy, promoted from one form of being into another by its won prepared must or leaven" (I.2 ¶48)
    - also: "it reacts convertively on the elements of its original extraction; having previously passed on, through many stages, from imperfection to perfection" (I.2 ¶48)
    - stated; ATWOOD

16. **One method in all three kingdoms; only the preparation differs.** Supplying Form to the purified Spirit follows the same method in mineral, vegetable and animal. Only the preparation varies. Plants need more skill and coction.
    - "the method of working to this discovery, and to supply the deficiency of Form to the purified body of the Spirit, is described as the same in each of the three kingdoms of nature" (I.2 ¶49)
    - also: "through superior skill and coction in the vegetal life, the elixir of the wise" (I.2 ¶46)
    - stated; ATWOOD

17. **Extraction by "chalybs".** The precious thing is "our sea water", fixed in gold and silver and drawn out with the help of "our chalybs, or steel". The chalybs is not explained.
    - "nothing else than our sea water, which is congealed in gold and silver, and extracted by the help of our chalybs, or steel, by the art of philosophers" (I.2 ¶33)
    - interpreted (as a procedural step); QUOTED: Synesius / the New Light

## Signs (what shows the work is proceeding)

1. **A white, silver-like fume.** Atrephius: the subject appears as a white fume like pure silver, returning bodies to their original whiteness, and as a life that makes things grow, multiply and revive.
   - "a white fume, in substance like to pure silver, resolving bodies into their original whiteness" (I.2 ¶29)
   - also: "a vegetable life making all things to grow, multiply and resuscitate" (I.2 ¶29)
   - stated; QUOTED: Atrephius

2. **Pure and white at first appearance.** The Matter first comes out of the philosophical contrition pure and white (Procedure 8).
   - "the Matter only as it first appears, pure, as they say, and white, out of the philosophical contrition" (I.2 ¶36)
   - stated; ATWOOD

3. **Three principles appear after the annihilate state.** When nature reacts, Salt, Sulfur and Mercury appear out of the unity (Procedure 8).
   - "nature re-acting, as it is said, exhibits from out her unity three great magnetic principles of being" (I.2 ¶36)
   - stated; ATWOOD

4. **Colours, "elements" and "operations" in the texts are signs of changes during preparation.** When adepts speak of elements, colours and operations, they mean qualities and changes in the subject during preparation and while the Mercury is multiplied by its Light, not surface phenomena. The stage names (Procedure 10), Arnold's citrine and salty stages (Procedure 11) and Aquinas's white, red, yellow and black (Procedure 12) are such markers.
   - "it is to the qualities and changes which take place during the preparation, and multiplying the Mercury by its proper Light, they allude" (I.2 ¶39)
   - also: "not to any superficial phenomena or those elements which the moderns have so triumphantly decomposed" (I.2 ¶39)
   - stated; ATWOOD

5. **The matter comes alive and works with the Form.** After the seed stamps its character, the matter "lives" (Procedure 14).
   - "The Matter lives" (I.2 ¶45)
   - stated as wording; reading it as an observable sign is interpreted. QUOTED: Eiranaeus

6. **Gold takes in the Light wholly.** Gold absorbs the Formal Light completely, without residue, like ice in warm water. Other metals lose some weight because of their impurity.
   - "the Formal Light is wholly swallowed up in it without residue, dissolving intimately, gently, and naturally, as they compare it to ice in warm water" (I.2 ¶48)
   - also: "in all other metals there is some impurity, and therefore a certain weight is lost in transmuting from them" (I.2 ¶48)
   - stated as fact; reading the weight loss as a sign of impurity is interpreted. ATWOOD

## Impediments and dangers

1. **Reading the texts literally or through their metaphors.** Weighing elements, fixing metallic vapour with artificial heat or with sun and moon rays, and talk of a "sulphur tingens" are follies wrongly attributed to the adepts. The texts must be read by the one essential nature, not by their metaphors.
   - "by no means be led aside by their metaphoric language into a common misconstruction of its meaning" (I.2 ¶12)
   - also: "weighing and proportioning the elements so exactly as to constitute them into lasting accord" (I.2 ¶9); "consolidating the metalline vapor by heat artificially introduced, or by the rays of the sun and moon drawn to simultaneous cooperation" (I.2 ¶9); "those imposters who tell you of a sulphur tingens" (I.2 ¶8)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: the adept

2. **Deliberately confusing descriptions.** Writers describe the subject at random stages, without order, under countless names that all partly fit and all mislead. They use titles such as gold, mercury or vitriol. The texts seem to contradict each other until the starting ground is understood.
   - "now describing in the natural state, then in its purified condition, or otherwise in any one of the intermediate stages through which it passes, without note of order in the art" (I.2 ¶42)
   - also: "Adepts have taken advantage of the mutable nature of their subject, to baffle the blind searcher" (I.2 ¶39); "is warily concealed by them under an infinity of names, all more or less applicable, yet delusive" (I.2 ¶37); "though these titles are found interspersed abundantly with others, equally deceptive, in the pages of the adepts" (I.2 ¶34); "Be not therefore deceived with manifold descriptions and operations, for it is One Thing to which nothing extraneous is added" (I.2 ¶29, Arnold)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Arnold

3. **Mistaking common air for the spirit.** Common air must be told apart from the true ethereal spirit, which is mixed into nature and known only by its action. Ordinary light phenomena look like the adepts' descriptions but are shadowy and mixed by comparison.
   - "mistaking Olympus, embrace some cloud whilst the life-giving Juno is far away above all our idea and sight" (I.2 ¶18)
   - also: "calling that Jove which is not Jove" (I.2 ¶18); "only that they are shadowy and mingled, compared with the alleged virtue and perfective properties of the Philosophic Subject" (I.2 ¶34)
   - interpreted (¶18, from "It is requisite, however, to distinguish airs here"); ATWOOD

4. **Destroying the spirit with fire-digesters.** Mechanical dissolution by heat drives off the very nature that is sought and leaves only dead ashes. Atwood says this is why Bacon's method fails.
   - "Such instruments do, in fact, expel the very nature which the ancients prized; leaving us without all recompense in the dead ashes of her consuming vesture" (I.2 ¶24)
   - also: "particular digesters applied to the fire" (I.2 ¶24)
   - stated; ATWOOD

5. **Letting the freed spirit escape: "the common catastrophe".** If the released spirit leaves unseen and is not pursued, other outside influences seize it and it is defiled again. Atwood calls this the usual failure.
   - "if she is suffered to depart invisibly without pursuit or amendment, which is the common catastrophe, then she is caught up by other external compellants, and becoming defiled, is imprisoned by them" (I.2 ¶24)
   - stated; ATWOOD

6. **In nature the spirit is enclosed and impure.** In nature as it is, forms ("sulfurs") are entangled and the passive spirit is shut in and contaminated, so truth does not appear. Dissolution is the remedy (Procedure 2).
   - "her forms or sulfurs are perplexed, and the passive spirit of nature is included and impure" (I.2 ¶48)
   - interpreted (as the obstacle that dissolution removes); ATWOOD

7. **In metals the spirit is smothered by earthy matter.** Metals cannot grow for lack of heat, because their spirit is overwhelmed by gross elements. They must first be purged of earthiness and their tincture set free.
   - "the Spirit being overcome in the gross, preponderating elements of their hard composition, so that they cannot fructify" (I.2 ¶49)
   - also: "Vulgar gold Sendivogius compares to a herb without seed, which when it is ripe bears seed" (I.2 ¶49)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Sendivogius

8. **Poison.** In Arnold's sequence the matter becomes a deadly poison that must not touch the tongue. The putrefying stage is "a stronger poison".
   - "no man may touch it with his tongue, for it is a deadly poison" (I.2 ¶29)
   - also: "which putrefying, becomes a stronger poison, and their venomous toad" (I.2 ¶40)
   - stated; QUOTED: Arnold; ATWOOD

9. **Theory without experience.** The moderns who held the Ether doctrine had no tangible proof, perhaps no experience of it, and were not believed. The alchemists claim "another experience".
   - "which in part it was perhaps, in their minds, without experience" (I.2 ¶15)
   - also: "But then they could adduce no tangible proof of their doctrine." (I.2 ¶15); "these alchemists profess to have enjoyed another experience" (I.2 ¶12)
   - interpreted (the contrast); ATWOOD

## The result

1. **The Stone is the purified, condensed Ether of nature.** The philosophers agree that the Stone is the pure ethereal substance of nature, separated by art, purified, and made concrete by multiplying its own Light. As fermented Spirit it is called the Stone.
   - "their stone is nothing more or less than the pure Ethereality of nature, separated by artificial means, purified and made concrete by constriction and scientific multiplication of its proper Light" (I.2 ¶44)
   - also: "this fermented Spirit or Stone, (as in the crystalline perfectness of its essence it has been called,)" (I.2 ¶48)
   - stated; ATWOOD

2. **Seed of gold, Stone of Fire.** The seed of gold is Light thickened into a fiery form, and this is the Stone of Fire. In the mineral kingdom the work reaches perfection earliest and most easily.
   - "the seed of gold, says the adept, is a fiery form of Light inspissate, and this is the Stone of Fire" (I.2 ¶44)
   - stated; QUOTED: the adept

3. **Once transmuted, it transmutes and multiplies.** The perfected matter converts imperfect bodies to itself in proportion. Its power depends on the virtue it gained in fermentation. Transmuting means drawing the crude life of nature out of its forms into the Stone's own light.
   - "which being transmuted, transmutes; and multiplying, multiplies its proper substance freely, in proportion to the virtue which it has acquired in the fermentation" (I.2 ¶48)
   - also: "converts to itself proportionally all imperfect bodies that it touches" (I.2 ¶2, Arnold); "transmutes, i.e., attracts the same away from other forms into intimate coalescence with its own assimilative light" (I.2 ¶48)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Arnold di Villanova (endorsed)

4. **It perfects any body within that body's own kind.** The perfected Light, applied to animals, vegetables or minerals, raises each in its own kind. Minerals go "from the worst to the best condition". The pure mineral spirit raises things of like nature to the level of the Elixir.
   - "applied to any body, exalts and perfects it in its own kind" (I.2 ¶8)
   - also: "translates them from the worst to the best condition" (I.2 ¶8); "it will work such an alteration in things of like nature with themselves, that they shall equalize the Philosophical Elixir" (I.2 ¶49, n49: Nuysement)
   - stated; QUOTED: the adept, Nuysement

5. **A universal medicine.** The Stone and the medicine are one. Made from the salt of the world, it preserves from all illness. Cheap in its common state, it becomes the most potent medicine when fully prepared.
   - "in its perfectly prepared form, a medicine the most potent and precious in the whole world" (I.2 ¶40)
   - also: "Of it is made that medicine which will preserve you from all maladies" (I.2 ¶10, Lucerna Solis); "The Stone is one, says the monk in his Rosary; the medicine is one, in which the whole mystery consists" (I.2 ¶11); "the intermediate stages partake of the predominance of either extreme" (I.2 ¶40)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Lucerna Solis, the monk

6. **The inner form brought to life.** The reversal suppresses the outward, imposed image and develops the central form into life and act.
   - "the adventitious or externally generated image may be constricted utterly, and the central form contrariwise developed into life and act" (I.2 ¶20)
   - stated; ATWOOD

7. **Final names: Red Stone, elixir, Red Lyon.** The perfected state is named phoenix, salamander, royal essence and Red Stone. Ripley's recombined elements make the elixir. A verse says whoever has the subject's vapour has the Red Lyon and pure Mercury, and whoever knows its red Sulfur has the whole foundation; neither image is explained.
   - "until raised to the final perfection, it is henceforth a phoenix, salamander, their royal essence and Red Stone" (I.2 ¶40)
   - also: "make the elixir complete" (I.2 ¶25, Ripley); "For he who has the vapor of this thing, Has the gilded splendor of the Red Lyon, The pure and clear Mercury." (I.2 ¶30); "And he who knows the red Sulfur which it contains, Has within his power the whole foundation" (I.2 ¶30)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Ripley, Lucerna Salis Phil.

8. **Results differ by kingdom; in man, a divine image.** The Formal Light multiplied in its Ether gives gold in minerals and, with more skill and coction, the elixir of the wise in plants. It is seldom realised in animals and most fully in man. In man it takes a divine Image, still embryonic in this life, which through a new birth in universal intelligence passes into communion with the highest life.
   - "in him it assumes an Image that is Divine and more potent than all the rest: which is in this life yet an embryo" (I.2 ¶46)
   - also: "when unfolded through a new birth in universal intelligence, transcends the limits of this nether sphere, and passes into communion with the highest life" (I.2 ¶46)
   - stated; ATWOOD

9. **Proof of the unknown Cause.** The Hermetic experiment claimed to demonstrate the hidden cause behind light, electricity and magnetism, which science uses but cannot find.
   - "These phenomena are effects then of a Cause unknown, and that very unknown Cause it was the alleged object of the Hermetic experiment to prove." (I.2 ¶47)
   - stated; ATWOOD

## Principles (general laws of the work)

1. **Transmutation acts on the subject, not on species.** Species (lead as lead) cannot change, no more than a dog into a horse. Their common subject can be freed and given a new form.
   - "Species non transmutantur, sed subjecta specierum optime et propriisime" (I.2 ¶5; ours: "Species are not transmuted, but the subjects of species, most properly")
   - also: "the fundamental possibility and principle of transmutation being not of species, but of their Universal Subject or first matter" (I.2 ¶7)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Rosarium (endorsed)

2. **One pure matter, perfected by art, converts imperfect bodies to itself.** This is the whole theory, in Arnold's words.
   - "there abides in nature a certain pure matter, which, being discovered and brought by art to perfection, converts to itself proportionally all imperfect bodies that it touches" (I.2 ¶2)
   - stated; QUOTED: Arnold di Villanova (endorsed)

3. **One matter, worked by itself, with nothing foreign.** Mercury alone does the work. It is the root of gold and silver, so it is not foreign to them, and it acts as both agent and patient.
   - "It is Mercury alone which perfects in our work" (I.2 ¶11, Aquinas)
   - also: "gold and silver are not unlike in kind to this our Mercury; for it is their root" (I.2 ¶11); "nothing different must be added" (I.2 ¶11)
   - stated (one matter, nothing added); "agent and patient in one" is interpreted (defective ¶11 sentence, supported by ¶8 and ¶48). QUOTED: Aquinas (endorsed)

4. **Nature's ordinary course runs against her perfect law, so the work must reverse it.**
   - "nature operates her ordinary manifestation in direct contrariety to her perfect law" (I.2 ¶20)
   - also: "neither sanity nor beauty can permanently supervene in bodies, unless the contrary be operated in them" (I.2 ¶20); "the order of natural procedure ought to be introverted for a true and perfect manifestation" (I.2 ¶27)
   - stated; ATWOOD

5. **Imperfections are accidental and can be removed.** Particular forms and imperfections are added to the universal source from outside, as water takes colour from the ground beneath it. They can and should be expelled.
   - "specie subsist in it adventitiously, as it were, by sufferance, and may be expelled, and ought to be for the attainment of perfection" (I.2 ¶22)
   - also: "by a real experimental inversion, the Hermetic Art has proved imperfections to be accidental to nature, and introduced to her from without" (I.2 ¶22)
   - stated; QUOTED: unnamed ("it is said"), ATWOOD

6. **Bodies must first be reduced to their original matter.** Bodies must be brought back to their original matter, by turning their generated life inward ("introversion"), before anything can be truly manifested or made lasting.
   - "the reduction of bodies to their original matter, by introversion of the generated life, is requisite to a true manifestation and permanence in any form" (I.2 ¶49)
   - interpreted ("introversion" is not defined); ATWOOD. The clause that follows is possibly garbled (see Open questions).

7. **Nature produces the matter; art only unveils and completes it.** Art cannot create the Matter. Nature makes it but leaves it unfinished. Helped by art, nature exceeds her own limits, which she cannot do alone.
   - "But nature alone engenders it; it is not possible to make it by art; for to create is proper to God alone" (I.2 ¶33, Synesius / the New Light)
   - also: "Thus nature, by the help of art, is said to transcend herself" (I.2 ¶44); "It is vain, therefore, to seek for that in nature which is an effect beyond her strength; she must be helped, that she may exceed herself, or all will be useless." (I.2 ¶49); "help her to exceed the ordinary limits of her law" (I.2 ¶9); "if at any time nature be sweetly and wittily helped, then art may perfect that which nature could not" (I.2 ¶49, Sendivogius)
   - stated; ATWOOD, QUOTED: Synesius / the New Light, Sendivogius

8. **Separation is the beginning of all generation.** It holds in the "lesser" mystery as in the "great".
   - "The greatest miracle of all in philosophy is separation: separation was the principle and beginning of all generation." (I.2 ¶25)
   - also: "And as it was in the great mystery, so it is in the lesser." (I.2 ¶25)
   - stated; QUOTED: Paracelsus

9. **Common fire is only an effect of the source-spirit.** Nature's source-spirit is distinct from its visible effects. Ordinary fire is only an excitation or effect of that prior power.
   - "the fontal Spirit of nature, apart from its manifestation" (I.2 ¶15)
   - also: "an excitation only or effect of the antecedent potency" (I.2 ¶15)
   - stated; ATWOOD, reporting the moderns with approval

10. **One substance, many forms; all its effects happen within itself.** Like white light split by a prism and recombined, the one "Alchemical Pan" has many offspring and returns to unity. The quintessence hardens, softens, destroys and nourishes within itself, without mixing with outside things.
    - "so is it said to be with the Alchemical Pan, who, being but one himself, is in his offspring multitudinous" (I.2 ¶34)
    - also: "For Pan contains Proteus" (I.2 ¶36); "so is the Mercurial quintessence said to produce all various effects, but within itself consummately without external reference, or elementary confusion" (I.2 ¶36)
    - stated; ATWOOD

11. **Salt, Sulfur and Mercury are modes of one thing; the names mark stages.** The three principles, Geber's argent vive and sulfur, and the "twofold" or "third" matter are not separate substances. They are relations (action and passion) within the one ethereal being. The many names mark its stages of growth, like the parts of a tree.
    - "The three principles, the Salt, Sulfur, and Mercury, are merely different as modes of being of the same thing" (I.2 ¶39)
    - also: "the many names arising out of the action and passion of these, do but indicate the stages of progress and development, as of a tree" (I.2 ¶39); "but of the magnetic relation, action, and passion of the Ethereal being in itself" (I.2 ¶42); "the thing which perfects in minerals, is the substance of argent vive and sulfur, proportionally commixt in the bowels of clean inspissate earth" (I.2 ¶42, Geber)
    - stated; ATWOOD

12. **Reading rule: keep the one simple Substance in mind.** All the animal and bird images derive from one simple Substance. The whole doctrine is to be read by this unity.
    - "Constantly holding in mind the simplicity of the Substance, whence these images are all derived" (I.2 ¶42)
    - also: "this Hermetic labyrinth of birds and wild beasts" (I.2 ¶42); "it will therefore be requisite to consider their whole doctrine with reference to this presumed unity" (I.2 ¶12)
    - stated; ATWOOD

13. **One material, diverse forms.**
    - "though the material is one throughout, forms are diverse" (I.2 ¶46)
    - stated; ATWOOD

14. **Change by one's own ferment, without addition.** Fermentation (juice to wine, milk to cheese) and metamorphosis (caterpillar to moth) turn one thing into other natures with nothing different added. So transmutation is not absurd. The philosophic Proteus, unlike species bound by natural law, is free to form itself around its own self-multiplying Light.
    - "without requiring the addition of anything different" (I.2 ¶48)
    - also: "the will of the philosophic Proteus is free to be drawn without hindrance to form itself about the universal magnet of its own infinite self-multiplicative Light" (I.2 ¶48)
    - stated (analogy); who or what "Proteus" is here is ambiguous (see Open questions). ATWOOD

15. **Gold is closest to the common Spirit.** Gold and the Spirit differ only in state and were originally one, so gold absorbs the Light completely (Signs 6).
    - "the commingling natures differ in state only and were originally one" (I.2 ¶48)
    - stated; ATWOOD

## Open questions

1. **Is the philosophers' Mercury the Ether?** At ¶12 Atwood sets out "to identify this mercury" and turns at once to the Ether, "a certain pure and hidden fire". No single sentence says Mercury = Ether. The chapter does chain stated identities: Quintessence = Free Ether (¶16); Proteus = Mercury = quintessence (¶37); Universal Spirit = Mercury (¶49); Stone = "pure Ethereality of nature" (¶44); Water of Hermes = "ethereal body of life and light" (¶43). Reading A: the chapter's answer is Mercury = Ether, reached by these steps (strongly supported, but composite and so interpreted). Reading B: Atwood only places the doctrines side by side and never asserts the identity in one sentence.

2. **How does the freed subject relate to the Light (¶8)?** The sentence is defective: "the thing transmuting also, when set free and segregated in its proper essentiality, the fermented Spirit assimilates the Light throughout." Reading A: the freed subject, as fermented Spirit, takes in the Light. Reading B: the freed subject is itself the Light the adept calls the only thing that multiplies. ¶39 ("the Mercury by its proper Light"), ¶44 ("its proper Light") and ¶33 ("a clear Light") suggest the Light is the subject's own, inherent light. ¶45 ("an inward agent", "This Form is Light") and ¶49 ("supply the deficiency of Form to the purified body of the Spirit") suggest the Light is a Form added to a purified matter that lacks it. The chapter does not settle whether the Light is inherent in, or supplied to, the subject.

3. **What is the "superior ferment or attraction" (¶4)?** ¶4 does not say. Candidates: the freed subject itself (¶8 "the thing transmuting"), the Light as "fermental principle" (¶44), or the Spirit's "own prepared must or leaven" (¶48). These may be one thing, but the text does not say so.

4. **Whose will drives the spirit through death (¶24)?** "she passes through death from one form into another, as in the chemic vessels, without self-discovery, being instigated by a most forcible excentric will, which she has no power but to obey". Reading A: this describes ordinary art and nature, an outside compulsion opposed to the alchemical method. It follows "is not the power of ordinary art, any more than of nature herself; but" and echoes "other external compellants". Reading B: it describes the alchemical reversal itself, with the operator's overpowering will driving the spirit, as in ¶23's forced passage through shapes. This bears on whether the operator forces the spirit or frees it to act by "her own intrinsical freed will".

5. **Can the subject be found on earth?** ¶18 (Lully, Latin): "quae reperiri non potest supra terram"; ¶20: "does not subsist of itself, separate or tangibly on earth"; ¶30: "You will not find it on earth"; ¶49: "not found of itself on earth". Against these, ¶29 (Lully): "it is found flowing upon earth". Reading A: it is present on earth only mixed and defiled, never pure, so the statements agree. Reading B: the sources conflict. ¶12's question "whether the same ancient material be yet on earth" stays formally open in this chapter.

6. **"Their earth", the "caverns of the mountains", "minera", "chalybs".** These are the sources or means of extraction (¶10, ¶30, ¶33), and the chapter explains none of them. For chalybs: either an agent used in the work, or another epithet of the subject (¶33).

7. **"Introversion" and the possibly garbled ¶49 clause.** "the reduction of bodies to their original matter, by introversion of the generated life, is requisite to a true manifestation and permanence in any form, as by the ordinary process of unassisted nature, as is evident". Reading A: as worded, reduction happens as in nature's ordinary processes. Reading B: a word such as "not" is missing, and reduction is impossible by unassisted nature. The next clause ("she must be helped") supports B. ¶27 also defers "introversion" to fuller treatment "hereafter".

8. **What is "Proteus" in ¶48?** "the will of the philosophic Proteus is free". Reading A: the fermented Vital Spirit of the preceding sentence, which "being transmuted, transmutes". Reading B: the shape-changing matter of the Art in general, with an unexplained "will". ¶37 names the subject as Proteus = Mercury = quintessence, which fits either.

9. **Whose "waters of her extreme life" (¶36)?** "her" may be nature (which reacts next) or the Matter. A parallel ambiguity: "the fermental principle of the spirit and her distilled quintessence" (¶33), where "her" is the spirit or nature/the Matter.

10. **Order of colours and stages.** The chapter gives three partial sequences that do not line up: ¶40 (sublimed → putrefying → calcined → dissolving → whitened oil → final red), ¶29 Arnold (clear and volatile → citrine → saltish), ¶11 Aquinas (white, red, yellow, black, no order). ¶36 puts a first whiteness "out of the philosophical contrition" before the annihilate condition. ¶42 warns that writers describe stages "without note of order". How these fit together is not stated. The four phases of ¶44 (preparation, generation, birth, specification) are also not tied to these stage names.

11. **The "great" and the "lesser" mystery (¶25).** "And as it was in the great mystery, so it is in the lesser." Reading A: great = the original generation of all things, lesser = the Art (supported by the preceding "beginning of all generation"). Reading B: the referents are undefined here.

12. **Mercury and "his sister" (¶11).** The sentence is defective (words missing before "that thou addest"). Reading A: the error is to require a second substance as partner; Mercury alone is agent and patient (supported by "nothing different must be added" and "conceives itself"). Reading B: the parenthesis only glosses the pair as agent and patient, without saying which is which. This bears on whether the work uses one substance or two.

13. **Two Elixirs?** ¶46 names "the elixir of the wise" as the result in the vegetable kingdom. ¶49 speaks of things raised to "equalize the Philosophical Elixir". ¶25 has Ripley's elixir from recombined elements. The chapter does not say whether these are the same.

14. **"Are by God endued with greater and more ignoble perfection" (¶49).** Reading A: living things receive a greater, nobler perfection (supported by Atwood's gloss "where life predominates over corporeal consistency" and Geber's ranking). Reading B: the printed word "ignoble" stands as written.
