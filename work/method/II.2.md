# Method material — II.2 Of the Mysteries

## The subject (what the work is done on)

1. **The universal Spirit as it first becomes conscious in man: a receptive, convertible intellect.** The subject is the universal Mundane Spirit as first revealed in human life. Aristotle calls it passive intellect because it takes on whatever it receives, good or bad, true or delusive.
   - "which Aristotle, in his Metaphysics, calls passive intellect, because capable of receiving and being converted to all" (II.2 ¶17)
   - also: "the universal Mundane Spirit, as it is at first consciously revealed in the recipient life of humanity" (II.2 ¶17); "the best pr worst inclination, the highest truth or the most delusive imaginings" (II.2 ¶17)
   - stated; ATWOOD (adopting Aristotle's term).

2. **The same thing is the mesmeric agent, the Common Soul and the alchemists' despised subject-matter.** Atwood names one subject under several names: the agent of modern mesmerism, the Common Soul, and the alchemists' "abject" matter. That matter is common, present everywhere, unvalued, and turned to any will without anyone knowing it.
   - "the identical agent which is spread abroad in the present day mesmerizing, the photogenic medium, our New Imponderable, for it is the Common Soul; also the subject-matter of the Alchemists aforesaid" (II.2 ¶18)
   - also: "a thing indifferent, abject, and exposed in all hands" (II.2 ¶18); "invisibly and unconsciously converted to every will and various use" (II.2 ¶18); "It is what the world cares not for, as the adept says, but disesteems it; it hath it in its sight, carries it in its hands, yet is ignorant thereof" (II.2 ¶18)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with the adept (II.2 n16) quoted and endorsed.

3. **The ancient Theurgists and the modern mesmerists work on the same material; only principle and aim differ.** Mesmerists take the "Universal Medium" as it naturally comes. Yet it can be improved, raised or lowered, and has a "right determination". The ancients worked the same material by established principles toward a philosophic end.
   - "although working in the same material and with similar instruments, on the same ground, yet their practice was different" (II.2 ¶8)
   - also: "it was conducted upon established principles and with a truly philosophic as well as benevolent aim" (II.2 ¶8); "as respects the Universal Medium, they even, who believe in such a thing, take it as it presents itself naturally, having no knowledge of the capabilities or means of improvement, whither it is able to ascend or descend, or what is its right determination" (II.2 ¶7)
   - Same material: stated; ATWOOD. That the Universal Medium is this subject: interpreted (II.2 ¶7–8, ¶18 "photogenic medium"). See Open questions 1–2.

4. **In its natural state the subject is the imaginative ("phantastic", "Etherial") spirit, an intermediate nature.** It lies between rational and animal life and between bodily and bodiless. It links the divine to the lowest things. In man it ought to serve a divine understanding. Unpurified, it rules most of our thinking through imagination.
   - "the phantastical condition also of the natural understanding essence, before it is purified by art and exalted" (II.2 ¶10)
   - also: "situated on the confines of the rational and brutal life, and is of a corporeal and incorporeal degree; and it is the medium which conjoins divine natures with the lowest of all" (II.2 ¶10, Synesius); "Synesius describes not only the life that is operated upon and, in graceful terms, the artifice" (II.2 ¶14)
   - The intermediate nature: stated; QUOTED: Synesius, endorsed. That this spirit is what the art works on: interpreted (II.2 ¶10 "purified by art", ¶14 "the life that is operated upon", ¶15–16).

5. **The natural subject is degraded, diseased and defiled, and the alchemists' crude "vulgar Matter" is this same defiled state.** In ordinary life the spirit is unfit for true knowledge. It "falsifies and is dulled". The alchemists' raw Matter is described as mixed, fallen from its sphere and defiled, and Atwood introduces it as a consequence ("It is therefore") of the spirit's impurity.
   - "the vulgar Matter as it is at first made known, full of heterogeneous qualities and notions, as a subject fallen from its sphere and defiled" (II.2 ¶16)
   - also: "condemn the Spirit of the natural life as degraded and incapable of true intelligence" (II.2 ¶8, Theurgists); "in what the disease of the Spirit consists, and from what cause it falsifies and is dulled" (II.2 ¶9); "the microcosmic life is not dissimilar from the vitality of the greater world, which is included by respiration in the blood of all creatures" (II.2 ¶18); "how much such a life is in need of melioration" (II.2 ¶18)
   - Degraded or diseased state: stated; ATWOOD, with the Theurgists endorsed. Vulgar Matter = the impure spirit: interpreted (II.2 ¶16 "It is therefore", following ¶15).

6. **One essence that contains everything yet is divided in each particular: the "Compound Simple".** Because it is one and many at once, the magians wrote about it confusingly.
   - "This is the Monster and the Eternal Riddle explained to common sense as suits it, but misunderstood to this day; that Compound Simple and ground of the magians’ elements" (II.2 ¶19)
   - also: "an essence all comprehending, yet separated in each particular, by so great an interval from itself" (II.2 ¶19)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

7. **A passive, undetermined middle substance that looks dead outside but lives within.** The subject is neither perfect nor wholly imperfect. It is open to every extreme, which is why art can perfect it (see Principles 4).
   - "but this Universal Spirit is described as a middle substance --- passive, undetermined, susceptible of conversion and all extremes" (II.2 ¶26)
   - also: "although our metal is exteriorly dead, yet it has life within" (II.2 ¶26, Aristhenes); "this our Undetermined Subject" (II.2 ¶27)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with Aristhenes endorsed.

8. **Mesmerism has not investigated the "subject-being" that causes its own effects.** Atwood points to a cause behind the phenomena that mesmerists have not examined. The chapter does not define it further.
   - "has it yet made an attempt even to investigate the subject-being, the cause of its own effects?" (II.2 ¶7)
   - interpreted (that it denotes the subject above); ATWOOD.

## The agent (what does the work: fire, spirit, light, intellect…)

1. **The subject purifies itself: it is both agent and patient, and these two sides are the "Hermetic luminaries", the philosophical Sun and Moon.** No outside substance does the work. The one thing acts on itself. Its active and passive sides pass through phases from imperfection to perfection.
   - "the one thing requisite, purifying and to be purified itself by itself, in turn agent and patient, which are the Hermetic luminaries" (II.2 ¶26)
   - also: "in their full representative advancement, the Sun and Moon philosophical, passing through many phases from imperfection to perfection in the true magistery" (II.2 ¶26)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

2. **Nature has one secret agent in the universe, distinct in every species by its seed. The life of things is a hidden light seen by "soaring minds".** The verse supports ¶27's account of the art. The chapter does not say outright that this agent is the subject of ¶26.
   - "For she her secret agent doth possess, Which in the universe is only one, But is distinct thro’ species numberless, According to their seeds" (II.2 ¶28)
   - also: "Their life is light which in them hidden lies, Discerned by the eyes of soaring minds" (II.2 ¶28)
   - stated (in verse); QUOTED: Eirenaeus (II.2 n19), endorsed ("Nor may this seem a fable to the wise"). Identity with the self-purifying subject: interpreted.

3. **Operative "media" or "medicines", not contemplation or belief, did the work in the Mysteries.** These media healed imperfect souls, and bodies too. The chapter never says what they were. The particulars are deferred to "the Practice" (II.2 ¶24).
   - "by certain ineffable and sublime media that Theurgists became cognizant partakers in the Wisdom of true Being" (II.2 ¶5)
   - also: "it was by arts divinely potent, and not by theoretic contemplation only or by mere doctrinal faith or representations either of reality" (II.2 ¶5); "Heraclitus calls these medicines, as being the help and remedy of imperfect souls" (II.2 ¶5); "by divine arts and media, he healed and purified the souls of his followers" (II.2 ¶11, of Pythagoras)
   - stated; QUOTED: Iamblicus, Heraclitus, Pythagoras' biographer, all endorsed.

4. **The divine assists, and for a few gives, the restoration.** Restoration comes to "one or two" as a gift of divinity and initiation, and the soul gains strength "with divine assistance". Clemens calls the Lord himself the hierophant who seals and illuminates the adept.
   - "this restoration indeed one or two may obtain as a gift of divinity and initiation" (II.2 ¶13)
   - also: "the soul acquires fortitude with divine assistance" (II.2 ¶13); "it is the Lord himself who is the hierophant; he sets his seal upon the adept whom he illuminates with his beams" (II.2 ¶5)
   - Synesius: stated; QUOTED: Synesius, endorsed. Clemens' image of the divine as initiator: interpreted; QUOTED: Clemens Alexandrinus, not glossed by Atwood.

5. **Will is the main purifying force on the human side** (see Operator 1).
   - "Will is the greatest part of purgation" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

## The operator (requirements: knowledge, moral state, will, guidance, secrecy…)

1. **Desire, will and consent come first. Without them technique fails.** Only someone conscious of "something better" seeks improvement. Will does most of the purifying. Consent ("assent") is what gives the soul any means of purification. Disciplines are far more useful when willingly endured.
   - "desire is the first requisite; indeed, without desire on our part, art will labor for us in vain, since Will is the greatest part of purgation" (II.2 ¶13)
   - also: "he who lives in the consciousness of something better will meditate improvement" (II.2 ¶13); "the soul is deprived of every purifying machine because destitute of assent, which is the greatest pledge of reconciliation" (II.2 ¶13, Synesius); "disciplines willingly endured become of far greater utility" (II.2 ¶13); "the conditions of desire and will, so indispensable for advancement" (II.2 ¶14)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with Synesius endorsed.

2. **Only a philosopher who labors rationally succeeds. The idle, the vicious and fools are excluded.** The operator must have the "principles of melioration" in himself. No one can pass on what he does not have, and one has it only by rational labor.
   - "None but a philosopher ever achieved the work, or for reasons that are imperative, ever will." (II.2 ¶29)
   - also: "The idle and vicious are totally excluded, nor are the rewards of Wisdom to be won by fools wanting the very principles of melioration in themselves" (II.2 ¶29); "He only that hath it can impart --- and he only has it who has labored rationally in the pursuit" (II.2 ¶29, rendering "Nemo enim dat id, quod non habet"); "The earth giveth much mould, whereof earthen vessels are made, but little dust that gold cometh of" (II.2 ¶29, Esdras)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

3. **Three things are needed: knowing the Art, understanding its veiled "expressions", and having "the medium".**
   - "he that knows the Art, and the expressions, and hath the medium, will be richer than any other" (II.2 ¶18)
   - interpreted ("the medium" read as the subject, which ¶18 calls "the photogenic medium"); QUOTED: the adept (II.2 n16), endorsed.

4. **Long preparation, intellectual training and "auxiliaries" are needed. Entranced subjects without a guide go astray.** Pythagoras put disciples through years of ordeals before teaching them. Atwood stresses "intellectual preparation and auxiliaries" for the first step. Mesmeric sleepwalkers fail because they have no guide and cannot tell true from false.
   - "the necessity of intellectual preparation and auxiliaries" (II.2 ¶20)
   - also: "Pythagoras instituted long preparations and ordeals to train the minds of his disciples, previously to admitting them into the deeper mysteries of his school" (II.2 ¶11); "without a guide, unable of themselves to educate their latent talents, or discriminate truth from falsehood in their revelations" (II.2 ¶7)
   - Preparation: stated; ATWOOD and QUOTED: Iamblicus. That a guide is required: interpreted from the failure Atwood describes in II.2 ¶7.

5. **Love of truth and faith drive the ascent.**
   - "it is carried up through the love of truth by faith into vivid contact with its Whole" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

6. **Heavy, sleepless labor and self-correction.** The closing Latin verse says the ascent is not light: much sweat, nights by the lamp, and erasing in oneself what one has just praised.
   - "Non levis adscensus, si quis petat ardua, sudor Plurimus hunc tollit" (II.2 ¶30)
   - interpreted from our own translation ("by: ours"); voice UNCLEAR (unattributed verse closing the chapter).

## The procedure and its stages (in the order the chapter gives, if it gives one)

1. **The order of the Mysteries is: the first revelations of the natural spirit, then the Purificative Rites, then the final initiations or Greater Mysteries.** The Lesser Mysteries apparently went past the first sphere of the natural spirit's revelations. After "the revealment of the Medial Life" came the Purificative Rites, which restore reason's rule in the soul. These rites prepare for the final initiations and are not the goal. Only Greater-Mystery initiates were promised final assimilation. Atwood hedges each step ("as it appears", "as we take it").
   - "Close upon the revealment of the Medial Life then, as we take it, in order of the Mysteries followed the Purificative Rites, which were designed also to restore the monarchy of reason in the soul" (II.2 ¶20)
   - also: "not either as an end so much, but as preparatory to undergoing the final initiations" (II.2 ¶20); "passing these, as it appears, in the Lesser Mysteries, to dissolve the medium more entirely" (II.2 ¶8); "to those who were initiated in the Greater Mysteries only" (II.2 ¶5)
   - stated, with Atwood's hedges; ATWOOD. How the ¶8 and ¶20 sequences line up: interpreted (see Open questions 4).

2. **Initiation marks the start of a new life of reason and virtue.**
   - "Initia, for they were the beginning of a life of reason and virtue" (II.2 ¶4)
   - stated; QUOTED: the Platonist, Psellus, Cicero, endorsed.

3. **The core operation: separate the Vital Spirit from the defilements and imprinted images that birth into the senses left in it, by dissolving the medium further. The ancients began with this.** Atwood says modern experiment ends where the ancients started: purifying the Vital Spirit "in its proper Light". The Mysteries did this through lustrations or purifications.
   - "to segregate the Vital Spirit away from those defilements and imaginative impressures which, by the birth into sense, had become implanted there" (II.2 ¶8)
   - also: "to dissolve the medium more entirely" (II.2 ¶8); "purifying the Vital Spirit in its proper Light" (II.2 ¶24); "by the lustrations in the Mysteries, as they describe, the soul becomes liberated and passes into a divine condition of being" (II.2 ¶9); "the sacred rites of Bacchus pertained to the purification of souls" (II.2 ¶5, Servius)
   - stated; ATWOOD. "Proper Light" is not defined.

4. **Quieting the senses is not enough. The leftover sense impressions and false imagination must be destroyed, and all outside influence shut out.** Trance silences the senses, but their imprint remains.
   - "a false imagination, which it is requisite to destroy, as well as to banish all influxions from without, before the understanding spirit can superinduce Divinity" (II.2 ¶10)
   - also: "when in a state of trance they are quiescent, their impressure yet remains" (II.2 ¶10)
   - stated; ATWOOD, citing Synesius.

5. **Cleanse first, then fix.** Like dyers, the teacher spends years wiping stains from the soul before fixing doctrine in it, so that it lasts.
   - "as dyers previously purify garments, and then fix in the colors with which they wish them to be imbued in order that the dye may not be evanescent" (II.2 ¶11)
   - also: "wiping away the stains which had insinuated themselves into our breasts, till, after the lapse of some years, we became fit recipients" (II.2 ¶11)
   - stated; QUOTED: Iamblicus, endorsed.

6. **Before contemplation, clear everything else from the mind and make it clear and receptive before the light of Reason. This is the condition everything depends on.**
   - "that all else be effectually obliterated, and the mental atmosphere made clear and passive before its objective light. Without this, they promise nothing; with it, all." (II.2 ¶22)
   - stated; QUOTED: the ancients, endorsed.

7. **Turn inward the faculty that now looks outward, and remove natural impediments. The prior source-life may then show itself.**
   - "If that were applied inwardly which now looks out, and every natural impediment removed, experience might then reveal to us the antecedent life" (II.2 ¶21)
   - stated (as a conditional); ATWOOD.

8. **Once deeply conscious inside the spirit, exert the intellect at once and "solve", that is comprehend, the spirit on its own ground. Reason must not stay passive.** The impure perceptive medium needs "alternation and solution" so that good and evil can be discerned.
   - "solve, that is comprehend, it on its own ground" (II.2 ¶15)
   - also: "if he assume not quickly his intellectual energies" (II.2 ¶15); "it needs alternation and solution, as the oracles teach, for the discernment of good and evil, and the proper choice of life" (II.2 ¶15)
   - The gloss solve = comprehend: stated; ATWOOD. "Alternation and solution": interpreted; QUOTED: the oracles (see Open questions 6).

9. **The great labor is to redirect the flow of life and purify its natural source: the cleansing of the Augean stable.**
   - "But this is that Augean Stable that was to be cleansed, that most famous labor of the philosophic Hercules" (II.2 ¶19)
   - also: "to turn the current of life into another channel, and purify the natural source" (II.2 ¶19)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

10. **The ascent is slow and achieved by Art, away from the lower desires. "Old age" means this late, gradual attainment, not literal age.**
    - "by a transition gradually effected by Art away from the profound strains of a baser affection" (II.2 ¶13)
    - also: "Plato appropriates the possession of Wisdom to old age" (II.2 ¶13); "nor is to be hoped for at all either in the early awakening of the life within" (II.2 ¶13)
    - stated; ATWOOD.

11. **A stage of trials and temptations must be passed before central illumination.** Breaking the soul's compact with the senses stirs up forceful opposition, which the labors of Hercules represent. It lasts until the higher spirit is beyond nature's reach.
    - "those temptations and trials which the consciousness must necessarily pass through, all the while regressive, before it reaches into the central illumination of truth" (II.2 ¶14)
    - also: "the labors of Hercules, and the dangers which he was required to endure, and which every one must experience who bravely contends for liberty" (II.2 ¶13, Synesius)
    - stated; ATWOOD and QUOTED: Synesius (the Sacred Discourse), endorsed. For "regressive" see Open questions 8.

12. **In alchemical terms: prepare, dissolve, calcine and wash the crude Matter until it becomes the Mercury of the Philosophers. The first rule is perfect solution.**
    - "Take, says Albertus Mangus, the occult Arcanum, which is our brass, and wash it that it may be pure and clean. The first rule of the work is a perfect solution" (II.2 ¶16)
    - also: "Hence all those preparations, solutions, calcinations, etc. before it becomes to be the Mercury of the Philosophers" (II.2 ¶16)
    - Operations: stated; QUOTED: the Alchemists and Albertus Mangus. That they are the spirit's purification: interpreted (II.2 ¶16 "It is therefore"). See Open questions 6.

13. **Start from the least-valued thing and bring it by art into "the ferment". Keep what the philosopher values, reject what most people value, and remove only what is superfluous.**
    - "Take that which is least, and draw it by artifice into the true ferment of philosophers" (II.2 ¶26)
    - also: "That which in the eyes of a philosopher is the most precious should be collected, and that That which the many set more value on be rejected" (II.2 ¶26); "only in the preparation removing superfluities" (II.2 ¶26)
    - interpreted ("least" read with the "abject" subject of ¶18; "ferment" not defined); QUOTED: Aristhenes, endorsed.

14. **The art picks the subject up where nature leaves it and handles it correctly through amalgamation, distillation, filtration, digestion and finally sublimation to the Head of the Vessel.** Repeated solution and skilled handling turn this same principle into the Stone. The operations are only named here; their explanation is promised for later.
    - "the hermetic art would seem to consist simply in the right disposition and manipulation of this our Undetermined Subject, taking her where nature leaves" (II.2 ¶27)
    - also: "as of amalgamation, distillation, filtration, digestion, and lastly by sublimation to the Head of an appropriate Vessel" (II.2 ¶27); "it is the very same which solved and resolved, and wisely manipulated, becomes to be the concrete Stone of philosophers" (II.2 ¶25)
    - stated; ATWOOD.

## Signs (what shows the work is proceeding)

1. **The spirit mirrors the mind. When the mind is purified the vehicle is fine and clear-seeing; when the mind is sensual it is dull and earthy.** The state of the vehicle therefore shows how far purification has gone.
   - "the primary and proper vehicle of the mind, when it is in a wise and purified condition, is attenuated and clear seeing; when however the mind is sensually affected, then this vehicle is dulled and becomes terrene" (II.2 ¶15)
   - also: "the Spirit understand the affections of the mind, and reflects its image as it is, whether good or evil" (II.2 ¶15)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

2. **The ability to grasp universals shows the soul can work without imagination, and promises a higher reality.**
   - "the perception of Universals proves that it is able" (II.2 ¶12)
   - also: "As a rational promise to this life of a higher reality, the subsistence of these Universals cannot be too often or too distinctly brought to mind" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; QUOTED: Olympiodorus, endorsed and extended by ATWOOD.

3. **When the soul breaks with the senses, violent opposition arises.**
   - "the material inflictors will then be roused to vengeance, by the decrees of fate, against the rebels of her laws" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated that it happens; reading it as a sign of progress is interpreted; QUOTED: Synesius, endorsed.

4. **The philosophical Sun and Moon pass through many phases from imperfection to perfection.**
   - "passing through many phases from imperfection to perfection in the true magistery" (II.2 ¶26)
   - stated that phases occur; the chapter does not describe them; ATWOOD.

5. **Lucidity, prevision and shared sense or thought are not signs of the higher work.** Atwood treats them as the first-sphere revelations that the Theurgists did not value and passed in the Lesser Mysteries.
   - "nor did they therefore value the revelations of its first included sphere" (II.2 ¶8)
   - interpreted (linking ¶6–7 on mesmeric phenomena with ¶8); ATWOOD.

## Impediments and dangers

1. **Ordinary thought depends on the senses and always works through images, so it cannot touch true being.** Sense draws the inner light outward until it forgets its source.
   - "we are not accustomed to cogitate without imagination" (II.2 ¶10, Synesius)
   - also: "reason is always more or less debilitated in its energies by the habitual dependence on sense for data and objective proof" (II.2 ¶10); "that very light is drawn outwardly, and enchanted by sense that it is internally unconscious and oblivious everywhere of the great Identity whence it springs" (II.2 ¶21)
   - stated; QUOTED: Synesius and ATWOOD.

2. **Imagination and divine inspiration exclude each other.**
   - "enthusiasm and the phantasy are contrary to each other" (II.2 ¶12)
   - also: "The phantasy, says he, is an impediment to our intellectual conception" (II.2 ¶12)
   - stated; QUOTED: Olympiodorus, endorsed.

3. **The uninitiated mind is overgrown with obstructions.**
   - "dense thickets, which are full of briars" (II.2 ¶11)
   - stated (they "surround the intellect and heart of those who have not been purely initiated"); QUOTED: Iamblicus.

4. **The worst evil is not knowing that one needs improvement.** That ignorance removes the desire the work requires.
   - "which being ignorant that it is ignorant has no desire to emerge" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; QUOTED: Plato, the ancients, endorsed.

5. **Breaking the soul's compact with the senses is a hard fight.**
   - "it is no trifling contest to abrogate the confession and compact which she has made with sense" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; QUOTED: Synesius, endorsed.

6. **Looking into oneself with an unpurified spirit is useless and dangerous.** One sees only shadowy leftover impressions, not one's real substance. The loosed vehicle produces dream-like or drug-like fantasies that, if they persist, would consume reason.
   - "If, therefore, with this same misunderstanding and infected Spirit, we enter in for the discovery and contemplation of ourselves, it will be useless" (II.2 ¶14)
   - also: "we shall not there discern the true hypostasis, but err amongst the turbulent and shadowy impressures" (II.2 ¶14); "our vehicle disports herself oftentimes in many mingling forms; as it is with those who dream or make to themselves a fool’s paradise with the druggist’s gas" (II.2 ¶14)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

7. **Passive dreaming in the imagination is perilous.** Its contents are often falser than ordinary sense images.
   - "who trust themselves to remain passively dreaming in the region of the phantasy, with its notions and instincts, often more false, fleeting, and evil that the corporeal images with which sense is conversant" (II.2 ¶22)
   - stated; QUOTED: the ancients, endorsed.

8. **The spirit "sphinx" overwhelms anyone who enters it without understanding it. If reason stays passive, the spirit takes over the whole mind.**
   - "that very Sphinx of the Labyrinth, the devourer of strangers and all who have not the wit to unriddle her and know themselves" (II.2 ¶15)
   - also: "if reason remains passive, this nature at length prevailing, will ravage and devastate and take possession of the whole mind" (II.2 ¶15); "the whole identity, being submerged together, would be converted to her life" (II.2 ¶14)
   - The danger: stated; ATWOOD. Sphinx = unpurified Phantastic Spirit: interpreted (II.2 ¶15, next sentence).

9. **The spirit can fall as far as it can rise.** If the will turns downward or evil agencies interfere, it sinks.
   - "the Spirit grows heavy and sinks into profound Hades" (II.2 ¶14)
   - also: "if the will incline downwards, persisting to grovel, or evil agencies intervene" (II.2 ¶14); "capable of so much higher, even as they say of the highest intelligence, yet in proportion may suffer also the most fearful degradation" (II.2 ¶14)
   - stated; QUOTED: the Sacred Discourse, and ATWOOD.

10. **At the threshold, the human spirit's own resemblances lure people into "Magic", where many go astray.**
    - "the Sphinx is even now present, rapidly propounding her dark riddles in the world, images of the obscure and intricate nature of the human spirit" (II.2 ¶19)
    - also: "into that Hermetic wilderness and wild of Magic in which so many adventurers have gone astray" (II.2 ¶19)
    - stated; ATWOOD.

11. **The understanding may be too corrupted to reflect reason. In that case introspection is useless.** Atwood treats this as a possibility to be tested. She also raises the bias of sense, birth and education as an objection.
    - "if by this traditionary fall and outbirth, the understanding is so polluted as to be no more able, as Lord Bacon supposes (17), to reflect the total reason to itself" (II.2 ¶21)
    - also: "retaining as it must the original bias, not only of sense but of birth and education, its experience will be neither trustworthy nor important" (II.2 ¶20, anticipated objection)
    - stated; ATWOOD, citing Bacon's supposition and objectors she answers.

12. **No mind, however well formed, can examine itself at first.**
    - "the best constituted minds would be inadequate to self-inspection on their first entrance into life" (II.2 ¶15)
    - stated; ATWOOD.

13. **Mesmerism fails for lack of principles: it gives no guidance and no discrimination, takes the medium in its natural state, and repeats the same mechanical operations.**
    - "Our sleepwalkers are little better than dreamers" (II.2 ¶7)
    - also: "repeating the same mechanical operations, and witnessing similar effects continually over and over again" (II.2 ¶7)
    - stated; ATWOOD.

14. **The first gates of the path are barred.** The image is not explained.
    - "And the strong massy gates thro’ which we pass, In our first course, are bound with chains of brass;" (II.2 ¶1)
    - interpreted; QUOTED: Oracle of Apollo, from Eusebius (epigraph).

## The result

1. **Real knowledge of Being (Ontological Wisdom), gained by the rites themselves.**
   - "philosophy, that is to say, Ontological Wisdom, as the true object of initiation" (II.2 ¶4)
   - also: "became cognizant partakers in the Wisdom of true Being" (II.2 ¶5); "philosophy, according to Strabo, was the object of the Eleusian rites" (II.2 ¶5)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with Iamblicus and Strabo endorsed.

2. **Assimilation to, and contact with, the source the soul came from. Reason becomes receptive and is filled with its Whole, and the truth reached is the same for all.**
   - "an assimilation was induced, and final contact with the object of rational inquiry, which is that identity whence, as a principle, we make our first descent" (II.2 ¶5)
   - also: "wherein this reason, becoming passive, receives the substance of her Whole" (II.2 ¶13); "it is carried up through the love of truth by faith into vivid contact with its Whole" (II.2 ¶13); "one and the same in all, where difference is merged in objective union" (II.2 ¶13)
   - Assimilation: stated; QUOTED: the Pythagoreans, Plato, endorsed. "Whole": interpreted (not defined in the chapter); ATWOOD.

3. **The inner "divine eye", once purified, sees the truth of all beings, and nature as a whole is unveiled.**
   - "that divine eye which, as Plato says, is better worth than 10,000 corporeal eyes" (II.2 ¶8)
   - also: "by looking through this alone, when it is purified and strengthened by appropriate aids, the truth pertaining to all beings is perceived" (II.2 ¶8); "nature and all things she contains were unveiled" (II.2 ¶5, Clemens)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with Clemens Alexandrinus.

4. **The spirit is cleared and purged and returns to its original simplicity. The soul passes into a divine condition.**
   - "how it becomes clarified and defecated, and restored to its innate simplicity" (II.2 ¶9)
   - also: "the soul becomes liberated and passes into a divine condition of being" (II.2 ¶9)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

5. **Wisdom as intuitive intellect free of imaginative error ("old age").**
   - "signifying by this Intellect diving intuitively without imaginative error" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

6. **The higher spirit rises above nature and receives the divine. Reason rules again, and the hidden causal power of the understanding becomes conscious.**
   - "till the Understanding Spirit rises superior to the dominion of nature and is palced beyond the reach of her hands" (II.2 ¶13)
   - also: "before the understanding spirit can superinduce Divinity" (II.2 ¶10); "restore the monarchy of reason in the soul" (II.2 ¶20); "developing to consciousness its occult Causality" (II.2 ¶22); "experience might then reveal to us the antecedent life" (II.2 ¶21)
   - stated; QUOTED: Synesius, endorsed, and ATWOOD.

7. **The initiate's life is purified, perfected and essentially changed: Self-Knowledge, immortality, and healing of soul and body.**
   - "were real and efficacious for the highest ends that philosophy can propose to itself, namely, the purification and perfection of human life" (II.2 ¶24)
   - also: "the Self-Knowledge and perfection of life and immortality promised" (II.2 ¶7); "they possessed a power of healing the body likewise" (II.2 ¶5); ordinary worship continues "without essentially, changing the state of life" (II.2 ¶5)
   - stated; ATWOOD. That the Mysteries do essentially change the state of life is interpreted from the contrast in II.2 ¶5.

8. **In alchemical terms, the Mercury of the Philosophers: pure, agile, intelligent, living, back in its own sphere.**
   - "the Mercury of the Philosophers --- pure, agile, intelligent, living --- as they say, in her own sphere, as a queen upon her throne" (II.2 ¶16)
   - stated as the alchemists' end; that it is the purified spirit is interpreted (II.2 ¶15–16; compare ¶13 "that which rightly belongs on high"); QUOTED: the Alchemists.

9. **The Philosophers' Stone is the same despised principle, solved and worked. In its pure passive state it mirrors the reason of nature and mediates divine experience.**
   - "it is the very same which solved and resolved, and wisely manipulated, becomes to be the concrete Stone of philosophers" (II.2 ¶25)
   - also: "in its pure, passive expanse, a mirror of the catholic reason of nature, and the medium of that holy and sublime experience granted to man alone in the divine alliance" (II.2 ¶25)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

10. **The subject is established in a new, concentric "Form of Light".** The chapter does not explain the phrase.
    - "establishing her in a new and concentric Form of Light" (II.2 ¶27)
    - stated; ATWOOD.

11. **The rejected Keystone regains her Head place, but only after long labor.**
    - "all the labors of that Heroic Intellect to be passed through before the rejected Keystone regains her Head place" (II.2 ¶29)
    - interpreted (see Open questions 9); ATWOOD.

## Principles (general laws of the work)

1. **The Hermetic Art is Theurgy, an operative art. Its rites really produce the Wisdom; they do not merely stand for it.**
   - "represents the rites themselves as really efficacious to procure it" (II.2 ¶4)
   - also: "it was by arts divinely potent, and not by theoretic contemplation only or by mere doctrinal faith or representations either of reality" (II.2 ¶5)
   - stated; ATWOOD (against Taylor's "immaterial ceremonies"), with Iamblicus endorsed.

2. **The rites follow fixed, rational, unchangeable rules.**
   - "Theurgic rites conspiring to this end were scientifically disposed and early defined by intellectual canons" (II.2 ¶13)
   - also: "neither is it lawful to consider these canons as mutable" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; QUOTED: Iamblicus, endorsed.

3. **The whole mystic philosophy depends on purifying the understanding and making its hidden causal power conscious.** The doubt is about the means, not about whether self-knowledge is possible.
   - "namely, of purifying the human Understanding Essence, and developing to consciousness its occult Causality" (II.2 ¶22)
   - also: "the doubt rather remains also about the discovery of means, than the possibility of self-knowledge" (II.2 ¶23)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

4. **Art can perfect only a middle thing. The perfect cannot be changed, and the utterly imperfect cannot be made perfect.**
   - "From that which is perfect nothing more can be made; for a perfect species is not changed in its nature, neither from an utterly imperfect thing can art produce perfection" (II.2 ¶26)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

5. **Once the mind has entered the spirit, it either leads it or is led by it.**
   - "It is necessary that the mind, once seated in this Spirit, should either follow or draw or be drawn by it" (II.2 ¶14)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

6. **The spirit mediates in both directions.** It carries sense impressions up into opinion and fixes intellect's impressions down into life. It wraps around the intellect at its centre.
   - "it grows upon and fashions all the powers the soul, exciting in opinion the illuminations from the senses, and fixes in that life the impressions which descend from intellect" (II.2 ¶15)
   - also: "it folds itself about the indivisibility of true intellect (which is in its center)" (II.2 ¶15, Proclus)
   - stated; QUOTED: Iamblicus, Proclus.

7. **The imaginative spirit can be purified even in animals, so the rational soul is at fault if she leaves on earth what belongs on high.**
   - "the phantastic Spirit may be purified, even in brutes" (II.2 ¶13)
   - also: "leaves lingering upon earth that which rightly belongs on high" (II.2 ¶13)
   - stated; QUOTED: Synesius, endorsed.

8. **The ancients began where modern experiment ends.**
   - "For does not all our experimentalism and philosophy end in fact where the ancients began, purifying the Vital Spirit in its proper Light?" (II.2 ¶24)
   - stated; ATWOOD.

9. **The Mysteries hold a secret that ordinary worship lacks, and it is spoken of only in riddles that initiates understand.**
   - "There was undoubtedly a secret, hanging about these celebrations, both Ethnic and Christian" (II.2 ¶5)
   - also: "except in obscure terms, in such a manner, however, as that the faithful who are initiated may comprehend, and the rest be discouraged" (II.2 ¶5, St Cyrillus)
   - stated; ATWOOD, with St Cyrillus.

## Identities asserted

- Hermetic Art = Art of Divine works = Theurgy — "Now the Egyptians, that is the Hermetic Art, or Art of Divine works, was by the Greeks called Theurgy" (II.2 ¶3) — ATWOOD (grammar defective; see Open questions 10).
- philosophy = Ontological Wisdom = the object of initiation — "philosophy, that is to say, Ontological Wisdom, as the true object of initiation" (II.2 ¶4) — ATWOOD.
- Initia = beginning of a life of reason and virtue — "Initia, for they were the beginning of a life of reason and virtue" (II.2 ¶4) — QUOTED: the Platonist, Psellus, Cicero.
- theurgic media = medicines of imperfect souls — "Heraclitus calls these medicines, as being the help and remedy of imperfect souls" (II.2 ¶5) — QUOTED: Heraclitus.
- hierophant = the Lord — "it is the Lord himself who is the hierophant" (II.2 ¶5) — QUOTED: Clemens Alexandrinus.
- Animal Magnetism = Mesmerism = Vital Magnetism — "Animal Magnetism, or Mesmerism, or Vital Magnetism, or by whatever other term the unknown agency is better expressed" (II.2 ¶6) — ATWOOD.
- the theurgists' material = the mesmerists' material — "working in the same material and with similar instruments, on the same ground" (II.2 ¶8) — ATWOOD.
- natural understanding essence = Etherial Spirit — "the phantastical condition also of the natural understanding essence, before it is purified by art and exalted. This Etherial Spirit" (II.2 ¶10) — ATWOOD introducing Synesius.
- Etherial Spirit = the medium joining divine and lowest natures — "it is the medium which conjoins divine natures with the lowest of all" (II.2 ¶10) — QUOTED: Synesius.
- Wisdom ("old age" in Plato) = intuitive Intellect without imaginative error — "Plato appropriates the possession of Wisdom to old age, signifying by this Intellect diving intuitively without imaginative error" (II.2 ¶13) — ATWOOD.
- labors of Hercules = the forced contest against fate's "material inflictors" on breaking with sense — "this is what the Sacred Discourse testifies by the labors of Hercules" (II.2 ¶13) — QUOTED: Synesius.
- Phantastic Spirit = mundane spirit — "such is said to be the nature of the Phantastic Spirit before it is mundified" … "Thus Iamblicus, speaking of this mundane spirit" (II.2 ¶15) — ATWOOD (an identity by reference).
- unpurified Phantastic Spirit = the Sphinx — "For is she not that very Sphinx of the Labyrinth" (II.2 ¶15) — ATWOOD; interpreted (antecedent of "she").
- solve = comprehend — "solve, that is comprehend, it on its own ground" (II.2 ¶15) — ATWOOD.
- the mind's vehicle = the Spirit = the perceptive medium — "the primary and proper vehicle of the mind" … "the perceptive medium is impure" (II.2 ¶15) — ATWOOD; interpreted (same paragraph, same subject).
- vulgar Matter = the impure spirit, and Mercury of the Philosophers = the purified spirit — "It is therefore that the Alchemists so much declaim against the vulgar Matter" (II.2 ¶16) — ATWOOD; interpreted.
- occult Arcanum = our brass — "the occult Arcanum, which is our brass" (II.2 ¶16) — QUOTED: Albertus Mangus.
- universal Mundane Spirit (as first revealed in man) = passive intellect — "which Aristotle, in his Metaphysics, calls passive intellect" (II.2 ¶17) — ATWOOD.
- Mundane Spirit = mesmeric agent = photogenic medium = New Imponderable = Common Soul = the Alchemists' subject-matter — "the identical agent which is spread abroad in the present day mesmerizing, the photogenic medium, our New Imponderable, for it is the Common Soul; also the subject-matter of the Alchemists aforesaid" (II.2 ¶18) — ATWOOD.
- Sphinx's riddles = images of the nature of the human spirit — "her dark riddles in the world, images of the obscure and intricate nature of the human spirit" (II.2 ¶19) — ATWOOD.
- the Monster = the Eternal Riddle = the Compound Simple = the ground of the magians' elements — "This is the Monster and the Eternal Riddle explained to common sense as suits it, but misunderstood to this day; that Compound Simple and ground of the magians’ elements" (II.2 ¶19) — ATWOOD.
- Augean Stable / labor of the philosophic Hercules = turning the current of life and purifying its natural source — "nor the least of labors, to turn the current of life into another channel, and purify the natural source" (II.2 ¶19) — ATWOOD.
- Purificative Rites = restoring the monarchy of reason (as preparation) — "the Purificative Rites, which were designed also to restore the monarchy of reason in the soul" (II.2 ¶20) — ATWOOD.
- the "mere nothing" principle = the Stone of philosophers (when worked) — "it is the very same which solved and resolved, and wisely manipulated, becomes to be the concrete Stone of philosophers" (II.2 ¶25) — ATWOOD.

## Open questions

1. **What "the medium" is in ¶8 ("to dissolve the medium more entirely").** (a) The Universal Medium of ¶7. (b) The Spirit of the natural life or Vital Spirit named in the same sentence. Basis: ¶7 discusses improving the Universal Medium, and ¶8 goes on to "segregate the Vital Spirit". The two readings may describe one subject, but the chapter does not state it.

2. **Whether the "Imponderable" equals the Universal Medium (¶7).** (a) The same thing, named in adjacent sentences. (b) A separate agent found by experiment. ¶18 does equate "our New Imponderable" with the Common Soul and the alchemists' subject, which favours (a) for the chapter as a whole.

3. **What the "ineffable and sublime media" were (¶5).** (a) Operative means like the mesmeric agency, since ¶8 says the ancients worked "with similar instruments". (b) Rites the chapter deliberately withholds. ¶24 defers "the particulars" of the means to "the Practice". Also undefined: "appropriate aids" (¶8), "auxiliaries" (¶20), "its proper Light" (¶24), "the true ferment" (¶26), and the named operations "to be hereafter noted" (¶27).

4. **What the "Medial Life" is, and how the two orderings line up (¶8, ¶20).** (a) The Mundane Spirit or Common Soul as first revealed (¶17–19, joined by "then"; ¶26 calls the Spirit "a middle substance"). (b) A stage of the Mysteries not otherwise identified. If (a), then "revealment of the Medial Life" corresponds to the first-sphere revelations passed in the Lesser Mysteries (¶8), followed by Purificative Rites, then final initiations. That alignment is not stated in the text.

5. **"in her Door-keeper, Isis is not revealed" (¶18).** (a) The natural, first-manifested life does not reveal the full nature (same sentence: it is "in need of melioration"). (b) Present-day mesmeric manifestation falls short of the mystery (¶18 identity with the mesmeric agent; ¶19 "only to the gate"; ¶24).

6. **What "solution" means (¶15–16).** (a) Comprehension, as in ¶15 "solve, that is comprehend". (b) Dissolving or washing, as in ¶16 "wash it", "solutions, calcinations". Both may be intended. This matters because "The first rule of the work is a perfect solution" (¶16). Related: "alternation and solution" (¶15) may mean alternating states as printed, or "alteration" (transformation) if it is a misprint.

7. **"by labor and a transition into other lives" (¶13, Synesius).** (a) Literal successive lives. (b) Passage into other states of life within this one, for example through initiation (the same paragraph: restoration "as a gift of divinity and initiation"). The chapter excludes neither.

8. **"all the while regressive" (¶14).** (a) Consciousness turning back toward its origin (compare ¶13 "the regression of the rational soul"). (b) Setbacks or descent during the trials.

9. **"the rejected Keystone regains her Head place" (¶29).** (a) The despised subject restored to supremacy (¶18 "abject"). (b) Reason restored to rule (¶20 "monarchy of reason"). (c) The subject raised by sublimation "to the Head of an appropriate Vessel" (¶27; "her" matches "taking her" in ¶27).

10. **Corrupt or defective wording.** ¶3 "Now the Egyptians, that is the Hermetic Art" is read as "the Egyptians' art". ¶25 "become sin that of a philosopher" probably means "becomes in that of a philosopher". ¶26 "O, how wonderful different and detract nothing" can be recovered only as "add nothing different, take nothing away but superfluities". The Aristhenes attribution in ¶26 sits beside a footnote reading "Aristiteles in Roasrio" (n18).

11. **What role the mesmeric trance plays (¶6–8, ¶14).** (a) A real point of entry into the same material, which the ancients passed beyond ("with that key in hand", ¶6; "same material", ¶8; mesmerized patients' Spirit "of a higher birth", ¶14). (b) Only a parallel; ¶10 says trance leaves the false imagination intact. The referent of "that key" in ¶6 (the Trance, or inquiry into the ancient rites) is unclear.
