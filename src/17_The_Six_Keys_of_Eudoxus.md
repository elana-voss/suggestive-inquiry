# The Six Keys of Eudoxus

*Part IV, Chapter 3*

The chapter has two voices. It opens with a long first-person text, *The Six Keys of Eudoxus*, which sets out the practice as six successive operations; Atwood adds no comment while it runs (IV.3 ¶1–16). She then fills its admitted gaps from other authors, tells gold-seekers that the metallic claims are not literally true, and reads the last operation, Fermentation, as the union of man with God. She closes by saying that the Hermetic method and its end are self-surrender into union, which she calls "the true Christian Philosopher's Stone" (IV.3 ¶17–62).

## The first two Keys: the white Stone and its dissolution

Eudoxus warns first that "the operations of the three works have a great deal of analogy one to another", and that the philosophers "designedly speak in equivocal terms", so that a passage seeming to describe one work may describe another (IV.3 ¶3). Each work repeats one cycle: the Artist must "dissolve the body with the spirit", "cut off the Raven's head, whiten the Black, and vivify the White" (IV.3 ¶3).

The First Key "opens the dark prisons in which the Sulphur is shut up". It forms the Stone by joining spirit with body, "sulphur with mercury" (IV.3 ¶3). Quoting Hermes, Eudoxus places the Stone "in the caverns of the metals" and calls it "the Sea of the Wise" (IV.3 ¶3). By separating "the black, foul, and stinking fume of the Blackest Black" the white Stone is formed, and the Key ends at this "First Purification" and "shining whiteness" (IV.3 ¶3).

The Second Key is sublimation, meaning the raising of the subtle, pure parts above the thick, earthy ones (IV.3 ¶5). Its only instrument is "the Fire of the Wise", a Secret Fire that no philosopher has openly revealed. It is known "rather a gift of Heaven, than a Light acquired by the natural force of reasoning", and Eudoxus tells the reader who cannot recognise it to stop and pray (IV.3 ¶5). Sublimation alone allows the conversion of the elements, "Water of Earth, Air of Water, and Fire of Air", which is "the only way whereby our Mercury can be prepared" (IV.3 ¶5). The Fire dissolves the Stone gently into Water: "the vine of the Wise" becomes their Wine (IV.3 ¶5). The Key ends after "a proportioned digestion and putrefaction" (IV.3 ¶5).

## The Third Key: preparing "our Mercury"

The Third Key is longer than all the others together. Mercury's perfection depends on it, so the writers kept silent or "feigned" (IV.3 ¶7). The Water holds three principles: "Salt, Sulphur and Mercury --- which are the spirit, the soul, and the body". They only seem to be united (IV.3 ¶7); the text leaves open which principle answers to which. When the Water is distilled off, the body is left as "a dead, black, and dredgy earth" that must not be thrown away. Quoting Pontanus, Eudoxus says "he who pretends to separate anything from our subject knows nothing of philosophy" (IV.3 ¶7). Fire and Sulphur lie hidden in this earth. Washing it "with its spirit" draws out "the Fixed Salt, which is the Blood of our Stone" (IV.3 ¶7). Fire and water are reconciled by moistening the earth often with its water. "The Spirit is Eve, the Body is Adam", and the two rise together into a nobler form, as rectified alcohol differs from wine (IV.3 ¶7).

The Water that springs from the Stone is the foundation of the Art and is properly "the Vinegar of the Wise" (IV.3 ¶8). The body must be dissolved entirely, or the desired blackness never appears. Once the black residue is washed white, the Water becomes "animated with this Fiery Essence" (IV.3 ¶8). Pouring the water back on the body many times fattens the Mercury and makes it fit to be fixed. The thickening of the water is the good sign (IV.3 ¶9).

From the liquor two Mercuries are made: "The Lunaria is the White Mercury, the most sharp Vinegar is the Red Mercury". They are fed with "the blood of innocents", that is, "the spirits of the bodies" (IV.3 ¶10). Eudoxus calls this "the most essential point in the Practice". The two must be kept apart "for fear they should beget a monstrous Lineage" (IV.3 ¶10). In Cosmopolite's allegory the old man is "our Mercury" (IV.3 ¶10). The finished liquor is "the great Alcahest", a permanent water that dissolves the metals and stays united with them (IV.3 ¶10).

## The last three Keys

The Fourth Key begins the Second Work. Boiling alone turns "our Water into Earth", because the Mercury carries its own coagulating Sulphur (IV.3 ¶12). If the signs of coagulation do not appear in time, the body was badly dissolved earlier (IV.3 ¶12). The Fifth Key ferments the Stone with the Perfect Body, as bread is made, with more meal than leaven and more water than both (IV.3 ¶14). The Sixth multiplies it by repeated dissolving and coagulating. Eudoxus admits he fully explained only the Third Key, and that he lost fifteen years for want of the secret fire (IV.3 ¶16).

## Atwood's additions and her warning

"The Keys of Eudoxus open no more," Atwood writes. Their parts on Multiplication and Projection are "avowedly defective", so she supplies them from other authors (IV.3 ¶17). The Open Entrance gives Multiplication, each round faster and stronger (IV.3 ¶18); Philalethes and Lucerna Salis give Projection (IV.3 ¶20, ¶24). Atwood herself distinguishes a spiritual fermentation, which cannot use common gold or silver, from a bodily one (IV.3 ¶21). Sendivogius, Vaughan and Eirenaeus add more, down to a grain that tinged "ninety thousand more" (IV.3 ¶21–25).

Then she tells gold-seekers to disregard all of it: "literally taken they are not" true (IV.3 ¶26). The rewards come only after "a long-suffering and laborious life". The philosophers sought "not gold however, or silver, but the substance of these things", which once gained was "swallowed up in the vision of a yet more alluring prospect of the Light" (IV.3 ¶26).

## Fermentation as union, and the healer

Atwood sums up. Fermentation, the last operation, is what "the Kabalah" calls "the union of man, reduced to the simplicity of the Monad, with God" (IV.3 ¶27). As such a man does what he wills, the fermented Stone "coequals itself to all". Metallic perfection, she adds, belongs only to life's first stage (IV.3 ¶27). Khunrath lists its powers over disease of mind and body (IV.3 ¶28). The Stone is fermented with "its metal" (IV.3 ¶29), and Atwood reads that metal as Light: "is not Light the true Aurific Seed", meaning the light asleep in all nature (IV.3 ¶30). Its chief benefit to man is "the vital rectification". In Julian's words, which she cites, the purified soul receives Divine Light and her spirit keeps the body healthy (IV.3 ¶30). Helmont writes that the healing art perished "after that the study of ambition and gain were practiced" (IV.3 ¶34).

## Self-knowledge and the return

Mechanical Mesmerism, Atwood says, is only "a first step" toward Divine Wisdom (IV.3 ¶37). The theosophist she endorses puts the whole work in a few lines. Wrath fire and light fire "are in every thing", and the light must subdue the wrath. "Two Natures" must "be reduced to One". "The Mercury which is therein doth all this itself", and the Artist only prepares the way (IV.3 ¶38); the text leaves unclear where his words end and Atwood's begin. Only "he that the Spirit has already prepared" is competent to do the work (IV.3 ¶39).

In her closing pages Atwood repeats her warnings: study before any trial, and know that success "depends on the discovery of the true Intention" (IV.3 ¶54). Pride or private ends ruin the work (IV.3 ¶56). Immortality is "only to be consummated in union, and in the abandonment of the dual selfhood" (IV.3 ¶58). Her final image: "The line returns to form a circle into its' beginning". This, she says, is "the true Christian Philosopher's Stone" (IV.3 ¶62).

## The Work in this chapter

- **Matter.** The Stone, hidden "in the caverns of the metals" (IV.3 ¶3); nothing is cast away, for the black earth holds the Fixed Salt (IV.3 ¶7) (stated).
- **Agent.** "the Fire of the Wise" dissolves the Stone (IV.3 ¶5) (stated; the text never identifies it). In Atwood's reading the multiplying agent is Light (IV.3 ¶30) (stated). In the theosophist's words, "The Mercury which is therein doth all this itself" (IV.3 ¶38) (stated).
- **Stages.** Whitening, sublimation, the two Mercuries, water into earth, fermentation, multiplication (IV.3 ¶3–16) (stated).
- **Signs.** Whiteness ends the First Key (IV.3 ¶3). The water thickens (IV.3 ¶9). Coagulation must appear "in their time" (IV.3 ¶12) (stated).
- **Operator.** Inwardly alive, prepared by the Spirit, seeking "the true Intention" (IV.3 ¶38, ¶39, ¶54) (stated).
- **Result.** "Two Natures be reduced to One" (IV.3 ¶38). This is union with God, the line returning to a circle (IV.3 ¶27, ¶62) (stated).

## Terms introduced

- **Key** — one of six successive operations, placed within three analogous works (IV.3 ¶3, ¶5).
- **Fire of the Wise / Secret Fire** — the unrevealed sole agent of sublimation (IV.3 ¶5).
- **sublimation** — raising the subtle parts above the gross (IV.3 ¶5).
- **Salt, Sulphur and Mercury** — the three principles, equated with spirit, soul and body (IV.3 ¶7).
- **Fixed Salt** — "the Blood of our Stone", washed out of the black earth (IV.3 ¶7).
- **Vinegar of the Wise** — the proper name of the Water from the Stone (IV.3 ¶8).
- **White and Red Mercury** — the Lunaria and the sharp Vinegar; they must be kept separate (IV.3 ¶10).
- **blood of innocents** — "the spirits of the bodies" (IV.3 ¶10).
- **Alcahest** — the permanent water that dissolves the metals and stays united with them (IV.3 ¶10).
- **Fermentation** — the last operation; the Kabalah's union of man with God (IV.3 ¶27).
- **Aurific Seed** — Light, the Stone's "own metal" (IV.3 ¶30).
- **true Intention** — the thing on whose discovery success depends (IV.3 ¶54).

## Authorities cited

Eudoxus gives the Keys (IV.3 ¶1–16), citing Hermes, Pontanus, Cosmopolite, Senior, Geber, Flammel, Artefius and Trevisan (IV.3 ¶3–10). On Multiplication and Projection: the Open Entrance, E. Philalethes, Sendivogius, Vaughan, Lucerna Salis, Eirenaeus, the Rosary (IV.3 ¶18–26, n1–n7). On Fermentation and healing: the Kabalah, Khunrath, Julian, Petris, Helmont (IV.3 ¶27–35). In the close: the theosophist (n16 cites Freher), Solomon, Agrippa, Palingenius, Deuteronomy, the Sephir Jezirah (IV.3 ¶38–61, n16–n28).
